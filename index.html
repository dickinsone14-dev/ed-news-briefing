Container Ship Sinks in the Strait of Hormuz as Attacks Surge

The Singapore-flagged GFS Pride sank after being struck by an unknown projectile on Tuesday; all 21 crew were evacuated unhurt. A second Singapore-flagged ship was hit today and reached safe anchorage. Singapore’s maritime authority called the attacks “unprovoked, unjustifiable and a breach of international law”. Iran says the vessel struck a mine on an “unauthorised” route.

Dive deeper
The maritime trade monitor recorded twelve vessels attacked between 5 and 11 October, six of them on Sunday alone, and Kpler puts crude exports through the strait at 8.5 million barrels a day in the week to 12 October — nearly 40% below pre-war levels, after a September rebound under American escort. Two Iranian officials told Reuters the new tactics were decided at the top, with one aim being to frustrate efforts to lower oil prices before the 3 November midterms. The Revolutionary Guards’ naval commander says Tehran is “counting down every moment” to sinking American warships; Central Command replied that he “has no navy”. Only one other ship had been reported sunk since the war began.

Dive deeper

Iraq’s prime minister, Ali al-Zaidi, used his first visit to Riyadh to promise that Iraqi territory would not be a “launching pad” for attacks — pointed, since Baghdad’s own inquiry found last month’s drone strike on the East-West pipeline came from Iraq. Inside Yemen the cost is mounting. American Inflation Rises to 3.7% on the Eve of the Midterms

Consumer prices rose 3.7% in the year to September, above the 3.6% forecast and up from 3.4%, in the last reading before the 3 November elections. Petrol rose 9.1% in the month and accounted for more than half the increase; core inflation reached 2.6%. Treasury yields rose and Wall Street fell.

Dive deeper
This is the war arriving in the American shopping basket: energy is up 20.4% on the year and heating oil 74.2%, while food was flat in September. The core figure is what alarms bond traders — Christopher Rupkey of Fwdbonds called it a “shocking inflation report” and said “inflation is alive”. The ten-year Treasury yield rose to 5.242% and the two-year by more than five basis points. The Federal Reserve raised rates in September for the first time in three years and meets on 27–28 October; futures now price a further rise by December as near-certain. For Britain the transmission is through the dollar: sterling is down about a cent on the day.

Ukrainian terminals moving faster than 160km/h lost connection overnight, wrecking a number of drone missions, according to Serhiy Beskrestnov, a presidential adviser: “It’s clear that this action cannot be a coincidence.” Elon Musk said the military service “is not under SpaceX control”. The Ukrainian air force says no warning preceded Tuesday’s first ballistic barrage, though “foreign partners” flagged three later ones — and the European Commission says it has “no indications” American intelligence sharing has stopped. Energy Agency Warns the Diesel Squeeze Will Last Into 2027

The International Energy Agency’s monthly report says gasoil supply will fall short of demand this quarter and lost stocks are unlikely to be rebuilt before the second half of 2027. Global diesel exports fell 700,000 barrels a day year-on-year in September. Wholesale prices touched record highs of about $220 a barrel in Europe.

Dive deeper
The agency’s point is that markets went into winter without a buffer: heavy inventory draws over the summer “deprived global gasoil markets of crucial stock cover ahead of the peak winter demand season”. A loss of 1.3 million barrels a day from Russia and the Middle East was only partly replaced. That is the backdrop to the American deal for Russian diesel, which the European Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis dismissed in Bangkok: the volumes are “not going to have any macroeconomic significance”. A second shortage is building behind it — a senior United Nations official warns that Asia and Africa face a food crisis unless Washington and Tehran agree a stalled plan to move fertiliser through Hormuz before planting season.

Dive deeper

Labour committed in opposition to a single status of “worker” in employment law, and these proposals do not. Meri Ahlberg of the Joseph Rowntree Foundation calls them “a step forward” but warns that without single status and tax reform “it will still be cheaper to employ people in less secure jobs”, leaving millions without sick pay or parental pay. The incentive she describes is a tax one — a lever only the Budget can pull.

Fire at Aramco Building as Saudi Hits Yemen’s Fuel Depots

Officials say a fire broke out at a Saudi Aramco building in Dhahran after an overnight strike, as the coalition says it intercepted four missiles and two drones near Riyadh. Saudi aircraft hit a fuel facility west of Sanaa in ten waves. Brent rose above $106. Aramco has not commented.

Dive deeper
Two things are worth not overstating. No source confirms Aramco’s headquarters was hit — the company’s head office sits in the same eastern cluster of cities, and what is established is a fire at a building in Dhahran, reported by unnamed officials, with no casualties recorded and no word from the company. The Houthi-run Yemen Petroleum Company insists the depot struck near Sanaa “does not contain any stock of petroleum products”, though queues formed at the capital’s petrol stations. Kyiv’s Power Plant Struck for a Second Night Running

Russian ballistic missiles hit the Darnytska plant that heats much of eastern Kyiv, killing two people and injuring twenty-one in an apartment block. “Once again, no advance warning from our partners,” Volodymyr Zelensky said. He has separately said Washington warned it could cut intelligence sharing unless Ukraine stops striking Russian refineries.

Dive deeper
Two separate things are being said and they should not be welded together. The president told an American broadcaster that the administration had warned it could cut off intelligence sharing unless Ukrainian forces stop long-range drone strikes on oil refineries inside Russia — a specific condition, though he did not say who delivered it or when. Separately, he says no early warning came before last night’s strike. He does not claim the second followed from the first, and his own foreign minister Andrii Sybiha attributed the missing alert to a “special type” of Russian weaponry rather than to anything political. The White House declined to say whether it had made such a threat, pointing instead to Mr Trump’s remark that “we are helping them, and we are sharing intel” — which answers a different question. The plant has been hit before: a strike in February left more than a thousand buildings, forty-seven schools and eighteen medical institutions without heating. Pope Leo Diagnosed With Lung Cancer After Surgery

The Vatican says tests during the operation confirmed an adenocarcinoma, and that the entire lower lobe of the 71-year-old’s right lung was removed to ensure the procedure was radical. The surgery was successful with no complications. The stage has not been disclosed.

Dive deeper
Two descriptions are circulating and they are not the same thing. On Sunday the Pope told the crowd in St Peter’s Square that the growth was “contained” and found “at a very early stage” — his own words about a nodule, before any diagnosis. What the Vatican issued after surgery is a confirmed cancer, with no stage given and no statement on whether it has spread; a source says further tests are under way. The lump was found during routine checks and he had no symptoms. The operation took ninety minutes, led by the Gemelli hospital’s chief thoracic surgeon, and he is expected to stay about a week. The question now is the diary. Audiences are cleared only to 20 October, and he is due in Latin America in early November including Peru, where he served as a missionary and bishop — a cardinal who saw him on Monday says he moved quickly precisely to keep that trip. Patients are usually advised against flying for about three weeks after such a procedure, which would take him to the edge of it.

Turkish Police Raid an Opposition Broadcaster Mid-Transmission

Police entered the Istanbul headquarters of Halk TV on Wednesday morning, halting live broadcasts. The channel says staff cannot get into the building and that a new manager has been appointed to run it. Turkish media report seventeen arrest warrants linked to its owner.

Dive deeper
The station is widely seen as close to the main opposition Republican People’s Party and is routinely critical of President Erdogan. Installing a manager is the part that distinguishes this from a search: a raid interrupts a broadcaster, an appointment replaces it. The warrant figure comes from Turkish media rather than from any confirmed official source, and is reported here as such. It follows a pattern that has tightened through the year. In May a Turkish court removed the opposition party’s leadership from running it, a ruling the party said cemented the president’s grip, and Istanbul’s mayor Ekrem Imamoglu — the president’s most serious rival — has now been in prison for more than a year on corruption accusations he denies. Turkey is a NATO member and a candidate for European Union membership, which is what makes the treatment of its press a matter beyond its borders rather than an internal affair. Watch whether the channel returns to air under its existing editorial team or under the new appointment.

Germany Tries a Ukrainian Officer Over the Nord Stream Blasts

Dive deeper
The charge is unusual: prosecutors are proceeding under Germany’s code of crimes against international law, treating the sabotage as an attack by military means against civilian objects. They say he entered Germany on a forged passport three weeks beforehand and that the operation was carried out on the orders of Ukrainian state agencies. The trial is expected to run into February and is the first public airing of evidence after years of investigation. Kyiv is not conceding the account — Oleksandr Merezhko, who chairs the Ukrainian parliament’s foreign affairs committee, calls it “one of the versions” and argues the pipeline was “nothing but a tool used by Russia in order to get prepared for the war”. A German analyst notes successive chancellors have worked to keep the case out of politics and in the courts. The British interest is historical: Vladimir Putin blamed “the Anglo-Saxons” for the blasts, meaning Washington and London, and both denied it. Nord Stream 1 had supplied about half of Germany’s gas imports.

UK Domestic

Zelensky at Downing Street as Britain Doubles Down

The Ukrainian president arrives in London this morning, with a £45m package of surface-to-air missiles announced and £10m of it accelerated for what the government calls Ukraine’s most urgent winter requirements. “Our rock-solid support to Ukraine is vital for keeping us safe here in the UK,” the prime minister said.

Dive deeper
The timing is the point. Washington has eased sanctions to let Russian diesel reach world markets, the American president has called for Ukraine to find new leadership, and overnight Russian missiles hit a Kyiv power plant with what Mr Zelensky says was no advance warning from allies. The foreign secretary Ed Miliband called it a “very difficult few days” for relations with Washington. Against that, a British pledge reads as a deliberate counterweight. Two cautions on the numbers. The £1.6bn for more than 5,000 lightweight multirole missiles over four years is a standing commitment being restated, not money announced today; and the prime minister has asked ministers to draw up further sanctions, military aid and fiscal packages “in the coming weeks”, which is a commission rather than a package. What is genuinely new is the £45m and the acceleration of deliveries aimed specifically at Russia’s jet-powered drones, which fly faster and higher than the propeller-driven type and are harder to bring down. The chancellor is urging partners at the IMF meetings in Bangkok to match Britain’s ban on Russian liquefied natural gas.

Businesses Face Water Bans as the Drought Persists

Three water companies have applied to Defra for powers to stop non-essential use in parts of their regions, which would bar commercial car washes and prevent golf clubs and private schools irrigating playing fields from the mains. Eleven areas of England remain in drought and reservoirs stand at 54%.

Dive deeper
The asymmetry is what has brought this to a head. Households have been under hosepipe restrictions for three months while businesses have been exempt, and a power hose can use twenty-five litres a minute. Mark Owen of the Angling Trust puts the objection plainly: “It makes little sense to ban people from washing cars at home while commercial car washes can continue operating.” The underlying figures are worse than the headline. Reservoir stocks are a fifth below normal for the time of year, nineteen reservoirs or groups are under half full and eight are described as exceptionally low, up from five in August. Seven areas have exceptionally low river flows, including the Derwent, the Yare, the Ely Ouse, the Tone and the Hampshire Avon catchments. September brought only 65% of typical rainfall and Yorkshire entered drought on 9 October. The Met Office’s assessment is the sobering part: recovery needs above-average rain sustained over about seven months, which is a winter and a spring, not a wet fortnight. Defra has not yet responded to the applications.

Royal Mail Losing Four Hundred Staff a Week, Union Says

The Communication Workers Union says departures have risen from about 330 a week to 400 from a workforce of 83,000 since a new delivery system was introduced, and that it is “not working in hundreds of offices”. Royal Mail says the reforms are part of making the service financially sustainable.

Dive deeper
The system, known as DM26, reorganises rounds into groups of three workers and changes walking patterns, with second class delivered every other weekday and first class and parcels daily. It has reached about 1,000 of 1,200 delivery offices. Of more than thirty staff interviewed across the four nations, eight tracked their steps and all had risen by a quarter to a third, some to around 25,000 a day. Bryn Dymott, who delivered in Wootton in Bedfordshire for nine years, left in July rather than face another Christmas he called shambolic, saying it was “proving it’s impossible to have four people’s work done by three people”. One postal worker in the West Midlands described leaving first class mail behind on a road that had been running late for weeks, “but I’m sure a manager would have ticked a box to say all first class mail went out”. The attrition figures are the union’s and the company has not disputed them, answering instead on delivery times. It announced 2,500 job cuts six days ago.

Lib Dems Rally Behind Davey After Frontbench Resignations

Two frontbenchers have quit in the past week — Charlie Maynard, the former Treasury spokesman, and Alison Bennett, who covered care and carers — over the party’s direction and how policy is decided. Dive deeper
Neither has spelled out their reasons publicly and the two did not coordinate, which is part of why colleagues were irritated: separate resignations in one week look like a pattern whether or not they are one. The changes already made are organisational rather than political — a new chief of staff, a political director who served through the coalition years, and an upgraded social media operation. The leader’s allies put the number of unhappy MPs at no more than half a dozen, though the party’s own political correspondents detect wider anxiety about a lack of impact. That anxiety has a cause worth stating: the party has 72 MPs, up from 15, making it the largest third party in the Commons in a century, and a caucus that size expects to be heard. The deputy leader Daisy Cooper reported “overwhelming support” afterwards. One MP’s summary of the position was more candid than any of it: “Why would we get rid of our leader when no one else is pitching to be our leader?”

Heating Oil Users Face Fuel or Food Choices This Winter

Ministers have promised to regulate the heating oil market and promote competition, but no price cap. Many customers will pay close to double last year, with 500 litres costing over £500. About 1.7 million households are off the gas grid and must buy in bulk rather than spread payments.

Dive deeper
The reporting behind this is from Shropshire, where the problem is concentrated rather than unique: roughly 15% of the county’s households use oil, and in South Shropshire 53% are off mains gas, among the highest proportions in the country. Liz Humphris, a retired teacher in Bishop’s Castle who volunteers at a food bank, now orders 500 litres at a time because a full tank costs too much: “For people who are on a very fixed income, it literally is coming down to the point where it is fuel or food, which is awful because you’ve got to heat your home.” The structural problem is the lump sum. As Hanna Clarke of a local charity running a bulk-buy co-operative puts it, “you can’t necessarily put it on a direct debit and spread that cost out across the 12 months”. Corrine Pluchino of Action with Communities in Rural England says the government’s package does not go far enough and wants VAT cut on heating oil immediately, because the real issue is a high and volatile price paid all at once.

Republican Senators Demand the Russian Diesel Deal Is Torn Up

Dive deeper
Their argument is that the deal buys almost nothing at home while paying for the war abroad: it “will have a negligible impact on fuel prices, but the revenues the Kremlin will receive from this agreement will undoubtedly finance its desired war of attrition and advance its security cooperation with China, Iran, and North Korea”. They want the general licence revoked and the sanctions act the president signed this year actually used. Argentina Takes the Falklands Oil Dispute to a Tribunal

Buenos Aires has asked the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea to block drilling at the Sea Lion field, 130 miles from the islands, which holds an estimated 1.7 billion barrels and is due to start pumping in 2028. Britain has separately asked Interpol to reject Argentine arrest warrants against those involved.

Dive deeper
The field could triple the islands’ economy for a population of about 3,500, which is why the pressure is escalating on both tracks at once. Argentina has sought “red notices” against people linked to the project, including British nationals; the Foreign Office has written to Interpol’s headquarters in Lyon arguing they are politically motivated, and Interpol has replied that it will respect the article of its constitution forbidding political intervention. The foreign secretary Ed Miliband called the attempts “to intimidate businesses and individuals engaged in legitimate commercial activity… unacceptable”, and the minister Uma Kumaran told MPs this was “economic coercion” and that the filing “does not invalidate licenses issued by the Falkland Islands government”. British officials believe President Milei is picking the fight ahead of elections next year; he has called the United Nations “a useless organisation”.
