Twenty-Four Killed in Ukraine, Eighteen in One Apartment Block

Russia fired seventy ballistic missiles and forty-six jet-powered drones at eleven Ukrainian regions overnight, killing at least twenty-four people. Eighteen of them, four of them children, died in a single five-storey apartment block in Pryluky in the Chernihiv region. Volodymyr Zelensky called it among Russia’s “most vile” attacks and said the timing was chosen.

Dive deeper
Mr Zelensky named the dead, among them a one-year-old called Serhii and an eight-year-old called Maksym, and said Russia had “presented” Vladimir Putin with “the destruction of a residential block in Pryluky” for his seventy-fourth birthday: “The Russians know very well that they are striking ordinary cities, ordinary life. It is an absolutely deliberate choice of terror.” Ukraine’s air force says it brought down more than 80% of the drones, against an average nearer 50 to 60%. About fifty people were injured, children among them, and two were killed in Kyiv, where BBC Verify authenticated images of an unexploded Iskander lying on open ground ringed by tower blocks. The same campaign is now reaching well beyond Ukraine’s borders. Bulgaria has ended its search for the crew of a cargo ship sunk by a drone about 150km east of Byala, outside its territorial waters: “There is no trace of the sailors,” said Rumen Nikolov, who co-ordinated the operation. Eighteen Turkish and Indian crew were rescued from a second vessel that caught fire, and no drone debris has been recovered. Rumen Radev called the attack “absolutely unacceptable” and Ursula von der Leyen condemned it. A separate ship sank off Romania on Monday, killing two. The toll was still rising as rescuers worked. Mr Zelensky had warned the day before that intelligence pointed to a massive strike coming, and it could not be stopped: without ammunition for its American-made Patriot systems Ukraine cannot intercept ballistic missiles, and Patriots are scarce because of the war with Iran. That is the thread tying this to the rest of tonight’s page.

A Drone Threat Linked to Iran Emptied RAF Fairford

A credible threat linked to Iran, potentially involving a drone attack, is why the United States abruptly removed at least a dozen B-1 and B-52 bombers from RAF Fairford on Sunday, American officials say. A seventh man, a 22-year-old Briton, was arrested in Westminster on Tuesday. Police found petrol in three vans linked to the first five suspects, but no explosives. Six have been bailed and nobody has been charged.

Dive deeper
This is the first reason anyone has put on the record, and it now rests on several American officials and a Defence Department statement rather than a single briefing. Andy Burnham says Britain sees “strong indications” that Iran played a part. The sequence began on 27 September, when police stopped five young men in white vans near the Gloucestershire base in the middle of the night. Other American aircraft that had used Fairford for strikes on Iran had already gone; the B-1s followed on Sunday and are back at home stations in the United States. The seventh man was held under section 5 of the Terrorism Act 2006, which covers preparing acts of terrorism, and a property was searched; one of the earlier arrests was a dual British-Iranian national. Vicki Evans, the senior national coordinator for counterterrorism policing, says detectives “continue to pursue multiple lines of enquiry”. Pentagon officials say the aircraft moved not because of a new threat but because of “the threat reporting that was already out there”, and the Ministry of Defence declined to comment, so the link between the arrests and the withdrawal is still not formally drawn. Tehran denies involvement, while maintaining that any base used to attack its territory is a legitimate target. What makes this a serious worry rather than a rumour is the precedent. NPR’s national security correspondent draws the comparison with Ukraine’s truck-launched drone raid on Russian bomber bases last year, which damaged or destroyed somewhere between twenty and forty aircraft without a single manned sortie, and which changed how every air force now thinks about parking expensive aeroplanes within reach of a public road. Watch whether any of the seven are charged, and whether the bombers come back.

The interior minister Laurent Nuñez has ordered a temporary halt to police use of stun grenades in the school protests, after a fifteen-year-old’s hand was amputated following an incident in Lens on Monday. His mother told French television: “His life has been stolen.” The prime minister addresses the nation tonight on the protests and on the public finances.

Dive deeper

He maintained that talk of “police repression” was “excessive”, because officers do not intervene where protests are peaceful; the authorities say up to two hundred people were throwing projectiles at Lens, and the boy’s mother denies he picked the grenade up. Amnesty’s French president Anne Savinel-Barras called the suspension “an important first step” and wants the devices banned from all protest policing: “Protests should not be battlefields.” Emmanuel Macron, after an emergency ministerial meeting, asked the police for “empathy” and more “pedagogy”; Mr Nuñez, himself a former chief of the Paris police, observed that “we don’t deal with a movement of highschoolers like we deal with a movement of adults”. Mr Lecornu has told lawmakers that lessons will be replaced to the end of the week by a “period of internal democracy” in which pupils air their grievances — the device Mr Macron reached for during the Yellow Vests. The other half of tonight’s address is money. France ran a deficit of 5.1% of GDP last year against an EU reference value of 3%, and its ten-year borrowing cost has risen more than a hundred basis points this year, now trading above Italy’s. The IMF’s Kristalina Georgieva put it plainly: “my message is — get your house in order.”

China Puts Attack Jets at Its First Overseas Air Base

Satellite images show eight light attack aircraft and more than a dozen military vehicles at Ban Keun airport in Laos, fifty kilometres north of Vientiane, a day after China opened a joint “support and training centre” there on 29 September. It would be Beijing’s first facility outside China capable of handling fighter jets.

Dive deeper
The airframes match the Hongdu JL-8, a two-seat trainer that can carry bombs; the International Institute for Strategic Studies believes Laos owns four of them, which is rather fewer than eight. Construction at the site appears aimed at extending the runway, which would in time allow it to take bombers. It is also China’s first military foothold in mainland south-east Asia. Beijing’s defence ministry says the centre will “help Laos strengthen the training of its air force pilots” and is not “directed against any third party”. The analyst Collin Koh judges that it “may not be wrong to conclude this is the first full-fledged PLA overseas base in south-east Asia”, while the Institute for the Study of War calls it the first Chinese overseas facility focused on air power but cautions that it is “not yet equipped to provide the PLA with the ability to execute major combat operations, unlike US military bases”. Thailand’s defence ministry says Bangkok will “closely monitor developments”. Laos has said nothing at all. One detail deserves its own note: the images come from a commercial provider, Vantor. This is a strategic shift disclosed by private satellites rather than by any government, which is increasingly how these things surface. It should be read with that in mind: the account rests on one newspaper’s reading of commercial imagery, no other approved source has examined the pictures independently, and the identification of the aircraft turns on their shape.

No Inheritance Tax on the Family Home, Badenoch Pledges

Kemi Badenoch told the Conservative conference that “the next Conservative government will legislate so nobody will ever pay inheritance tax on their family home”, with couples able to leave an additional £1m tax free. The party puts the cost at £6bn a year and says it comes from £71bn of identified savings, £36bn of it from welfare.

Dive deeper
This morning’s edition reported the cut as briefed but unconfirmed, with the deputy leader declining to confirm it on air. It is now announced from the platform, and one figure has moved with it: the welfare saving reported overnight as £30bn is £36bn in the party’s published costings. The terms are that the nil-rate band rises from £325,000 to £500,000 a person, so £1m for a couple, and the home leaves the estate altogether however much it is worth. BBC Verify’s worked example: an estate with a £2m house facing about £400,000 today would face nothing. Most couples can already pass on about £1m under existing allowances, so the real gain is the uncapped exemption on the house. Abolishing the tax outright remains an ambition “as soon as we can afford it”, not a pledge. Around 30,400 deaths in 2024-25 left a liable estate, about 5% of the total; the Institute for Fiscal Studies expects 12% by 2032-33 and says the biggest beneficiaries “would be those with the highest wealth and the most valuable homes”. The sharpest objection is mechanical rather than moral. Professor Andy Summers of the Centre for the Analysis of Taxation calls it “just about the worst possible way to deliver an inheritance tax cut”, because exempting the home whatever its value will “encourage older people to concentrate their savings in their home, prevent downsizing, and so further gum up the housing market” — which is where the supply for first-time buyers comes from.

Worst Parliament for Living Standards Since Records Began in 1961

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation says average household incomes after housing costs will be between £440 and £770 lower in real terms in 2029-30 than when the government took office two years ago. Dive deeper
Chris Belfield, the foundation’s chief economist: “Without bold policy action on living standards, families are set to be poorer than they were a decade ago. This is terrible both for families who are just treading water to get by and the more than 7 million families who are already routinely going without essentials like food and basic toiletries.” The analysis used the Bank of England’s July energy projections, and notes that events have tracked the adverse scenario rather than the central one, with oil back above $100 a barrel. Markets are now pricing four quarter-point rate rises to 4.75% by the end of 2027, the average five-year fixed mortgage went through 6% this week for the first time in three years, and some forecasts put the typical dual-fuel bill up 16% to around £2,000 a year in the first quarter of 2027. Against that, John Healey is expected to offer about £1bn of extra energy support at the Budget, and is reported to be minded to rebuff the energy secretary’s call to spend billions more stripping levies out of bills; the likely route is a higher warm homes discount, though that remains reporting rather than announced policy. Louise Haigh is running two cross-government taskforces on living standards and has told officials there will have to be “trade offs”. This analysis rests on a single source and should be read as one think tank’s projection.

Royal Mail to Cut 2,500 Jobs as Letter Volumes Collapse

Royal Mail will cut 2,500 head-office and other non-frontline roles by the end of 2027, just under 2% of a workforce of more than 131,000. Posties and drivers are excluded, and the company says it will use voluntary redundancy and natural wastage rather than compulsory cuts. Consultation with the unions has begun.

Dive deeper
Letter volumes have fallen by more than 70% since the mid-2000s, which is the fact underneath everything else here. Alistair Cochrane, the chief executive: “These proposed changes remove duplication and allow us to invest further in the service we deliver for our customers... The proposed changes will not be easy, but they are an important part of building a stronger, simpler and future-ready Royal Mail.” The money saved is directed at a £500m investment programme over five years. Martin Walsh, deputy general secretary of the Communication Workers Union, reads it the other way: “further evidence of a company that is demoralising staff and failing to deliver for customers”, and he wants ministers to “confront the reality of a collapsing Royal Mail and intervene to save this national institution”. The union says about 200 of its own members, in administration and revenue protection, are affected. Unite blames the company, Ofcom and the government for failing to answer competition from gig-economy delivery firms. The service record is the part that reaches a letterbox: just over 75% of first-class post arrived on time in the year to the end of March against a 93% target, the company has been fined by Ofcom for missing it, and more than a hundred MPs have written to the regulator and the business secretary about delivery in their constituencies this year. Royal Mail has been owned by Daniel Křetínský’s EP Group since a £3.6bn takeover.

Wittington Investments, the Weston family holding company, is buying Boots with the Toronto-based Fairfax Financial Holdings in a deal worth $8.9bn, or about £6.7bn. We see a meaningful opportunity to make a great business even better through stable long-term ownership, further capital investment, and the renewed operating focus required to serve customers with excellence for generations to come.” Mr Pessina called it “an iconic brand and a British institution”. The Westons are not new to British retail: they owned Selfridges from 2003 to 2021, and a separate branch of the family holds the majority of Associated British Foods, which owns Primark. In Canada they own Loblaws and Shoppers Drug Mart, the closest equivalent to what they have just bought. The family is fifth on this year’s Sunday Times Rich List at nearly £19bn. The deal still needs regulatory approval, and the phrase to hold them to is “stable long-term ownership” after eighteen months of private equity.

A Tanker Is Attacked in Hormuz and Eleven Indians Hurt

Oman’s air force has evacuated ten crew from the Panamanian-flagged On Peace after a projectile struck it nine nautical miles off Limah on Tuesday, setting the vessel alight. India’s foreign ministry says at least twelve of the nineteen crew were injured, eleven of them Indian nationals; seventeen of the crew were Indian. No group has claimed the attack.

Dive deeper
The shipping intelligence service Marisks counts at least seven other tanker incidents in the past week. Iran effectively closed the waterway when the war began on 28 February, has repeatedly attacked vessels that do not use its designated route, and says it wants to charge ships for passage. India, the largest single supplier of seafarers to merchant shipping, said in July that at least ten Indian sailors had been killed since late February; another Hormuz attack in September killed one more. The counter-intuitive part is the cargo. Middle East crude exports are back to pre-war levels — a seven-day moving average of 18.3 million barrels a day on 30 September, against about 18 million a day in the twelve months before the war, on provisional Kpler figures. What has not normalised is the price: Brent closed Tuesday at $101.51 against $72.48 the day before the war began. That gap is insurance, detours and risk premium rather than any shortage of barrels. The evacuated crew were treated at Khasab hospital in Musandam. Watch for attribution, because an unclaimed strike on a Panamanian hull with a mostly Indian crew pushes Delhi towards a posture it has spent eight months avoiding.

Three Years On From 7 October, With All Hostages Home

Memorials are being held at the Nova festival site near Kibbutz Reim and at intersections and bomb shelters across southern Israel, three years after the Hamas-led attack that killed about 1,200 people and took 251 hostage. It is the first anniversary with every hostage returned. A flag flew at half mast at Kibbutz Kfar Aza this morning.

Dive deeper
Elkana Bohbot, 37, abducted from the Nova festival and released after 738 days, is marking the day in Israel for the first time. He says he was held fifty metres underground in a dark, damp tunnel, in a small cell with other hostages, fed very little, allowed to bathe only every few weeks, and forced into propaganda videos, one of them a staged call to his family. In August his wife Rivka gave birth to a daughter, Michele, fulfilling a promise he made to himself in the tunnels. Gaza’s health ministry, which does not separate civilians from combatants but says around half the dead were women and children, counts more than 74,000 killed; United Nations agencies and independent experts regard its figures as broadly reliable. Since the ceasefire took effect on 10 October last year the ministry counts 1,145 Palestinians killed, and seven Israeli soldiers have died in the same period. The politics sits underneath the grief. The government held its official service on 5 October, the Hebrew-calendar date, and the split commemorations track the argument over Benjamin Netanyahu’s conduct of the war and his reluctance to order an inquiry into the failures of the day itself. An Israeli election falls at the end of this month.

Thursday marks thirty days since Israel’s foreign minister Gideon Saar ordered the closure of the British consulate general in East Jerusalem, in retaliation for Britain’s sanctions on illegal West Bank settlements. Reports on Monday that Britain could expel twenty-seven Israeli diplomats are unverified. Fean’s practical warning is the one to watch: the consulate depends on Israeli electricity and water.

A Quarter of a Million March in France’s School Revolt

The interior ministry counted 266,000 demonstrators across France on Tuesday, 56,000 of them in Paris, in the largest day of student protest in decades. Puigdemont Can Go Home, and May Hold Spain’s Balance

Spain’s Constitutional Court has ruled that the amnesty law for Catalan separatists covers the embezzlement charges against Carles Puigdemont, and a Supreme Court judge lifted his arrest warrant on Tuesday. Dive deeper
Jordi Turull, secretary-general of Junts, put it as a verdict on the state rather than a court ruling: “They held firm, they resisted, they prevailed in Europe, and today they have prevailed against the Spanish state.” Pedro Sánchez says he does not regret the amnesty and welcomed the decision — “the important message is that of coexistence among Catalans” — which is easier to say now than it was in 2023, when Junts votes were the only reason he could form a government, given in exchange for those amnesties. The arithmetic is where it becomes interesting. Dive deeper
What has been trailed and what has been confirmed are not the same thing, which is worth holding on to all day. The Times reports she will announce a cut to the 40% inheritance tax rate, which bites above £325,000, or £500,000 where a home passes to children or grandchildren. The deputy leader Alex Burghart would not confirm it on BBC Breakfast: “Kemi has said she’d like to abolish inheritance tax but it’s very important we can afford any announcement we can make”, with the shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith still working out what is possible. Healey Lines Up Over £1bn Against January’s Energy Bills

The chancellor is preparing an intervention worth more than £1bn at the 28 October Budget, most of it a bigger discount for households on certain benefits, after forecasts that the war with Iran will push the January price cap up by as much as £442. The most likely route is raising the warm homes discount from £150 by a further £100.

Dive deeper
A £442 rise would entirely erode the VAT cut Andy Burnham announced on becoming prime minister, which is why the Treasury has moved at all. Mr Healey is expected to rebuff the energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, who wants levies stripped from bills altogether — worth up to £120 off every bill, but costing as much as £3.2bn. Officials are working up two options for after the Budget: a social tariff, under which poorer households pay less per unit, and a rising block tariff, with a cheap essential block and a dearer one above it, a design the think tank Fahnbulleh used to run first proposed. Alex Chapman of the New Economics Foundation: “A serious price spike is coming, and without action, bills will exceed the peak of the last crisis.” Alfie Stirling of the Joseph Rowntree Foundation argues support “needs to support the majority of households” and should lower the price itself, thereby bringing down inflation. Against that, the chancellor has £4.7bn of extra defence spending to find and a fiscal buffer to rebuild, and is reported to be eyeing higher bank taxes. The Treasury would not engage. Watch what households above the benefit threshold are offered, because on this plan the answer is nothing.

Holborn and St Pancras Votes Tomorrow With Labour Ahead

Labour has held it since its creation in 1983 and is the favourite; Polanski calls the contest “really on a knife-edge” and the deputy Labour leader Lucy Powell says a Green win is “unlikely”. Labour figures are now ruling out ever working with the Greens.

Dive deeper

Labour’s chair Bridget Phillipson called it “shameful”; one minister cannot foresee the parties working together and a government official wants a coalition formally ruled out. Senior Liberal Democrats want their Southwark coalition ended, the peer Lynne Featherstone saying: “I would ditch them.
