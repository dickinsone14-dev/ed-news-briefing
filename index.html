What It Means For You

Zelensky Asks the EU for at Least €70bn More Next Year

Volodymyr Zelensky told EU leaders in Brussels that the war will cost €155bn in 2027 and that “we need at least €70bn more from you – for defence, for weapons, and for our resilience”. He answered Donald Trump’s description of Kyiv’s renewed refinery strikes as “a disgrace” by saying: “If I don’t stand up to this… who else will?” Starlink speeds, he said, are still not fully restored.

Dive deeper
The money is the hard part. The EU’s €90bn plan agreed last December was meant to cover two-thirds of Ukraine’s needs for 2026–27, and the shortfall has put Russia’s €210bn of frozen assets back on the table; Belgium, which holds most of them, remains opposed, and Mr Zelensky floated an EU-controlled custodian instead. Ursula von der Leyen promised a 22nd sanctions package: “We will not lift any sanctions unless Russia stops the war.” Washington is moving the other way: the American diesel deal with Moscow, which Mr Zelensky says will “put more money straight into Putin’s pocket”, is the root of the quarrel. A bipartisan group of senators has asked the Pentagon’s inspector general to investigate the Starlink curbs.

Trump Rules Out Nuclear Weapons and Ground Troops in Iran

“I wouldn’t use nuclear. We don’t have to,” the president told Newsmax, and on troops on the ground: “No, I don’t see that.” He neither confirmed nor denied reports that the Pentagon has drawn up plans to use the Massive Ordnance Air Blast bomb. An Iranian MP warned that using it would make American bases and allies “legitimate targets”; oil rose almost 3% this afternoon.

Dive deeper
CNN reports that Mr Trump has ordered strike packages including “a lot of energy infrastructure” and that plans for Iranian nuclear sites, including Pickaxe Mountain, explicitly call for the MOAB — though sources doubt it can penetrate the granite there. The White House says only that the president “keeps all options on the table”; he has said he will not resume strikes before the 3 November midterms. Tehran’s acting oil minister, Hamid Bovard, promised “precise, appropriate retaliatory measures at the right time” against threats to energy plants. At sea the attrition goes on: the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre recorded twelve attacks on vessels in the past week, one ship lost, and Hormuz transits about 70% below pre-conflict levels.

Settlers Beat a Washington Post Photographer During the Olive Harvest

Dozens of masked Israeli settlers attacked Palestinians picking olives near Abu Falah, north-east of Ramallah, setting trees and cars alight. A local official said twelve Palestinians were hurt, four of them dual American citizens. Dive deeper
A BBC team witnessed the attack, the arrival of Israeli forces and tear gas fired into the fields. The United Nations says the past fortnight has seen more settler attacks than the whole of last year’s harvest, with one Palestinian killed and more than 120 injured this season. The Washington Post’s executive editor, Matt Murray, said Ms Levine was clearly identifiable as press and demanded “a thorough investigation”; the Israeli military said “all circumstances” would be examined. The State Department said it condemned “criminal violence by any party”. Earlier this month an AFP photographer, Jaafar Ashtiyeh, was also beaten covering the harvest; the Committee to Protect Journalists calls it “a disturbing pattern”.

Dive deeper
The protests began as a student movement over education costs and the cost of living, but they draw on a summer of peaceful “Flamingo” demonstrations against a coastal resort project linked to Jared Kushner and on growing corruption allegations: one deputy prime minister has fled the country and another has been told to step down. Mr Rama also blamed the opposition leader Sali Berisha, though Berisha’s own rallies have been far smaller. Police made 65 arrests on Wednesday, and Tirana’s hospitals treated 89 people, 61 of them officers. The stakes are European: Albania is among the leading candidates for EU membership, and Mr Rama says his mandate is to finish those negotiations. The Foreign Office advises visitors to avoid demonstrations.

IMF Tells Healey to Look at Pensions and Welfare, Not Banks

The International Monetary Fund has urged the chancellor to find savings rather than tax the banks at the Budget. “With taxes roughly at 40% of GDP – historically, for Britain, a high number – then of course balancing these measures on the fiscal side… makes good sense,” said Helge Berger, citing the triple lock and benefits. Lloyds and NatWest shares fell on expectations of a tax rise.

Dive deeper
The Treasury is understood to be leaning towards the bank surcharge, cut from 8% to 3% in 2023; reversing it fully would raise about £2bn, though it may not go all the way back. UK Finance says doing so would leave GDP £1.2bn lower by 2029–30 and lending £10.4bn lower. The other side is equally specific: the TUC says the cut has cost £6bn in three years, and the big four made £29.2bn in the first half. Sir Philip Augar, the former banker, framed it on the Today programme: “Banks can’t be too big to fail and too big to tax.” If more revenue is needed, Mr Berger suggested property taxes or VAT. The Treasury says tax decisions are for fiscal events.

Dive deeper

Mr Leech’s question is whether a fund meant to unlock “currently unfundable” projects should have gone to well-capitalised borrowers. The timing is awkward for the prime minister: he has asked for a “manual trawl” of his past contacts with the club after records of two dinners with its chairman disappeared from the authority’s website. The Conservatives say he has “serious questions to answer”; Downing Street did not comment.

Dive deeper

Government Commits to Overhaul the Vaccine Damage Payment Scheme

Ministers have promised to review the scheme that compensates people injured or bereaved by vaccines, responding to the Covid Inquiry’s call for “urgent” reform. More than 24,000 Covid-related claims have been made, but just over 1% have produced the one-off £120,000 award. Campaigners say a review is not a commitment: “Today, we have no clear timetable.”

Dive deeper
Baroness Hallett’s fourth report, on vaccines and therapeutics, found the scheme “not sufficiently supportive”, and in particular that the requirement to show at least 60% disability does not work for Covid vaccine injuries; most claims relate to the AstraZeneca jab. The running costs are the awkward number: the scheme has cost £83m to administer since April 2022, more than double the £32m actually paid out. Vaccine Injured and Bereaved UK welcomed the acknowledgement that the scheme is not fit for purpose, but said that after five years of campaigning “there is a real difference between saying you will look at something and committing to deliver it”.

Three Children and a Teacher Shot Dead at an Oregon School

A sixteen-year-old forced his way into Silver Rail Elementary School in Bend by shooting at locked doors, killing three children aged five, eight and eleven, and a teacher. A seven-year-old and a second teacher are critically injured. The gunman was shot dead by a member of the public who saw him go in and followed him inside.

Dive deeper
The police account is unusually precise about timing. The first emergency call came at 12:59 local time from a driver who said they were being shot at by someone with a long gun; a force-wide alert went out at 13:00, the school locked down at 13:01, and the first officers arrived at 13:02 to find the suspect already dead. “An officer reported that the suspect had been shot by a community member who had seen the suspect enter the school and pursued him into the school and engaged him,” the police chief Mike Krantz said. Neither the gunman nor the man who stopped him has been named, and no motive has been given. Oregon’s governor Tina Kotek said he “heroically intervened to stop further loss of life” and added a thought for what that will cost him: “your actions carry a heavy emotional burden.” The school has more than 400 pupils and over forty staff. Reported Attacks on Shipping Near Hormuz Hit a Record High

Twelve vessels reported being attacked in and around the Strait of Hormuz between 5 and 11 October, the most in any week since the war began, with six struck last Sunday alone. Flows through the strait have fallen to about 12.6 million barrels a day, against 15 million at the end of September and roughly 17 million before the war.

Dive deeper
The pattern has changed, not just the count. Martin Kelly of Orbital Risk Intelligence says Iran has “evolved its strategy” into “near daily or multiple attacks every single day” and is explicit about the purpose: “The aim here is to stop the ships going through and to deter others.” American naval escorting is up 85% in the first ten days of this month against the same period in August, and crossings still fell to 131 in the week to 14 October from 176. No major container line has resumed services through the strait since suspending them in February. The human cost is on the smaller vessels: eleven Indian seafarers were rescued from the tanker Petro Dolphin after a drone strike off Oman and two are still missing. Yemen is the other front, and it worsened overnight — the strike on the presidential palace in Aden has now killed five and wounded sixteen, according to the Yemeni health minister Qasim Buhaibeh, where the figures last night were two and four. United States Central Command says traffic “continues to flow… with co-ordinated protection”, which is the American military describing its own operation.

Officials Say the Starlink Order Came From Inside the White House

The instruction to slow Ukraine’s military satellite service came from within the White House, two American officials and a Western official have said, with the Pentagon told the service “needed to be slowed”. A senior official denies it narrowly: “No such request was ever made by the president.” Britain’s defence secretary says his American counterpart has assured him the service is back.

Dive deeper
Two cautions. The sourcing is anonymous and the denial covers only the president — the White House declined to say whether anyone else gave the instruction, and no official would name who decided or who carried it out. And “restored” is doing a lot of work: a Ukrainian defence ministry source said receptors were working while moving but only up to 100mph, which is the cap that disabled interceptor drones in the first place. Wes Streeting said Pete Hegseth told him face to face that the service was back, while admitting “it’s not yet clear to us in the UK what happened”. Meanwhile the dispute that started it has hardened. Donald Trump called the Ukrainian refinery strikes “a disgrace”, saying “all he’s doing is making the world oil price higher”, and added that he finds Volodymyr Zelensky “more difficult to deal with than Putin”. Vladimir Putin was dismissive of the strikes — “they hit here and there. Nothing particularly significant” — and promised a response. Russia sent more than 850 drones at fifteen Ukrainian regions overnight, killing three in Kirovohrad.

Fed Chairman Declines to Signal an October Rate Rise

Kevin Warsh, asked at the International Monetary Fund meetings in Bangkok whether American rates will rise this month, would not say: “I don’t prejudge meetings. I don’t believe in saying now what I might do.” Traders now put the chance of no change on 27 and 28 October at 72.3%. Asian shares rebounded, with Seoul up 2.8% and Tokyo 1.8%.

Dive deeper
This is a reversal of the position two days ago, and worth saying so plainly: after Wednesday’s inflation figure the market put an October rise at close to even money, having been at about one in five before it. It is now roughly one in four, and Goldman Sachs economists led by Jan Hatzius read the remarks as settling it — “the FOMC is set to pause in October” — while still expecting a rise in December. Mr Warsh was pointed about the change of regime at the central bank, which raised rates last month for the first time since 2023: “It’s a new chapter at the Fed and we’re not in that business anymore.” On the technology boom he was optimistic but hedged, warning the road will be “very bumpy”. The rebound in Asia undid most of Thursday’s rout and followed Nvidia’s chief executive Jensen Huang saying of the most indebted data-centre builder that “their cost of capital is actually decreasing”. One market went the other way: Indonesia’s index fell 11% after being told it no longer merits emerging-market status.

Alberta votes on Monday on whether to instruct its government to begin the legal process towards a future referendum on leaving Canada. At a rally in Calgary the prime minister warned that “some outside our borders are working to break us so they can own us” and that a vote against Canada would be “a dangerous bluff”.

Dive deeper
Monday’s question is narrower than independence: it asks whether the provincial government should start down the legal road, not whether the province should leave. That distinction matters because Alberta holds the world’s fourth-largest proven oil reserves, which is what gives the vote weight well beyond Canada. Mark Carney did not name the United States in the passage above, though he has before. He conceded the grievance was real — Ottawa had made Albertans feel “like our resources weren’t our own” — and offered a pipeline he says would create more than 140,000 jobs and cut dependence on American buyers, joking that Washington “might think that’s election interference” after its ambassador warned Canadian politicians against meddling in the midterms. His office put the crowd at about 5,000, some queuing more than two hours, with a second rally in Edmonton today. The campaign on the other side is unimpressed: Tanya Clemens of Let Alberta Decide called him “formidable for sure” but dismissed his agreements with the premier Danielle Smith as “pinky promises”.

Banks Say a Tax Rise on Their Profits Is Planned

Senior banking executives say the chancellor intends to raise taxes on the sector at the Budget on 28 October, and that banks are “on the menu”. Bank shares fell at the open, with Lloyds the biggest faller on the FTSE 100, down about 1.9%, and NatWest down 1.6%. Nothing has been announced; the Treasury says tax decisions are for the chancellor.

Dive deeper
The mechanics matter more than the headline. Banks pay a 3% surcharge on top of the 25% corporation tax rate, cut from 8% under Rishi Sunak at the same time as the main rate rose from 19%; restoring it would raise around £2bn a year. A second option now in play, and absent from yesterday’s reporting, is a temporary halt to banks offsetting historic losses against profits, worth perhaps another £1bn a year — one executive calls that “a lot more bearable”. The sector’s own figure of nearly £40bn contributed to the Treasury last year only reaches that total once income tax paid by employees is counted; the taxes banks pay directly were far smaller, £1.4bn from the levy and £1.2bn from the surcharge. The big four made about £15bn in the first half of this year. UK Finance warns higher taxes would “weaken competitiveness, discourage investment and job creation”. Sir Philip Augar, a former City banker, is unsympathetic: the sector cannot be both “too big to fail and too big to tax”. Separately, Andrew Bailey said in Bangkok that second-round effects from energy prices look “quite subdued” so far.

Three Kinds of Pornography Become Criminal on 6 November

From that date it will be illegal in England and Wales to possess or publish pornography depicting strangulation or suffocation, sexual activity between people who are or pretend to be related, or adults role-playing as children under sixteen. Publication carries up to five years; possession carries up to three years for the third category and two for the others.

Dive deeper
Non-fatal strangulation is already an offence where there is no consent; what changes is that depicting it online becomes one too. The measure arrives through amendments to the Crime and Policing Act, which received Royal Assent on 29 April. The justice minister Alex Davies-Jones drew the line she says it does not cross — “this isn’t about criminalising harmless sexual preferences” — while arguing that “content that normalises, or even glorifies, violence against women is dangerous and can blur the line with what is acceptable in real life”. Baroness Bertin, who chaired the review that recommended it, said the change comes “not a moment too soon”; her report found choking rife on popular sites and cited a fourteen-year-old boy asking a teacher how to choke girls during sex. Professor Clare McGlynn of Durham University calls it “a potential turning point… a line in the sand” but is realistic about enforcement: “this is not about prosecuting individuals, but transforming the online pornographic landscape and that requires the platforms and regulator to act.” Campaigners want the Online Safety Act updated to compel removal.

Fresh undercover footage shows the Reform leader pushing back when senior staff said a mayoral candidate accused of racist remarks about David Lammy had to go. His communications chief, who had called the candidate “clearly a racist” and said “he needs to go, I’m getting rid of him”, told him to his face: “You’re protecting him.”

Dive deeper
This is new material rather than yesterday’s, and it reaches the leader personally. His reply, to laughter, was that an apology would do: “It’s showing a bit of… bit of sorrow… Just take the deal. Trouble is he’s a rear admiral. That’s the problem.” The candidate, Chris Parry, stayed on for months after it emerged he had said Mr Lammy should “go home to the Caribbean”; he was suspended in March, says there was never any investigation and has since left. He defends himself by pointing to context, saying he exercises his “right to criticise anyone in power… whom I judge to be acting against the interests of the British people”. The footage also has Sarah Pochin, one of Reform’s two female MPs, on her own colleagues: “they just exclude you if you’re a woman. Burnham Offers Holyrood a Deal to Devolve Power Further

The prime minister, in North Lanarkshire today, invites the first minister to work with him on pushing power below Holyrood, arguing that “power was never supposed to stop at the doors of the Scottish Parliament”. The visit falls on the weekend of the Scottish National Party conference, where John Swinney will make the case for independence tomorrow.

Dive deeper
The offer is specific: that the Scottish government work with the government’s northern office and the Scotland Office on what is called a new model of regional empowerment. “This is me making an offer to the Scottish Government,” Andy Burnham said. “With us working together, there is no limit to what we can achieve for the Scottish people.” He framed the argument around places rather than institutions — people in North Lanarkshire, Fife or the Highlands “know what their communities need better than any minister, whether they sit in London or Edinburgh”. The politics underneath it are less generous. Mr Swinney wants a second referendum by 2031 and has warned the prime minister against stripping powers from Holyrood; Mr Burnham has told him a second vote remains off limits, having earlier suggested to MPs that a border poll could be considered given clear consensus and changed opinion. The Conservatives are circling the same ground from the other side: Kemi Badenoch, in Edinburgh, said her party has “no plans” to remove powers but “maybe we need a review”, adding that devolution “is not a religion which we should worship”.

A Same-Day Test Is Finding Infections Doctors Were Missing

A test that sequences all the genetic material in a sample, rather than looking for an infection a clinician has guessed at in advance, found hidden and drug-resistant infections in critically ill children at Great Ormond Street Hospital. It is being rolled out to around thirty intensive care units across the United Kingdom.

Dive deeper
The study covered 122 children in the hospital’s paediatric intensive care unit across 2024 and 2025, with respiratory samples from more than a hundred of them, and has now been published in a peer-reviewed journal. The advantage is breadth: a single sample is checked for bacteria, viruses and fungi at once, so the answer does not depend on the right question being asked first. Dr Sam Ray, a consultant in paediatric intensive care medicine at the hospital and a co-author, describes the effect in both directions: “The study has helped us to spot infections we would have missed, get children on the right treatment and stop antibiotics when we know they aren’t going to work.” That second half matters as much as the first, given what resistance costs. One case shows the stakes. Willow Crowther, ten, from Kent, developed pneumonia in March that triggered a seizure and multiple cardiac arrests; her mother was told she had a fifty-fifty chance and to bring the family to say goodbye. The test found a drug-resistant bacterial infection needing a different antibiotic. She spent several months in hospital and recovered.

Houthi Missiles Hit Yemen’s Presidential Palace in Aden

Five ballistic missiles struck the al-Maashiq palace in Aden, the seat of the internationally recognised government. The prime minister and other ministers were unhurt. The information minister Moammar al-Eryani put the dead at two security personnel, while government military sources gave four; many others were injured. Houthi-run media said it had hit a military operations headquarters led by Saudi officers.

Dive deeper
Yemen’s civil war has returned to full-scale fighting, and the kingdom is now openly blaming Tehran for it. A Saudi foreign ministry official said today that Iran “continues to arm, train and support the Houthi militia” and that “the escalation by the Houthis at this latest stage of the conflict is encouraged by Iran”. Saudi Arabia says it intercepted a ballistic missile over the Medina area, close to one of Islam’s holiest cities; the Houthis denied responsibility for that one. Saturday’s strike on Riyadh’s international airport killed thirteen, the deadliest attack in Saudi Arabia in more than a decade, and King Khalid International remains shut until Saturday. Seven Missing After a Drone Attack Sets a Tanker Ablaze Off Romania

Seven of twelve crew are unaccounted for from the San Marino-flagged Wine Trader, which reported explosions and fire thirteen nautical miles east of the mouth of the Danube this morning. Romania’s president said a second chemical tanker had also been struck. Moscow said it had hit two cargo ships carrying military cargo bound for Odesa. Romania and Bulgaria are weighing whether to invoke Article 4.

Dive deeper
Article 4 of the North Atlantic Treaty lets any member call for consultations when it believes its security is threatened; it has been invoked only a handful of times and is the step below the collective-defence clause.
