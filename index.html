GEO Geopolitical

Houthis Seize Mocha, Turning the War Towards a Second Chokepoint

Houthi forces captured the Red Sea port of Mocha overnight — confirmed by four Yemeni government military sources — along with the inland city of Hays and the region’s largest military base. Government commander Tareq Saleh ordered a retreat south: his forces “paid a heavy price — martyrs and wounded — over the past days as they confronted the Houthi attack planned by Iran, and supported by it”. The advance points at Bab el-Mandeb, gateway to the Red Sea.

Dive deeper
Mocha is not the prize; it is the staging post — south of the city lie Dhubab and the Perim island narrows, and whoever holds them holds Bab el-Mandeb, through which every Suez-bound tanker and container ship must pass. Iran’s proxies closing on a second chokepoint while Tehran squeezes Hormuz would give the axis a hand on both of the world’s critical oil straits simultaneously — the scenario energy planners have gamed for decades without believing it. The offensive’s timing, days after Iranian commanders reportedly ordered attacks on Saudi Arabia, reads as coordination rather than coincidence. Saudi jets were already answering by morning. Watch the line at Dhubab — the distance between the Houthis and the strait is now measured in kilometres, not provinces.

Seven Killed in 149-Drone Barrage; NATO Jets Scramble Over Poland

Russia sent 149 drones at Ukraine overnight — jet-powered Shaheds among decoy swarms — with 128 downed but the leakers deadly: four killed in Mykolaiv, three in a strike on a Sumy shopping centre, emergency workers injured by a secondary blast in Khmelnytskyi. NATO military aircraft deployed in Polish airspace in response to strikes on western Ukraine, a day after Kyiv’s drones reached 3,200km into Siberia.

Dive deeper
An 86% interception rate is simultaneously a triumph and an indictment: the arithmetic of drone war means twenty-one leakers from a single night still find shopping centres and homes, and Russia’s mix of decoys is engineered precisely to spend Ukrainian interceptors on Gerberas so Shaheds get through. The NATO scramble over Poland is the recurring tell that this war’s geography is probabilistic — jet-powered drones move too fast for comfortable margins, and alliance aircraft now fly weekly sorties against the possibility of error. Meanwhile both capitals’ theories of coercion escalate in lockstep: deeper Ukrainian strikes, denser Russian barrages, each labelled leverage for talks neither side schedules. Watch Poland’s tally of airspace incidents — the alliance’s red line is being probed nightly at drone speed.

And if anything goes bad, we hit them” — and advised Tehran “not to get cute because we will have to hit them very hard”. He repeated that the war ends “immediately after the election”, and promised $5,000 “dividend” payments to citizens if Republicans win in November.

Dive deeper
Naming Pickaxe Mountain from a convention stage converts an intelligence question into a political commitment: the fortified tunnels near Natanz are where Iran’s unverified 440 kilograms may sit, the IAEA cannot look, and a president who has now publicly wagered that “we know everybody that’s moving” there has pre-authorised his own escalation the moment surveillance says otherwise. The $5,000 dividend — already being walked back by his vice-president — belongs to the same speech for a reason: war, oil prices and cash payments are being braided into a single electoral offer, with advisers privately warning the conflict could outlast the term that offer is meant to win. Watch activity reports around Pickaxe — the war’s biggest single escalation now has a named trigger.

Hormuz Down to Seven Ships a Day as Iran Claims Attacks on Ten

Just seven commodity vessels transited the Strait of Hormuz on Wednesday — down from twelve the day before and half the ten-day average — with not a single LNG tanker leaving the Gulf. Iran said it attacked ten ships near the strait after America sank five of its tankers. Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea workaround is carrying the load: Yanbu loadings rebounded to 3.7 million barrels a day — the very route the Houthi advance now threatens.

Dive deeper
The transit numbers are the war’s truest ledger: seven ships where dozens once passed daily is closure in everything but proclamation, achieved without mines or a single sunk neutral — insurance and instruction did the work. The system’s remaining resilience runs through exactly two paths: Saudi and Emirati pipelines to the Red Sea, and the Cape route’s weeks of extra steaming — which is why Mocha’s fall is not a Yemeni story but a global one, threatening the workaround at the moment it became indispensable. Zero LNG exits should alarm Europe specifically; Qatari cargoes are the marginal molecule in winter gas pricing. Watch Bab el-Mandeb’s own transit count — twenty-eight ships passed on Wednesday; that number is now the one that matters.

Riyadh Warns Tehran Through Pakistan; Iran Denies Controlling Houthis

Saudi Arabia passed a warning to Iran through Pakistan to rein in the Houthi offensive; Tehran’s reply — “Iran does not control the Houthis” — was undercut by two Iranian sources who said Tehran told the group last week to attack the kingdom, promising money and weapons, with IRGC commanders travelling to Yemen to coordinate. Saudi jets flew as many as forty strikes in a day; one monitoring group counted 276 dead in five days of escalation.

Dive deeper
The choice of messenger is the message: routing the warning through Islamabad activates the Mecca defence pact’s newest signatory and reminds Tehran that Saudi Arabia’s security now has a nuclear-armed underwriter — the pact’s first real-world test arriving barely a month after its signing. Iran’s deniability formula, meanwhile, has aged into ritual: the same sources confirming direction, funding and embedded commanders make “we don’t control them” a claim about org-charts, not operations. For Riyadh the dilemma compounds daily — forty airstrikes a day did not hold Mocha, and the ground force that could is the one it spent years withdrawing. Watch whether Pakistan’s involvement stays postal or becomes material — and whether the pact’s deterrence survives its first ignored warning.

Mayors to Get Uncapped Tourist Tax Powers on Hotels and Short Lets

England’s mayors are to receive powers to levy an uncapped percentage charge on overnight stays — hotels, B&Bs and short lets alike — with Angela Rayner meeting regional leaders to confirm the scheme. Liverpool’s Steve Rotheram says a modest levy could raise £18m a year; the hospitality industry warns of £100 added to a family holiday. The Conservatives call it “a Labour double whammy” on top of VAT; Mr Farage brands it a “holiday tax” his mayors “won’t touch”.

Dive deeper
The design choice that matters is the absence of a cap: an uncapped percentage converts a visitor charge into a genuine fiscal instrument, the first new local tax base in a generation — and the first concrete deliverable of the fiscal-devolution agenda the Chancellor sketched on Monday, arriving before the Budget that will formalise it. The politics split three ways by geography rather than ideology: tourism-heavy mayors gain revenue their high streets need, hospitality operators absorb another levy atop VAT and wage costs, and refusal itself becomes a Reform selling point — taxation as identity. Edinburgh’s 5% and Wales’s flat charge offer early evidence either side will cite. Watch which mayors move first and at what rate — the postcode lottery in a hotel bill starts here.

Pharmacists to Treat Migraines and Acne as Scheme Doubles Reach

Pharmacies in England will treat five more common conditions — migraine, acne, scabies, ear infections and rhinitis — taking the Pharmacy First scheme to twelve, with qualified pharmacists prescribing directly and no GP appointment needed. The service has delivered around fourteen million consultations since its 2024 launch, three million of them referrals from NHS 111 and general practice.

Dive deeper
The quiet structural shift is prescribing authority: this expansion leans on pharmacists holding independent prescribing qualifications, the vanguard of a workforce change that makes every high-street chemist a clinical front door — and the conditions chosen, chronic-adjacent rather than purely acute, push further into territory GPs considered theirs. Fourteen million consultations is the counterfactual that matters: appointments that never joined the 8am scramble. The constraint is the sector’s own fragility — pharmacies have been closing at hundreds a year under funding pressure, and every new NHS service loaded onto them is a bet that the network survives to deliver it. Watch uptake in deprived areas, where GP scarcity is worst and pharmacy closures fastest — the scheme’s value is decided there.

Dive deeper

The polling already shows the mechanism: seven in ten Britons calling the party sleazy is survivable noise until a jury question makes it permanent. The counterintuitive risk is to fundraising itself — the file’s subject matter chills precisely the money the party needs for the election it wants early. Watch for interviews under caution; names will tell the direction of travel.

Dive deeper
“Highest since 2007” deserves its full weight: British ten-year money now costs more than at any point since before the financial crisis, the entire QE era’s repression fully unwound and a war premium stacked on top — and unlike 2007, it prices against public debt near 100% of GDP and a Budget seven weeks out. The four-hikes-priced curve is the market talking its own book against Mr Bailey’s protestations, and one of them must be wrong: either the Bank validates the path and mortgages reprice accordingly, or it holds and the curve’s inflation fears feed the pound instead. Every basis point lands directly in October’s arithmetic. Watch today’s ECB language — a hawkish Frankfurt drags the whole European curve, gilts first.

NATS Given a Week to Explain Itself as the Backlog Finally Clears

The structural question airlines keep forcing is liability: a monopoly whose failures cost its customers tens of millions while facing “little or no financial consequence” itself has no market discipline and, on this evidence, insufficient regulatory discipline either. The chief executive’s position now depends on a report his own organisation writes. Watch whether the week’s deadline is met and the cause named plainly — obfuscation there would tell its own story.

IAEA Refers Iran to Security Council — First Time in Twenty Years

The IAEA’s board voted 23 to 3 — Russia, China and Niger against, eight abstaining — to refer Iran’s nuclear non-compliance to the UN Security Council, the first such referral since 2006. The agency says Iran holds 440.9kg of uranium enriched to 60%, unverifiable since June’s strikes. Tehran’s envoy called the resolution “a political tool”, arguing Washington bombs safeguarded sites and then “uses that very disruption as a pretext”.

Dive deeper
The vote formalises what the war made true: the world’s nuclear watchdog can no longer see the world’s most-watched stockpile, and 440 kilograms of near-weapons-grade uranium now exists as an intelligence estimate rather than an inspected fact. The margin matters less than the split — Russia and China voting no is advance notice of Security Council paralysis, so the referral’s force is legal scaffolding rather than enforcement: it resets the predicate for snapback measures, coalition action, and whatever Washington intends at the fortified site its president keeps naming. Tehran’s pretext argument has an uncomfortable kernel — the verification gap is partly bomb-made. Watch the Council’s first session on the file, and any IAEA language on where the uranium may have moved.

The president’s promise that oil prices will be “tumbling downward” after the election is now a testable proposition attached to a date. Watch the Dallas convention rhetoric harden — and whether Iranian operations begin timing themselves to American polling.

Dive deeper
State funerals are the last great neutral ground, and this one doubled as a summit nobody had to schedule: half of NATO’s heads of state in one cathedral, a wartime president among them, in the week the alliance’s aircraft scrambled over Poland — the Norwegian crown’s quiet function as Europe’s meeting-house on display one last time for Harald. The genuine grief was national as much as royal: tens of thousands lining the streets for a sailor-king who embodied a particular unshowy Scandinavian legitimacy that populist Europe conspicuously lacks. For Britain, William’s presence — not the King’s — continues the gradual transfer of the heavy diplomatic lifting to the heir. Watch the new reign’s first months; Haakon inherits the throne of a country now on NATO’s most sensitive northern flank.

The incident’s ambiguity is itself the pattern: Russian drones over Moldova are deniable weather, and a near-miss that may have been incidental still functions as a message received. It lands in a week when a drone found the SBU chief’s window and another found a newsroom live on air — precision and plausible deniability travelling together. Watch how Zelensky’s future travel is disclosed; the quieter it becomes, the more seriously the threat was taken.
