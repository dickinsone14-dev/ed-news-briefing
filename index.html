US Envoys Arrive in Moscow Today With 'a Proposal to End the War'

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner travel to Moscow today to meet Mr Putin, then on to Kyiv — and they are not travelling empty-handed. “They are bringing with them a proposal to end the war,” President Trump said, blaming the deadlock on a “personality problem”: the two leaders “truly hate each other… and it’s getting in the way.” Notably, no Russian attack on Kyiv was reported overnight — and no Ukrainian “mirror” strike either. The toll from Friday’s SBU headquarters strike rose to twelve injured.

Dive deeper
The overnight silence over Kyiv is the first genuine signal in days: after a fortnight of nightly bombardment and a drone in the security chief’s window, both sides holding fire on the eve of talks suggests each wants the weekend to produce something — or wants the blame for failure worn by the other. What the “proposal” contains is unknown, and the war’s arithmetic explains the scepticism: Russia gained barely ninety square kilometres in August at enormous cost, yet Mr Putin has conceded nothing on territory, and Kyiv’s intelligence expects another three-hundred-thousand-man conscription within weeks. Mr Trump reducing a continental war to a “personality problem” is either naivety or a negotiator’s framing that lets both sides climb down without losing. Watch Sunday’s Kyiv leg — whether it happens at all is the weekend’s first verdict.

Trump Calls Iran War 'Small Potatoes' but Renews Pickaxe Mountain Threat

The president downplayed a war entering its seventh month — “It’s not a big thing… In Venezuela, we lost nobody. We control it very strongly.” Yet he repeated his threat to strike Pickaxe Mountain, the fortified site near Natanz, “very soon”. Dive deeper
The rebranding campaign — from “large and powerful” strikes to “small potatoes” in seventy-two hours — tracks the polling more faithfully than the battlefield: six in ten Americans disapprove, petrol is up nearly a dollar in a year, and a war shrunk to “intermittent strikes” is easier to carry into the midterms than one with a name. The Pickaxe Mountain threat cuts against the minimising, though: the buried enrichment complex near Natanz is precisely the target whose destruction could not be called small, and pairing the threat with the shrug keeps Tehran guessing which president will show up next week. The independent shipping data telling against “we control it very strongly” — seven transits a day against a pre-war hundred — remains the war’s quietest inconvenient fact. Watch whether “very soon” survives the weekend’s Moscow diplomacy; the two theatres are now openly linked.

Explosion at Bolivian Barracks Kills at Least Two; 10-15 Feared Dead

Authorities warned of the risk of further explosions and pushed residents 150 metres back as emergency workers searched rubble; dozens of homes were damaged and the town declared three days of mourning. Police say fireworks were stored at the site; the cause is under investigation.

Dive deeper
Fireworks stored in a military barracks in a town of eighty thousand is the kind of detail that turns an accident into an accountability crisis, and Bolivia’s will unfold along familiar lines: why civilian pyrotechnics sat in an artillery regiment’s stores, whose signature approved it, and whether the soldiers and neighbours buried this week died of corruption rather than misfortune. The toll’s wide range — two confirmed against fifteen feared — reflects a search still working through unstable rubble under threat of secondary blasts, and it will move. For a military already strained by the region’s upheavals, the images of its own barracks levelled by its own stores are a institutional wound no communiqué repairs. Watch the toll and the first resignation — in Latin American munitions disasters, the second usually follows the first within days.

AfD Bids for First Far-Right State Power in Tomorrow's German Vote

Saxony-Anhalt votes tomorrow with the AfD aiming for an outright majority — which would hand the far right state-level power for the first time in Germany’s post-war history, bypassing the “firewall” entirely since no party will govern with it. Lead candidate Ulrich Siegmund’s manifesto includes segregating refugee children into separate classes and a deportation task force; he has not ruled out abolishing the state’s domestic intelligence branch, which classes his party as right-wing extremist.

Dive deeper
The firewall was built for coalition arithmetic, and an outright majority is the one result it cannot contain — which is why tomorrow’s vote in a state of two million carries national weight: an AfD government would control police, schools and the very intelligence branch that surveils it, a live experiment in what the party does with actual power. The context Berlin cannot ignore is hybrid: this is the week Germany formally blamed Moscow for the Leipzig airport plot, and security services warn Russian-backed disinformation is flooding exactly these electorates. A strong result also tightens the vice on Chancellor Merz, already dogged by doubts he can see out his term, with the AfD leading national polls. Watch turnout and the margin — between “largest party” and “majority” lies the difference between crisis and constitutional rupture.

UN Votes 164-1 for New World Map Showing Africa's True Size

The General Assembly adopted Togo’s “Correct the Map” resolution, replacing the Mercator projection — which shows Africa comparable to Greenland despite being fourteen times larger — with the Equal Earth projection in institutional use. Britain and France voted in favour; the United States cast the only vote against. “A fair world begins with a fair map,” said Togo’s foreign minister. “A map is never neutral. It shapes perceptions.”

Dive deeper
Cartography is the softest of soft power, which is exactly why this vote repays attention: the Mercator projection was drawn for sixteenth-century navigation and stayed for four centuries of habit, quietly teaching every schoolchild that the global north looms and Africa shrinks — and a hundred and sixty-four governments deciding the habit should end is the global majority flexing on symbolic ground where flexing is cheap. France committing its foreign ministry to the switch shows how quickly non-binding becomes normative. The American solo vote against — on a map, of all hills — completes a week in which Washington priced its Falklands guarantee, invoiced Seoul for convoy duty and stood alone with increasing frequency; allies notice patterns made of small things. Watch which atlases, textbooks and newsrooms follow — adoption, not resolution, redraws the world.

Dive deeper
