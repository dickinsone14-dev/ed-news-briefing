Lecornu Calls for Partial Curfews as France Unrest Deepens

France’s prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, has called for “local and partial” curfews after the government admitted police had “lost control” in several places during violent weekend protests. Speaking after an emergency cabinet meeting, he said he was “fully resolved to restore public order”. President Macron turned down his interior minister’s offer to resign.

Dive deeper
Tens of thousands of people, angered by rising costs and declining public services, took to the streets across France over the weekend. Lecornu said curfews would be confined to certain areas, such as “symbolic, emblematic places”, rather than whole cities such as Paris; a state of emergency remains available “if deemed necessary”, though he judged partial curfews “more appropriate” for now. The disorder has spilled into the bond market, where French debt sold off on Monday. The test this week is whether targeted measures calm the streets or harden opposition to an already fragile government.

Lavrov Meets Araghchi in Tehran to Discuss the US War

Sergey Lavrov has held talks in Tehran with Abbas Araghchi on his first visit to Iran since February 2025, as Gulf states press Moscow to help halt Iranian attacks and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Araghchi said: “The existing insecurity around the Strait of Hormuz is a direct result of the military aggression by the US and [Israel] against Iran.”

Dive deeper
Araghchi insisted Iran’s policy was to focus on diplomacy, since regional problems have “no military solution”, and turned his fire on Europe, which he said suffers, “just like the Americans”, from miscalculations. On Iran’s eastern flank, Pakistan — itself a mediator between Tehran and Washington — has halted trade at the Taftan and Gabd-Rimdan crossings, stranding about 2,000 lorries; a local official called it “a temporary measure” to support counterterrorism efforts. Moscow’s value to Gulf capitals lies in its access to Tehran; whether that converts into pressure over shipping is the open question.

Iran Strikes at Least 15 Container Ships in Ten Days

Iran has attacked at least 15 container ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz in the past ten days, using drones and mines; twelve vessels were struck in the seven days to Friday, according to UKMTO. On Sunday a ship was hit by a rocket-propelled grenade fired from a small boat north-east of Jubail. Brent ended the London session near $110 after falling earlier on prospective Russian supply.

Dive deeper
Most attacks have been close to the Omani coast, a route Tehran regards as unauthorised, and the escalation appears to have reasserted Iran’s disputed control over the waterway. A vessel sailing from Karachi to Fujairah sank last Wednesday after hitting a mine; all 21 crew were evacuated. Earlier on Monday Brent had slipped to $107.80 as traders weighed extra Russian barrels after Friday’s US sanctions relief, while Ukraine’s drone strikes set fire to an oil terminal in St Petersburg. The market is now caught between two supply shocks pulling in opposite directions.

Zelensky Says Russian Strikes Came With ‘No Warning From Partners’

Volodymyr Zelensky said Ukraine endured another night of Russian missile and drone strikes “and again there was no warning from partners”, renewing his accusation that Washington is limiting intelligence. Energy infrastructure was the main target, though a clinic, a school and a metro station were also hit. The Trump administration denies changing what it shares.

Dive deeper
The row follows Donald Trump’s weekend charge that Zelensky does not want to end the war, and Washington’s easing of sanctions to allow Russian energy sales in a bid to lower US fuel prices. Ukraine also says it still cannot use Starshield, the military version of Starlink, to intercept drones. Marco Rubio said he was “not sure Zelenskyy knows what he’s talking about, but it wasn’t deliberate”, adding that the slowdown “sort of happened” and had been restored. Kyiv fears the softer US line will embolden Moscow as winter approaches.

Houthi Missile Strike on Yemeni Camp Kills More Than 50

More than 50 security personnel were killed and 200 injured when the Houthis fired three ballistic missiles at a training camp near Ataq in Shabwa province, local authorities say — far above earlier tolls. Rashad al-Alimi, head of the Presidential Leadership Council, condemned a “criminal attack” and promised a “firm response”.

Dive deeper
The Houthis said they had targeted “Saudi enemy concentrations”. Yemen’s civil war has flared since September, when the Houthis seized ground overlooking the Bab al-Mandab Strait; a government offensive launched at the start of October, backed by Saudi-led air power, has since retaken part of the Red Sea coast. Fighting continues there, around besieged Taiz and in Marib and al-Jawf, and the Houthis have answered Saudi air strikes with missiles and drones aimed at Saudi airports and energy sites. The Shabwa strike is among the deadliest of the escalation, and raises the risk to Red Sea shipping alongside Hormuz.

Burnham Calls Youth Unemployment a ‘Moral Failure’

Andy Burnham has warned that almost a million young people not in education, employment or training represent an “economic catastrophe and moral failure”. Writing ahead of a youth jobs announcement expected this week, the prime minister said: “Benefits will become the backstop, not the starting point.” The Conservatives have labelled the strategy “a disaster”.

Dive deeper
ONS figures show around 39% of the near-million so-called Neets are unemployed and 61% economically inactive. The number of 18 to 24-year-olds claiming unemployment-related benefits stands at 331,000, almost double the 2016 level, though below the peaks of around 500,000 after the 2008 financial crisis and the pandemic. The government will spend £353bn on welfare next year, just under half on the state pension. Burnham’s framing — investing in success rather than “paying for failure” — sets up a welfare argument that will run into the Budget.

Downing Street Rebukes Hegseth Over Afghanistan Remarks

Number 10 has hit back at Pete Hegseth after the US defence secretary suggested “maybe five” countries contributed to the Afghanistan war, with “one or two” actually fighting. The prime minister’s spokesperson said “the facts speak for themselves”, that more than 450 British personnel never returned, and that “it is wrong to diminish their role”.

Dive deeper
More than 1,100 non-US coalition troops were killed in Afghanistan, and Britain’s campaign in Helmand was among the costliest it has fought since Korea. The spokesperson stressed that the sacrifice was made “in response to an attack on our ally” — a pointed reminder that Nato invoked Article 5 on America’s behalf after 11 September. The remarks are part of a renewed Washington attack on Nato allies, and land awkwardly for a government trying to keep the alliance and the US relationship steady during the Iran war.

Bryant Insists Troubles Bill Is ‘Not an Amnesty’

Sir Chris Bryant, the Northern Ireland secretary, has rejected claims that government changes to the Troubles legacy bill amount to an amnesty for former soldiers, as the legislation returns to the Commons. He called it “completely wrong” to suggest that requiring attorney general consent for serious prosecutions was designed to pardon veterans.

Dive deeper
Bryant described the consent requirement as a “sensible measure” to bring “more consistency” to prosecution decisions. Ministers say the amendments would “significantly reduce” the prospect of veterans facing “rinse and repeat” investigations. Sinn Féin has branded them a “veterans’ charter”, while the DUP complains MPs have too little time to scrutinise them. The bill rests on a joint framework agreed with Dublin; the government must hold that consensus while satisfying MPs who argue veterans’ protections still fall short.

Bank of England Reviews Barclays’ Private Credit Exposure

The Prudential Regulation Authority is reported to be reviewing how Barclays logged total return swaps used to finance loans to private credit firms, after concerns were raised by an insider. The derivatives are said to have been booked in a portfolio implying lower risk. Barclays said its treatment “is consistent with accounting and regulatory capital requirements”.

Dive deeper
If the swaps were misclassified, the bank may not have held enough capital against potential losses, and its profitability could have been flattered. Barclays is said to have built billions of dollars of exposure to private credit through such derivatives; it says the conditions for its booking were “supported by appropriate governance internally and by external advisers”. The review lands as the private credit sector is under strain, and as London shares fell more than 1% on Monday. For regulators, the episode is a test of how far risk has migrated from bank balance sheets into lightly supervised lending.

Reform MSP Quits, Leaving Party Third at Holyrood

Amanda Bland, MSP for Central Scotland and Lothians West, has left Reform UK Scotland to sit as an independent, relegating the party to the third-largest group at Holyrood, one seat behind Labour. She said her “principles and priorities remain unchanged” and gave no further reason.

Dive deeper
A Reform UK source told BBC Scotland that Bland was “very difficult to work with” and had been summoned to a meeting on Wednesday about high staff turnover in her office; she said her resignation was not linked to it. Bland, a member of the criminal justice committee, said she could best hold the Scottish government to account as an independent. Losing second place costs Reform standing in Holyrood’s set-piece exchanges, and comes as the party faces scrutiny elsewhere, including a BBC report that one of its mayors is being investigated over photographs.

Morning Briefing

Monday 19 October 2026 — 07:30 BST

What It Means For You

  • If you are selling a home or renewing a mortgage: asking prices were cut on more than 3,000 properties a day in September, the most in any month for fifteen years, by an average of nearly 7% or about £21,000. Average two-year fixed rates are now 5.55%, against 4.25% before February, adding roughly £250 a month to a new loan.
  • If you travel by bus in England: fixed penalties of up to £100 are being introduced for playing loud music, littering, vaping or threatening behaviour. Drivers will not enforce them, and each council’s rules still need the transport secretary’s sign-off, so no fine has a start date yet.
  • If you have children: the Children’s Commissioner reports waits for a mental health referral ranging from six days in Leicester to 118 in Hampshire and the Isle of Wight, and twenty-five areas where fewer than half of children saw a dentist last year.

GEO Geopolitical

Ukraine Launches Its Largest Drone Attack of the War

Russia’s defence ministry says it neutralised 1,015 Ukrainian drones overnight across fifteen regions, Crimea and the Black Sea — the largest aerial attack of the war. Fires burned at the St Petersburg oil terminal and the Ruchi depot, one person was killed and five injured there, and about 20,000 homes in the Leningrad region lost power.

Dive deeper
The thousand-drone figure is the Russian defence ministry’s own and describes what it says it shot down rather than what Ukraine says it launched; Kyiv has not given a number. What is attributed locally is more specific. Alexander Beglov, the St Petersburg governor, said firefighters were tackling blazes at the two oil sites and at industrial warehouses in the Kolpinsky district, and that the city’s main airport was restricting flights “for safety reasons”. Alexander Drozdenko, governor of the surrounding region, put the drones downed there at 145. The retaliation came within hours: Vitali Klitschko, the mayor of Kyiv, said a Russian strike killed a woman overnight, hit a subway station and damaged a twenty-five-storey residential block and a medical facility, injuring three more. Two attacks on Zaporizhzhia killed a woman and wounded a six-year-old girl. Volodymyr Zelensky says Russia has used almost 2,700 attack drones, 1,700 aerial bombs and 66 missiles in the past week and has struck Ukraine’s energy, oil and gas sector seventy-six times since the energy ceasefire was declared — Ukraine’s own count, and not independently confirmed.

Trump Blocks the Russia Sanctions Two Days Before They Bit

The president signed an order on 16 October waiving sanctions that Congress passed and he had signed into law in September. “I am in the process of negotiating Peace,” he wrote, saying he did not want to “drive, foolishly, Russia away from the bargaining table”. His secretary of state calls it a sixty-day pause while the economic impact is assessed.

Dive deeper
The administration is now pressing the argument back onto Kyiv. Scott Bessent, the treasury secretary, told American television that Volodymyr Zelensky should “be very careful about making statements like this that aren’t true” and that it was “very reckless” of him to allege the satellite slowdown had been ordered in Washington — a charge the Ukrainian president repeated at the weekend and which no American or corporate source has confirmed. Mr Zelensky’s own framing has hardened: he told one broadcaster that American support for Vladimir Putin would be a “historic mistake” and another that “steps have been taken to convince Ukraine to capitulate”. A Russian envoy called the waiver “an important and truly historic moment”, which is Moscow’s characterisation and not a fact. The sharpest assessment is from the Institute for the Study of War, which judges that the order “does not support Trump’s stated foreign policy objectives of ending the war in Ukraine and will only enable Russia to continue financing and protracting the war”.

French Cabinet Meets in Crisis as Parliament Demands an Inquiry

The prime minister holds an emergency cabinet meeting today after a weekend the BBC describes as some of the worst public disorder in modern French history. The president of the National Assembly wants a parliamentary inquiry into what happened and why. The interior minister has not resigned, despite calls from left and right.

Dive deeper
The interior ministry’s count stands at about 2,400 arrested and close to a thousand police, gendarmes and firefighters injured, seven of them seriously; an earlier count of 1,800 and more than 500 was superseded during Sunday. An estimated 1,500 shops and businesses were looted or destroyed, though the interior minister puts the number with considerable damage at several hundred, and the mayor of Bordeaux says up to a hundred were looted in that city alone. One protester died of cardiac arrest during the Paris demonstrations, police said, without giving further detail. Emmanuel Macron’s address has been widely criticised for lacking concrete detail, and the curfew and state of emergency that had been rumoured did not materialise. Sophie Binet, who leads the CGT union, accused him of failing to address the cost of living, declining public services or the students’ original complaint. The thing to watch is not this week but the next: fresh mass demonstrations have already been called for a fortnight’s time.

China Grows 4.5% as Factories Offset a Deepening Property Slump

The economy expanded 4.5% in the third quarter against the same period last year, up from a three-year low of 4.3% and ahead of forecasts. Growth over nine months is 4.6%, inside Beijing’s target range. Retail sales rose 1.1% and industrial output 5.5% in September, while property development investment fell 20.6% over the year to date.

Dive deeper
The shape matters more than the headline: this is an economy being carried by its factories and foreign buyers while its households and private investors sit on their hands. The monthly trade surplus reached a record $133.5bn, fixed-asset investment fell 7.2% over nine months, and the property slump deepened rather than bottomed. Sheana Yue of Oxford Economics is blunt that “the prolonged housing slump will continue to limit the spillover from stronger industrial activity to the wider economy” and sees no sign of the sector “bottoming out”. The number that explains the squeeze is in the price data: producer prices rose 5% in September, the fastest in more than four years, while consumer inflation stays below 1%. As Ms Yue puts it, that “potentially leaves firms to absorb more of the cost shock through lower margins rather than higher selling prices”. Eswar Prasad of Cornell expects no rescue: “the government might become more sanguine and maintain a passive approach, rather than infusing stimulus.” The Economist Intelligence Unit forecasts 4.7% for the full year.

World Food Programme Cuts Gaza Rations by About a Third

The United Nations agency says it must “drastically reduce lifesaving assistance” in Gaza for lack of money, cutting the number of people it helps from 1.6 million to 1.1 million. Hot meals from community kitchens fall from 250,000 a day to 150,000, and monthly flour and ration boxes from 1.1 million recipients to 600,000.

Dive deeper
The agency is unusually direct that this is not about access or need. Shaun Hughes, its Palestine director, says the cuts are being made because of finances, “not because the needs are coming down, or because we don’t have access — we do”. It has received less than half as much funding for its Palestinian operations this year as in previous years. The context is that nearly all of Gaza’s 2.1 million people remain displaced and two-thirds rely on food aid to survive; a year after the ceasefire there has been no reconstruction, most people are still in tents, and Mr Hughes describes “poor access to water and sanitation and healthcare”. One constraint compounds the cut: there is no cooking gas in Gaza because Israel treats it as a dual-use item that Hamas could put to military purposes, so prepared meals matter more than raw rations. Families in the hardest-to-reach areas move from a daily meal to one every other day. This rests on a single approved outlet, which interviewed two displaced mothers alongside the agency.

Private Firms Treating Housed Residents as Cash Cows, Investigation Finds

A broadcast investigation into supported housing for ex-prisoners, people recovering from addiction and women fleeing abuse finds providers drawing large sums in housing benefit for minimal care. One provider received £443 a week — £23,000 a year — for a single resident’s room and support. The department paid £410m for such accommodation in Birmingham alone last year.

Dive deeper
The loophole is in the law: a registered provider need only offer care that is “more than minimal”, and in return the accommodation is exempt from the usual housing benefit caps. The scale in one city is striking — more than 34,000 people are in this kind of housing in Birmingham, against about 22,000 in London, a city more than seven times the size. The money trail is documented rather than alleged. A sister company of one provider bought a house used for this purpose for £191,000 in 2024 and sold it a year later for £410,000, according to Land Registry records; its owner says he spent about £150,000 on renovation and associated costs, and has paid himself £2.9m in dividends over three financial years plus more than £900,000 in other earnings across his companies. One large provider wrote off £1.8m over payments to former directors and companies they ran. The resident at the centre of the case says the advertised support did not exist: “On the adverts, they will often say that you have weekly support sessions, help with GPs. It’s not the case at all.” The leader of Birmingham City Council calls it “a scandal”.

Burnham Calls a Million Young People Out of Work a Moral Failure

The prime minister, writing in a newspaper, says that almost a million 16 to 24-year-olds not in education, employment or training is “an economic catastrophe and a moral failure. And it must change.” He promises that “benefits will become the backstop, not the starting point”, with the system beginning from a young person’s ambitions rather than their entitlement.

Dive deeper
This is the government adopting on Monday the argument Alan Milburn made on Sunday, when the author of its own review urged ministers to move money out of welfare and into employment support. Mr Burnham’s description of the present system is unusually blunt for a prime minister describing his own state: it “provides an assessment, a payment and, after that, goodbye. Too often there is no support, no offer of skills and no opportunity to get a job.” He blames two years of lockdown, social media that erodes confidence and belonging, an education system built around the university route, and thin job markets. The guarantee he offers is conditional in both directions: “the safety net will always be there for those who cannot work. But if someone can work with the right help, the government has a duty to do everything in its power to make that happen.” Ministers are expected this week to announce tens of thousands of apprenticeships and placements in construction and in professional firms. The Milburn review itself publishes next month, and the Budget is on 28 October.

Asking Prices Cut on 3,000 Homes a Day, a Fifteen-Year High

More than 3,000 properties a day had their asking price reduced in September, the most in any month for fifteen years, by an average of nearly 7% — about £21,000 off a typically valued home. A third of all listed homes were marked down. Reduced homes took 119 days to find a buyer against 39 for those listed once at the right price.

Dive deeper
Three different measures are circulating this morning and they are not in conflict, so it is worth separating them. Asking prices for newly listed homes rose 1% this month to £371,015; house prices over the past year are down 0.1%; and a third of homes already on the market are being cut, heavily. The first is what sellers hope for, the second is roughly where the market is, and the third is the gap between them closing. The reason is affordability: average two-year fixed mortgage rates have climbed to 5.55% from 5.29% last month and 4.25% before February, which adds around £250 a month to a new loan. Colleen Babcock of Rightmove points to the penalty for getting it wrong first time: “Once a home has been on the market for a while and then has a price reduction, some buyers start asking questions, even when there is nothing wrong with the property. In a market with so much choice, first impressions matter.” The figures are the company’s own rather than official statistics, though a rival firm reports homes for sale up 5% on the year and sales agreed down 9%.

Profit Warnings Hit a Two-Year High Nine Days From the Budget

Listed companies issued 65 profit warnings in the three months to September, the most in two years, with 38% citing the war in the Middle East and rising costs the leading factor in 35%. Housebuilders have now issued fourteen this year, more than in 2008. Nearly a fifth of all listed firms have warned at least once in twelve months.

Dive deeper
The 2008 comparison is the one that should stop a reader: ten warnings from listed housebuilders at the peak of the financial crisis, fourteen already this year. Household goods and home construction was the worst-affected sector of the quarter, which sits uncomfortably beside the asking-price cuts elsewhere in this column — the same squeeze seen from the builder’s side rather than the seller’s. Jo Robinson of EY-Parthenon, which compiles the figures, identifies something that has changed in kind rather than degree: “with the autumn budget around the corner, profit warnings linked to policy change and uncertainty — once concentrated around fiscal events or elections — have become a regular feature of our data. Businesses have shown considerable resilience, but the pace of policy change is making forecasting, investment and capital allocation much harder.” The chancellor has promised the Budget will give households and businesses “breathing space” while conceding the conflict has pushed borrowing costs to their highest in decades. He is reported to be preparing to raise the small business rates threshold from £12,000 to £18,000.

NHS Managers Say up to Twenty Maternity Units May Have to Close

A body representing health managers says the way maternity care is organised in England is not fit for purpose, and that up to twenty of the 155 consultant-led units will have to shut or change role. Units delivering 1,500 to 2,000 babies a year are most at risk, concentrated in rural and coastal areas. Two royal colleges have objected within hours.

Dive deeper
The case for consolidation rests on three things happening at once: a shortage of obstetricians, a sharp rise in planned and emergency caesareans requiring more staff and more theatre time, and buildings described as outdated and decaying. A director at the NHS Alliance, Matthew Hopkins, argues the change is “inevitable” and “the right thing… the safe thing for patients”, and that politicians “must have the courage to make the case for patient safety” and grasp the nettle. The objections came immediately and from the professions themselves. The Royal College of Midwives’ chief policy officer said women and families “rely on local maternity units to be there when they need them”; the president of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists said there are “other solutions to our workforce challenges that are far safer than closing maternity units”. The argument is not abstract. The unit at Barnstaple has been suspended since August over staffing, leaving a fifty-three-mile journey to Exeter, and the trust decides this week whether it can safely reopen. A government maternity taskforce reports before Christmas, and the managers fear it will be tokenistic.

Four Soldiers Named Among the Dead at the Alberta Base Fire

Four Canadian service members were killed and thirty-nine are in hospital, nineteen of them critically, after a grass fire ignited on a training range and strong winds drove it towards soldiers on exercise. The dead were named on Sunday evening as Sergeant Morgan Wong, Private Shaun Farrell, Private Aidan Goulding and Private Hayden Lykkemark.

Dive deeper
This morning’s edition carried no figure and no cause, because the only ones available came from an outlet we do not use citing unnamed military sources. Both have now been settled on the record, and one of them was wrong. Major General Wade Rutland, commander of the 1st Canadian Division, said a grass fire started on a range and “uncommonly strong winds caused it to spread rapidly and shift direction”. The account that a gas main had been struck during live firing has been contradicted by the military itself, which says investigators do not believe any explosions were caused by oil or gas infrastructure. The toll is four, not the higher number that circulated. Mark Carney, addressing the country from Ottawa, called it “one of the worst tragedies for the forces on Canadian soil in peacetime” and ordered flags on all federal buildings to half-mast. Alberta’s premier Danielle Smith gave the hospital figures; a hospital in Edmonton declared a code orange on Saturday evening and stood it down on Sunday. There is no indication of terrorism. Alberta votes today on beginning the legal process towards a referendum on leaving Canada, and no approved source connects the two events.

French Arrests Double to 2,400 as Macron Speaks of Shame

About 2,400 people have been arrested and around a thousand police and gendarmes injured, the interior ministry says — double and sevenfold the figures available this morning — after 550,000 demonstrated against fuel prices, the cost of living and declining public services. In a national address the president said protesters had “sought to bring down the republic”, and: “We all feel anger and shame.”

Dive deeper
Emmanuel Macron criticised “unbridled violence” and blamed “factious groups from the ultra-left, joined by numerous delinquents” — a diagnosis his government and Marine Le Pen now share, which is itself notable. Prefects and mayors are to draft measures protecting shops and civic buildings, and prefects may be allowed to ban street gatherings. The political cost is landing on one man: politicians on both left and right are calling for the interior minister Laurent Nuñez to resign, after a government that appeared caught out by the scale of Saturday’s violence. The prime minister Sébastien Lecornu said the government would not be “intimidated”. The physical damage is concentrated in Limoges, where the nineteenth-century city hall has been gutted and its clock tower and roof have collapsed. Disorder continued on Sunday at the Place de la République in Paris and on a smaller scale in Lyon, with teargas, stun grenades, e-bikes piled and set alight and graffiti reading “introduction to civil war”. Protests also took place on Saturday in Spain, Italy and Portugal. The number of officers hurt is the figure that has moved most: about a thousand police and gendarmes, on the ministry’s count, against the 150 reported this morning.

St Petersburg Oil Terminal Struck as Kyiv Says Intelligence Is Back

Drones struck the St Petersburg Oil Terminal and the Ruchyi oil depot overnight, killing at least one person and injuring at least five, the city’s governor said. Separately the dispute reported this morning half resolved: Volodymyr Zelensky said American intelligence sharing is working again — “it was blocked for some period of time. And then it was deblocked by the American side… Now it works.”

Dive deeper
Kyiv has not commented on the strikes, so there is no Ukrainian confirmation of them, and Russia’s defence ministry claim to have downed more than a thousand Ukrainian drones overnight is its own unverified assertion. What is attributed on the record comes from Governor Alexander Beglov: fires at both oil sites, twelve apartment buildings and two houses damaged, and Pulkovo Airport temporarily closed. A logistics centre and a data centre belonging to two Russian internet companies were also hit; both confirmed damage and said services continued. On the diplomatic strand, Mr Zelensky still maintains the satellite slowdown was deliberate — “it was a message from the American government to limit the speed. And technically, SpaceX did it” — which remains his own claim with no American or corporate confirmation. Donald Trump wrote that “I am in the process of negotiating Peace” and would not “drive, foolishly, Russia away from the bargaining table”, while claiming Mr Zelensky “doesn’t want to end this war, and probably never did”. Mr Zelensky says Russia has attacked Ukraine’s energy, oil and gas sectors seventy-six times since the ceasefire was declared — Ukraine’s own count. There is still no sign of a summit.

Gulf States Invoke Joint Defence as Iran Holds Its Hormuz Terms

Gulf Cooperation Council foreign ministers, meeting in Manama, condemned Houthi attacks and invoked their joint defence agreement, under which an attack on one member concerns all six. Iran’s foreign minister said Tehran’s conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz remain in place. No new attack on shipping was reported on Sunday.

Dive deeper
The weekly record of ten vessels attacked, and twenty-three this month, stands where it did this morning; what moved today was the Saudi-Yemen axis rather than the strait. The council also pressed for tighter enforcement of the United Nations arms embargo and urged states to designate the Houthis a terrorist organisation. The coalition says it brought down two drones early on Sunday, near Riyadh and in the Eastern Province, after an earlier interception near the capital. The effect on ordinary life in the Saudi capital is now visible: the Kingdom Centre, one of its landmark towers, has ordered mandatory remote working for office tenants indefinitely. Six people were injured in Najran by a Houthi projectile, with three homes and three vehicles damaged, according to the Saudi civil defence agency.
