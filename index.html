Russia Fires 21 Missiles at Kyiv Hours After Agreeing Talks

Three waves of ballistic missiles struck Kyiv from Wednesday evening, 21 in all, hours after the Kremlin said President Putin had agreed in principle to resume three-way talks. President Zelensky said at least two people were killed and 34 injured, five of them children. Twelve regions were attacked overnight.

Dive deeper
What Russia agreed to is narrower than it sounds. A Kremlin foreign-policy aide said after Wednesday’s St Petersburg meeting that talks could resume only after 10 November — a week after the American midterms — possibly in Abu Dhabi, and at technical level only. The same aide dismissed the proposals the American envoy had brought from Oslo: “there wasn’t much there that was useful”. Mr Putin has not agreed to meet Mr Zelensky at all. Then came the missiles. Residential blocks, energy sites, a railway station, the metro, shopping centres and a monastery were hit; metro infrastructure was damaged near the Pivdenny bridge over the Dnipro. In Kharkiv region a woman was killed and four injured. The toll is still rising: Ukraine’s emergency service had earlier put it at one dead and 22 injured. Mr Zelensky’s own framing was mordant — the missiles came from Russian “humanitarians” — and his foreign minister was blunter: “Appeasement, servility, and flattery will not stop this war. Only strength will.” Moscow’s mayor separately claimed Ukraine sent 630 drones at the Moscow region, a Russian figure that nobody independent has confirmed.

AI-Found PHP Flaw Forces Governments to Pull Websites Offline

Two critical flaws in the PHP programming language, uncovered with the help of artificial intelligence, allow an attacker to run commands on a web server without any password. America’s National Security Agency told organisations to patch at once or take systems offline. Britain’s budget watchdog took its own website down.

Dive deeper
The flaws, together dubbed PHP2Shell, had sat in the code for more than a decade and were found by two security firms using large language models. The response has been precautionary and very wide: the National Cyber Security Centre told British organisations to consider taking systems offline and to check for “backdoors installed by an attacker”, and the Office for Budget Responsibility pulled its website down — a week before it publishes its forecast alongside the Budget. NHS Scotland went dark; at one point seven of Scotland’s 32 councils and six of its 14 health boards were offline, and six councils still report problems with payments. Edinburgh, Bristol and Oxford councils, the Paris police prefecture and the Dutch national digital identity service all went down. Amazon found an exploit attempt against a decoy server on 17 October, four days before public disclosure. But the distinction that matters has been drawn by the people best placed to overstate it. The researcher who found the flaw called it the most serious he had ever seen, then added: “it doesn’t look like exploitation has taken off or been widespread.” Attempts are not breaches.

Two Killed at Dammam Airport as US Orders Diplomats Out

Saudi Arabia’s aviation regulator says two people were killed and eight injured in an attack on King Fahd International Airport on Wednesday evening. Washington has ordered non-emergency staff and their families to leave Saudi Arabia. Brent crude rose about 3% to around $112 a barrel.

Dive deeper
An ordered departure is mandatory rather than advisory, and its scope is narrower than first reported: a State Department spokesman said it covers Saudi Arabia only, and called reports of similar withdrawals from the United Arab Emirates, Jordan, Qatar and Bahrain “inaccurate”. His stated reason was that Houthi attacks on civilian airports and infrastructure “have the potential to escalate rapidly”. The dead at Dammam were a Saudi citizen and an Indian resident; the toll rose from one overnight. Notably, the regulator did not say what kind of attack it was and the Houthis have not claimed it. Saudi authorities put the death toll from attacks on the kingdom’s airports since the start of October at 21, and said four more drones were intercepted on Wednesday night. A drone at the Shaybah oilfield killed two contractors, from the Philippines and Nepal. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards claimed their navy struck four vessels over alleged illegal oil transfers and that three more hit mines west of Hormuz; no vessels were named and nobody independent has verified it. They also warned that if the trend continues “the conflict will extend to” American bases in the region.

France Now Borrows More Dearly Than America as Protests Loom

French ten-year borrowing costs rose about nine basis points to just over 5.08%, moving above the equivalent American yield for the first time since 2013. The premium over German debt is the widest in almost fifteen years. Protests are banned across central Paris today and in Lyon on Saturday.

Dive deeper
France crossed 5% on Wednesday for the first time since 2002, and the move is now spilling outwards: Italy’s spread over German debt widened by almost four basis points and Spain’s is the widest since June last year. The cause is a budget nobody can pass sitting on top of a weekend of rioting, with the deficit at 5.1% of national income and debt at 115.6%. Carsten Brzeski of ING drew the uncomfortable conclusion for Frankfurt: the European Central Bank will “very likely stay on hold next week”, and “without any solution, bond markets have taken over the ECB’s role, making rate hikes less likely”. The policing is on the same scale as the borrowing. Today’s ban covers the Elysée, both chambers of parliament, the Louvre, the Champs-Élysées, Bastille and République from eight in the morning until eight at night; a junior interior minister said the weekend police presence would be 20% larger than last. In Lyon, where the mayor says between 150 and 200 shops were looted, all protests are banned on Saturday. Thailand, Malaysia and China have issued travel advisories.

Judge Fines Meta $25bn Over Eleven Years of Privacy Lies

A judge in New Mexico has ordered Meta to pay $25bn, the largest fine in the company’s history, finding it wilfully misled users about who controlled their data between 2010 and 2021. The state’s attorney general called it a warning to Silicon Valley. Meta says the ruling makes it all but impossible to do business there.

Dive deeper
The judgment followed a two-week trial in Santa Fe, and the finding is about honesty rather than any single breach: “Facebook promised users that they controlled their data. This was false,” the judge wrote, adding that the deception went to “the heart of Facebook’s core business practices”. The scale is worth putting in proportion. The sum is roughly a quarter’s profits for a company that reported revenue up 26% to $64.5bn last quarter, with profits tripling to $9.8bn; one account has the full calculated penalty at $219bn before the judge reduced it. The number of violations is given as more than 40 million by one outlet and more than 43 million by two others. Forty-eight other states settled similar claims; New Mexico, with a population of about two million, went to trial alone and will put the money into its education fund. Meta’s chief legal officer said the case amounted to “a tax on speech to enforce new rules for what people can say online”. The attorney general’s reply was that a small state’s judgment “should be a clear warning to everyone in big tech”.

Barclays to Cut 5,000 Jobs as Profits Rise Before the Budget

Barclays will shed about 5,000 roles, some 5% of its global workforce, after pre-tax profits rose 12% to £2.3bn in the third quarter. Its chief executive said an expected Budget tax rise on banks would be “bearable” but questioned the signal it sends. Roughly a thousand of the cuts have already been made.

Dive deeper
Two things are being conflated in the coverage and both are worth straightening. The first is geography: the 5,000 is a global figure against a workforce of about 93,000, the cost-cutting is focused on the British retail bank, but a significant proportion of the actual job losses is expected to fall in India, where back-office functions are performed. These are not 5,000 British jobs. The second is artificial intelligence. The finance director tied about 60% of the reductions to support functions, “including fewer developers, as AI makes our people more productive”. But asked how many cuts were attributable to AI, the chief executive declined to give a number and put the bulk of the gains down to years of technology investment: “it is the icing on the cake — it’s not the cake”. On the Budget, six days away, his position was unusually candid: the bank makes about half its money outside Britain, so “this is not a question of a financial hit to us. It’s bearable. It is a question of proportion and it’s a question of market signaling.” Profits matched forecasts; group income of £7.4bn fell short of the £7.61bn expected.

Fraud and Computer Misuse Rise as Knife and Gun Crime Fall

Fraud rose 11% to 4.5 million incidents in the year to June and computer misuse 23% to 817,000, according to official figures published this morning. Over the same period knife offences fell 6% to 48,615, firearms offences 8% and homicides 1%. Overall crime showed no significant change.

Dive deeper
The statisticians’ own summary is that crime against individuals and households has generally fallen over a decade “with some notable exceptions, such as fraud” — and the exceptions are where the growth is. Bank and credit account fraud alone rose 13% to 2.8 million incidents. Computer misuse is up sharply year on year yet remains 54% below its 2017 level, so the rise is a rebound rather than a record. Two figures in the release need handling with care. Recorded robbery appears to have risen 10%, but that is mainly an artefact of better recording of robbery from businesses; robbery of personal property actually fell 7%. And a reported jump in domestic abuse, to 9.6% of adults from 7.8%, comes with an explicit warning from the statisticians that changes to their own questionnaire have “likely affected the domestic abuse estimates and scale of the increase” and that it is too early to say whether it represents a new trend. Shoplifting, which has driven much retail commentary, was down 2% at 519,516 offences.

Judges Get Power to Confine Killers Who Skip Sentencing

Under rules laid before Parliament today, Crown Court judges in England and Wales will be able to order offenders who refuse to attend their sentencing to be confined to a cell for 21 days, stripped of privileges for 84 and barred from social visits for 27. Visits from their children are exempt.

Dive deeper
The reasoning is that existing sanctions do not bite. Refusing to attend is already contempt of court, carrying a fine or up to two years’ additional imprisonment, but for a murderer serving decades the department concedes that extra time “can offer little deterrent”. Social visits can also be restricted to one every four weeks for 84 days, and the powers extend to offenders removed from court for disruption, not only those who refuse to appear. For 18- to 20-year-olds the maximum periods are shorter. Catherine McKinnell, the prisons minister, said criminals “should not be able to pick and choose when they face justice”. Claire Waxman, the Victims’ Commissioner for England and Wales, said refusal “compounds trauma and robs families of one of the very few moments of accountability”, adding that it is “now essential that these powers are used consistently”. The aunt of Zara Aleena, murdered in 2022, said removing privileges “would rebalance the power in the justice system from offender to victim”. The Prison Reform Trust asked for evidence that sanctions actually increase attendance. The rules take effect next year.

Rosebank Decision Pending as Department Dismisses Reports as Speculation

Reports that the energy secretary is minded to refuse the Rosebank oil field have been met with an on-the-record denial: her department says “this is speculation” and that each project will be assessed on its merits. A decision is expected before the end of the year. The reports rest on unnamed sources.

Dive deeper
What Miatta Fahnbulleh has actually said on the record is narrower than the reporting but points in the same direction. Drawing the distinction with Jackdaw, approved on Wednesday, she said: “One is a gasfield that caters for the domestic market, while the other is an oilfield that is mainly for export. It is right that we judge each application in its own right, and it is absolutely critical that the decision is consistent with our climate obligations, our carbon budgets and the end destination we need to get to.” There is nothing on the government’s own publication service beyond the live case file, which does record one fact nobody has written up: the developer changed from Equinor UK to Adura Operations in February. Both fields are applications under existing licences, so neither touches the manifesto commitment to issue no new ones. The chair of the all-party group on climate change, the Labour MP Afzal Khan, said approving Rosebank on top of Jackdaw “cannot be justified”. Jackdaw alone is estimated to add 36 million tonnes of carbon emissions; the reported estimate that Rosebank would emit roughly seven times as much is a newspaper calculation, not an official one.

Six Million Households to Get £150 Energy Rebate Letters

Letters confirming £150 off energy bills this winter begin arriving from today, going to around six million households across Britain. Every billpayer on a means-tested benefit now qualifies, after an expansion that added nearly 2.3 million families last year. Separately, the prime minister has ordered a review of gas standing charges.

Dive deeper
The letters will go out between now and January, and anyone who needs to telephone their supplier about eligibility must do so before late February. The government says the scheme is funded through to 2030-31, and the minister for energy consumers, Polly Billington, urged people who think they qualify to “keep an eye out for a letter” — while warning recipients to contact suppliers only by their usual route and not to follow suspicious links, which is a sensible caution given what fraud figures published the same morning show. The standing-charges question is at an earlier stage than the headlines suggest. At Prime Minister’s Questions on Wednesday, Andy Burnham said he had asked the chancellor and the energy secretary “to look at whether we can remove standing charges from gas bills and to report back to this house at the earliest opportunity”. No mechanism and no timetable have been given, the review covers gas only, and the regulator has consulted for nearly two years without reaching firm conclusions. The averages are about 55p a day for electricity and 30p for gas. Energy prices are forecast to rise 16% in January to a four-year high.

Putin Frees American as Witkoff Opens Talks in St Petersburg

President Putin announced the release of Stephen Hubbard, a 74-year-old American held since shortly after Russia invaded Ukraine, during talks with the United States envoy Steve Witkoff at a palace outside St Petersburg. Witkoff said there is “a foundation in place to move toward a ceasefire”. No ceasefire and no territorial agreement emerged.

Dive deeper
Mr Hubbard is a retired schoolteacher from Michigan who had served in the National Guard and the Air Force. He was captured in Ukraine in 2022 and convicted at a closed trial in 2024 of fighting for Ukraine as a mercenary, for which he was sentenced to nearly seven years in a penal colony; he was the last American in Russia formally designated wrongfully detained, is in poor health, and is expected to be flown to a military hospital in Texas. Witkoff, filmed in the room, told Mr Putin: “Because of you, he’s going home to his family.” President Trump said he had pressed the Russian president repeatedly over many months. What Washington gave in return, if anything, has not been disclosed, and no quid pro quo should be inferred. Witkoff separately thanked Mr Putin for lifting export controls on Russian diesel, and the Kremlin’s own account has the first cargo leaving within two to three days. The other thread closed today too: President Zelensky confirmed American warning intelligence is reaching Ukraine again, saying simply, “Now it works. It’s great.”

RAF Typhoons to Defend Saudi Skies as the Gulf War Widens

Britain will fly Typhoon fighters on defensive missions over Saudi cities and infrastructure, and has extended an existing refuelling mission for Saudi jets by 90 days. Egypt’s president is seeking parliamentary authority for a defensive mission of his own, with the vote now delayed to Monday. Brent crude rose above $110.

Dive deeper
Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, was explicit that the aircraft “will operate in a defensive role and will not conduct offensive strikes against the Houthis”. The provocation is not in doubt: a missile on Riyadh’s international airport this month killed 13 and injured 308, the deadliest attack in the kingdom in over a decade, and on Monday three ballistic missiles killed 73 police and security recruits, all aged between 18 and 20, at a camp in Shabwa. Britain has sold Saudi Arabia 72 of these aircraft in a deal originally worth £4.4bn and is negotiating 48 more, so it is now defending its own customer. HA Hellyer of the Royal United Services Institute put the obvious question: “What does defensive mean? The UK might say it was defensive but the Houthis would see it as offensive.” On Egypt, one regional official says the contribution would be air defence and aircraft rather than ground troops. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards meanwhile claimed mines had halted three tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, naming no vessel or operator, two months after President Trump declared the strait “totally cleared” of mines.

France’s Borrowing Cost Tops 5% as Europe’s Riots Spread to Italy

French ten-year borrowing costs briefly rose above 5% this morning for the first time since July 2002, before easing back just below. The premium over Germany is near 160 basis points, and France now pays more to borrow than Italy. Italy has sent 500 soldiers to Milan after two nights of disorder.

Dive deeper
France’s debt is around €3.6 trillion, or 119% of national income, up from 98% in 2019 and the highest since shortly after the Second World War. The prime minister is seeking €54bn of savings, and his finance minister, Roland Lescure, was asked how he would force a budget through a hostile parliament: “I’m convinced of this because we have no choice. We have the tools to get it passed.” Nearly 2,400 people were arrested in France at the weekend. In Milan, where 271 young people were detained, Giorgia Meloni said “destroying and stealing is not protest, it is crime, and the state’s response must be firm”; her government plans penalties of up to five years and €20,000, and powers to detain 14- to 17-year-olds on evidence of danger before any crime is committed. President Macron, in Doha, attacked commentary he called “unacceptable and stupid from many quarters, including from foreign leaders” who were “campaigning at our expense”. The read-across for Britain is uncomfortable rather than identical: the ten-year gilt is steady today but sits at about 5.4%, close to its own highs, a week before the Budget.

Drone Strike on Abyei Market Kills 153, South Sudan Protests

The toll from a drone strike on a fuel depot at a market in the disputed Abyei region has been revised up from 73 to at least 153, and officials say it is still rising. South Sudan has summoned Sudan’s ambassador in protest. A military source said the Sudanese army carried out the attack.

Dive deeper
The strike hit Amiet Market, about ten kilometres from Abyei town, at around half past eleven on Monday morning, and the fire was still burning the following day. Yohanna Akol Ngor, the Abyei administration’s caretaker information minister, called it “a horrible and shocking situation” and said some bodies had been burned to ashes. Christian Lindauer, who manages an operation for Doctors Without Borders at a nearby hospital, reported more than 20 patients including several critical cases and burns. Satellite imagery has independently confirmed extensive burnt buildings at the market. Against that, a senior Sudanese officer said anonymously that those killed “were RSF members and fuel smugglers” and that the military does not target civilians; the Rapid Support Forces have not commented, and both are interested parties. The United Nations condemned the drone strikes without attributing blame. Abyei is oil-rich, claimed by both Sudans, and has hosted more than 2,800 United Nations peacekeepers since 2011 — one trader noted it is “a weapons-free zone under United Nations protection, yet it was targeted by drones”. Twenty million Sudanese now face acute hunger.

Nigeria Confirms First Ebola Case as Outbreak Reaches West Africa

A woman who arrived from Uganda has tested positive in Abuja, Nigeria’s first case of this outbreak, and 44 contacts are being traced. She developed symptoms on 7 October and was admitted to a private hospital four days later. The strain involved has no approved vaccine or treatment.

Dive deeper
That last point deserves emphasis because it is easily got wrong.
