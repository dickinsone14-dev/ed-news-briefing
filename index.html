Week of 20–26 July 2026

The Week In Numbers

  • Brent crude traced the widest weekly swing of the Gulf war. It opened the week around $88 after a seventh consecutive night of US strikes on Iran, climbed to a six-week high above $100 by Thursday as the Houthis choked the Red Sea and Saudi tankers turned back, then retreated to $95.90 by Saturday’s close once Washington paused its bombing and both sides signalled talks. WTI held in the mid-$90s. The VIX spiked to 19.36 on Thursday before easing to 17.79; the American death toll from the war reached sixteen by mid-week. US airstrikes ran from a seventh night on 18 July to a thirteenth by 24 July.
  • UK inflation slipped to 2.6 per cent in June, a fifteen-month low, as fuel and food costs eased — yet ten-year gilt yields touched a two-month high above 5.10 per cent, leaving Britain with the costliest borrowing in the G7. The FTSE 100 hit a five-month high of 10,717 on Wednesday before settling at 10,710, up on the week; sterling held around $1.334 and gold firmed to $4,078. Retail sales jumped in June on the warm weather and business activity reached a three-month high. The Bank of England is expected to hold Bank Rate at its meeting on Thursday.
  • Andy Burnham entered Downing Street on Monday as Britain’s seventh prime minister in a decade, and within his first week scrapped VAT on domestic electricity, capped bus fares at £2, axed Sir Keir Starmer’s £2bn digital ID scheme and cut business rates for pubs and music venues. Across the Channel, wildfires forced more than 267,000 people to flee France and Spain — up from 166,000 at midweek — while a Cairngorms blaze was declared a major incident and Britain braced for a fourth heatwave with 35C alerts. A record 165 migrants crossed the Channel in a single boat.

What Moved Forward

Burnham Enters Downing Street and Launches a Cost-of-Living Blitz

Domestic

Andy Burnham walked into Downing Street on Monday, vowing a “circuit-breaker for Britain” and reshaping the Cabinet at once — John Healey to the Treasury as Chancellor, Rachel Reeves and David Lammy out. Within days he had scrapped VAT on domestic electricity, capped bus fares at £2, axed Starmer’s £2bn digital ID scheme, cut business rates for pubs and music venues and stood up a “Number 10 North” in Manchester. The former mayor governs, unusually, without a Commons seat, shielded from Prime Minister’s Questions by the summer recess until September. A “Burnham bounce” narrowed Reform’s poll lead to near parity, though the new premier has ruled out a snap election.

US Halts Its Strikes on Iran as Both Sides Signal Talks

Geopolitical

After thirteen consecutive nights of American bombing — Tabriz, Natanz and the fortified “Pickaxe Mountain” enrichment site all struck — Washington paused its campaign by the weekend as mediated contacts resumed. Iran, which had spent the week firing back across the Gulf at bases in Kuwait, Bahrain, Jordan and Saudi Arabia, reported its first “peaceful night” and both sides confirmed indirect talks, with the Strait of Hormuz at the centre of the dispute. Trump ruled out a formal deal “for now” even as the guns fell quiet, and the Houthis’ claimed strikes on Saudi Aramco sites remained unverified. The first genuine de-escalation of the war, if it holds.

Oil Retreats Below $100 as the War-Risk Premium Unwinds

Markets

Brent crude, which had surged to a six-week high above $100 on Thursday as the Red Sea blockade bit and a Saudi tanker burned, unwound much of that spike to settle at $95.90 by Saturday once Washington halted its strikes. The FTSE 100 rebounded to 10,710 and gilts steadied as the oil panic faded; the VIX fell back from 19.36 to 17.79. Pump prices, which had jumped as crude topped $100, are expected to ease if the calm endures — a reprieve for Burnham’s cost-of-living plan, which had been squeezed by every dollar on the barrel. Markets closed the week pricing through the ceasefire signals rather than the strikes.

Inflation Falls to a Fifteen-Month Low of 2.6 Per Cent

Markets

Consumer price inflation slipped to 2.6 per cent in June, its lowest in fifteen months, as falling fuel and food costs offset stubborn services prices. Retail sales jumped over the month on the run of warm weather and a purchasing-managers’ survey showed business activity at a three-month high. The data handed the new government a rare piece of good economic news in its first week and firmed expectations that the Bank of England will hold Bank Rate on Thursday rather than resume cuts. The relief is fragile: the surge in oil through mid-week threatened to feed back into transport costs before crude retreated at the weekend.

What Stalled

The Iran War Grinds On With No Deal in Sight

Geopolitical

For all the weekend’s de-escalation, the war remained unresolved. Trump spent the week threatening a “massive” strike, vowing to bomb a bridge or power plant for every ship hit and menacing Natanz and “Pickaxe Mountain”, while Secretary of State Marco Rubio dismissed Tehran as “not serious” about talks. A mediators’ proposal for a ten-day truce circulated early in the week but never firmed into agreement, and Trump ruled out a deal “for now” even as the strikes paused. A senior Senate Democrat branded the conflict “a war of choice”. The ceasefire is a lull, not a settlement, and the Hormuz blockade has yet to lift.

Gilt Yields Hit a Two-Month High, the Costliest in the G7

Markets

Even as inflation eased, the price of government borrowing climbed. Ten-year gilt yields pushed above 5.10 per cent at a two-month high, leaving Britain with the highest funding costs in the G7 and sterling slipping on the strain. Markets took fright early in the week at Burnham’s run of unfunded giveaways — the VAT cut, the bus-fare cap, cheaper energy bills — with economists warning the largesse points to autumn tax rises. Chancellor John Healey vowed a “fiscal buffer” to reassure the bond market, but the arithmetic of a £110bn debt-interest bill and a stalled economy leaves little room. The gilt market is the first hard constraint on the new premiership.

Russia–Ukraine Talks Falter as Zelensky Sacks His Army Chief

Geopolitical

Rubio and Sergei Lavrov met in Manila to test peace hopes, but the diplomacy went nowhere as both armies traded heavy blows. Russian missiles killed ten on a grain ship off Odesa and struck a Kyiv event killing at least ten more; Ukraine answered with 400-drone raids on the Moscow region and strikes crippling Russia’s Black Sea fuel network. President Zelensky sacked his army chief after days of protests in Kyiv, and the Institute for the Study of War reported the Kremlin laying the ground for fresh mobilisation. Ukrainian civilian casualties reached a wartime record. Eighteen months of attrition show no sign of a breakthrough.

Channel Crossings Return as a Record Boat Lands

Domestic

A record 165 migrants crossed the Channel in a single boat, a stark reminder that small-boat arrivals remain Burnham’s toughest political test even as overall crossings ran 41 per cent below last year. The new government has offered no fresh enforcement legislation, and the asylum question continues to divide Labour’s benches. Nigel Farage, preparing to fight a record 33 rivals in the Clacton by-election, demanded an immediate general election on the grounds that Burnham “has no mandate”. With the Commons in recess, the argument played out in the newspapers rather than the chamber — but it will not wait until September.

What To Watch Next Week

The Bank of England’s Thursday Rate Decision

Markets

The Monetary Policy Committee meets on Thursday with markets betting on a hold at the current Bank Rate, the first policy verdict of the Burnham era. June’s fall in inflation to 2.6 per cent argues for patience; the two-month high in gilt yields and the mid-week oil spike argue against any dovish signal. The decision, and Governor Andrew Bailey’s language on the fiscal outlook, will be read closely by a bond market already nervous about the new government’s unfunded pledges. Any hint that rate cuts have been pushed further out would tighten the vice on mortgage-holders and on Healey’s promised “fiscal buffer” alike.

Whether the Iran Ceasefire Signals Hold

Geopolitical

The weekend’s pause is the war’s first real opening for diplomacy, but it rests on indirect contacts and a Trump who has ruled out a deal “for now”. The test is whether the quiet survives contact with the Strait of Hormuz, still effectively blockaded, and with the Houthis, whose claimed strikes on Aramco sites remain unverified. A durable truce would pull Brent back towards $90 and hand Burnham a cost-of-living dividend; a single tanker attack or a resumed American strike would send crude back above $100 within a session. Watch the shipping lanes, the mediators’ language and the first oil futures to reopen on Sunday night.

Burnham’s Funding Gap and the Threat of Autumn Tax Rises

Domestic

The bill for Burnham’s first-week giveaways now comes due. Economists have warned that scrapping VAT on electricity, capping bus fares and cutting business rates — without offsetting savings — points to tax rises in an autumn Budget, even as the Prime Minister has ruled out immediate increases. Sacked Starmer loyalists have already branded the energy cut “unfunded” from the backbenches. Healey must reconcile the “cost-of-living government” framing with a £110bn debt-interest bill and the costliest borrowing in the G7. Expect the Treasury’s search for revenue — and the internal Labour argument over where it falls — to sharpen through the recess.

Europe’s Fires and Britain’s Fourth Heatwave

Domestic

With more than 267,000 people already displaced across France and Spain and EU water-bombers stretched thin, the wildfire emergency shows no sign of easing as another hot spell builds. At home, Britain braces for a fourth heatwave with temperatures near 35C and widening heat-health alerts; a Cairngorms blaze declared a major incident forced a village to flee, and hosepipe bans continue to spread as reservoirs run low. The week also brought an Essex explosion that hospitalised twenty firefighters. Watch for fresh evacuations abroad, new fire and drought warnings across England, and the strain a prolonged heatwave places on water companies such as the embattled Thames Water.

President Trump has seized on reports that Andy Burnham may fast-track North Sea oil and gas, declaring that people in Aberdeen were “dancing in the streets” and that drilling would turn Britain “from a Poverty Stricken Disaster, to one of the Richest Countries anywhere in the World”. Downing Street has not confirmed any change of policy, and the decision on the Rosebank and Jackdaw fields rests with the Energy Secretary, Miatta Fahnbulleh.

Rosebank, some 130km off Shetland, is Britain’s largest unexploited field; a January 2025 court ruling found consent for it and Shell’s Jackdaw gas field had been granted unlawfully, forcing fresh assessments that now count downstream emissions. A public notice period closes on 17 August. The government insists it will honour Labour’s 2024 pledge against new exploration licences, while remaining “open-minded” on existing projects. The campaign group Uplift dismissed Mr Trump’s enthusiasm, its Robert Palmer calling the North Sea “an ultra-mature basin that has been in decline for years”. The row is an early test of how Mr Burnham balances Washington against his climate commitments. Watch Ms Fahnbulleh’s decision.

‘Burnham Bounce’ Narrows Reform’s Lead in the Polls

Labour has closed much of the gap on Reform UK since Andy Burnham entered Downing Street, according to a run of recent surveys. Survation’s final poll under Sir Keir Starmer put the two parties level on 24 per cent each, Labour’s largest single jump since the 2024 election. Ipsos found Mr Burnham favoured as prime minister by 30 per cent against 16 per cent for Nigel Farage.

The shift marks a striking reversal for a party that trailed Reform for much of the spring, though most pollsters still place Nigel Farage’s party narrowly ahead on voting intention, with the Conservatives languishing around 18 per cent. Ipsos’s Keiran Pedley said Labour would be “more optimistic about the future than they were a month ago”, citing public doubts over whether Reform or the Tories were “ready for government”. The honeymoon is fragile: immigration, the cost of living and the North Sea question could all puncture it. Watch whether the bounce survives Mr Burnham’s first difficult decisions.

Bank of England Set to Hold Rates on Thursday

The Bank of England is widely expected to leave interest rates at 3.75 per cent when the Monetary Policy Committee reports on Thursday, with markets putting the probability of no change at around 86 per cent. The decision comes after a bruising week for oil and bonds, though firmer retail sales and business-activity data late in the week steadied sterling and gilts.

Rates were last held in a 7–2 vote on 18 June, with Megan Greene and the Bank’s chief economist, Huw Pill, favouring a rise to 4 per cent; Mr Pill has since said publicly that rates will need to climb over the coming year. Services inflation at 3.7 per cent keeps the Committee cautious, and the war-driven rebound in crude has prompted traders to price in two rate rises by March 2027. The ten-year gilt yield eased to about 5.04 per cent on Friday after touching a two-month high. For borrowers, the decision and the accompanying forecasts will shape mortgage costs into the autumn. Thames Water Seeks Fresh Bill Rise as Burnham Vows Reform

Thames Water has applied to the regulator, Ofwat, to raise household charges again, presenting an early dilemma for a prime minister who has promised to ease the cost of living and overhaul the water sector. Britain’s largest water company, serving around 16 million customers, has been struggling under a heavy debt burden and repeated questions over its finances.

Dive deeper
The request lands as Mr Burnham weighs how far to intervene in a sector that has become a byword for public frustration over sewage discharges, executive pay and creaking infrastructure. Ministers face a familiar bind: allowing higher bills to fund investment risks a public backlash, while blocking them could push a fragile company closer to failure and a costly special administration. The episode tests the government’s promise of a tougher regime for the privatised utilities. Watch Ofwat’s response and any signal of direct intervention from Downing Street.

Small-Boat Arrivals Remain Burnham’s Toughest Political Test

Immigration remains the sharpest challenge facing the new government, and the issue on which Reform UK has built its lead. Around 36,000 people arrived by small boat in the year to the end of May, about 13 per cent fewer than a year earlier, according to House of Commons Library figures, but the crossings continue to dominate the political argument through the summer.

Dive deeper
Small-boat arrivals account for around 90 per cent of people detected reaching the UK without authorisation, and almost all go on to claim asylum; roughly three-quarters are adult men. Crossings peaked at about 46,000 in 2022 and rose again to some 41,000 last year, so the modest recent fall offers ministers only limited comfort. Reform has drawn much of its support from public frustration at successive governments’ inability to stem the flow. For Mr Burnham, demonstrating grip on the Channel is central to converting his early poll recovery into durable support. Watch the summer crossing figures and any new enforcement measures.
