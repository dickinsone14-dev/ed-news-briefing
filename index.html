Musk Says SpaceX Throttled Starlink Over Ukrainian Drone Strikes

Elon Musk has said his company restricted Starlink in Ukraine because its military “violated Starlink terms of service and used it for offensive drone strikes to kill people”, adding that the loophole “was closed last week”. It is the first acceptance of responsibility after a week of denials. President Zelensky says speeds are now restored.

Dive deeper
The admission is narrower than it first appears, and the distinction is the story. Mr Musk owns the restriction on Starlink, the commercial service, and justifies it: “Using Starlink, a civilian commercial service, for offensive drone strikes to kill people has never been allowed, nor should it be.” But he continues to disclaim the military network: “Starshield, a different system, is controlled by the US government. What they decide to do is up to them.” So responsibility for one network is now claimed and for the other still is not. Until yesterday the denials formed a closed circle, with the American president saying it was not him and that he did not think it was Mr Musk, the secretary of state saying the administration cannot direct a private company, and Mr Musk saying any limits were decided by the military “and ultimately” the president. Ukraine’s own account was that SpaceX acted on a “message from the American government”. While the cap held for more than a week, Kyiv says combat missions failed, drone interception was degraded and Russian missiles arrived without warning. Mr Musk also claimed SpaceX “has done more to defend Ukraine than any private company on Earth”.

Israel to Seal All West Bank Crossings on Election Day

The Israeli military will close every land crossing in the occupied West Bank on Tuesday, when Israel votes, and bar Palestinian workers from entering settlements from today. Hebron remains under blockade. The defence minister says about 2,000 displaced residents will not return until the operation ends.

Dive deeper
Israel Katz made the displacement open-ended by ministerial decision rather than court order: “They will not be allowed to return to their neighbourhood until we’ve finished dealing with all the Hamas issues in Hebron.” How many people that covers is disputed. He says about 2,000; the mayor of Hebron says 2,200; an Israeli rights group counts around 240 families cleared from two neighbourhoods, which is broadly compatible with both. The mayor’s reading of the timing is blunt: the city faces “a complete blockade” and the operation is “driven by the elections”, with ministers “attempting to secure the votes of the far right and the settlers”. One thread we have now followed for three editions remains loose. A narrow court order halting demolitions, obtained on Friday by two factory owners, has still not been reported as upheld, lifted or ruled upon by any source we can read. The only new material is one resident’s allegation that it was disregarded — “A court decree was in place that should have prevented any demolition… They demolished and took me out by force” — which is his account and not a finding. More than 100,000 Palestinians held work permits before October 2023.

Saudi Defences Down a Missile Over Riyadh as Its Airport Reopens

The Saudi-led coalition says air defences intercepted a ballistic missile fired at Riyadh overnight. The capital’s international airport is reopening today after a fortnight closed, and a flight left early this morning. A Houthi spokesman immediately warned that the airport remains “unsafe” and a “military target”.

Dive deeper
That sequence — an interception, a reopening and a threat within hours — is the state of this war in miniature. Everything in it is one party’s claim: the interception is the coalition’s, unverified independently, and the threat is the Houthis’ own. The confusion over Saturday’s missiles is worth recording rather than smoothing, because three accounts disagree about what was aimed at what. The coalition said two missiles were fired at Taif and Yanbu, which is what we published last night. Saudi civil defence said Mecca and Medina. The Houthis called that a “huge lie” and said their targets were three airports, oil infrastructure and an airbase. No independent account resolves it. What is not in dispute is the pattern: Yanbu is the Red Sea terminal through which Riyadh routes crude around the Strait of Hormuz, and the airport closure that has just ended lasted two weeks. Separately, two senior Iranian officials, one of them from the Revolutionary Guards, have told an American newspaper that Tehran is preparing to target United States diplomats and personnel, describing them as fair game.

Serbia Votes Today, With Brazil Choosing a President Tonight

Polls opened in Serbia at six this morning and close at seven tonight, with first indications about two hours later. A student movement born of two years of protest is level with the party that has governed since 2012. Brazil votes later today in a run-off that polling has tied.

Dive deeper
Serbian queues formed before polls opened in Belgrade and Novi Sad. One poll on Thursday put the governing party at 47% against the student list’s 40%, though others have had the students ahead; there is no turnout figure yet. The list has 250 candidates chosen by university faculty assemblies, none with political experience, and no leader, so the cardiologist at its head would not automatically become prime minister. Aleksandar Vucic gave up the presidency, being barred from a third term, to stand for prime minister. In Brazil the run-off follows a first round on 4 October in which Flavio Bolsonaro led Lula da Silva by 47% to 45%. Two polls published since disagree: one on Saturday had Bolsonaro ahead by 46.3% to 45%, another on Thursday had them level on 50% each. Nearly 159 million are registered. Lula drew very large crowds on Saturday at two separate rallies, in Rio and in São Paulo, though no police or independent estimate of either exists. Results should follow within hours of polls closing.

Obama Says the Iran War Handed Tehran Veto Power Over Hormuz

Campaigning in Ohio and Michigan nine days before the midterm elections, Barack Obama said the conflict with Iran had given Tehran “veto power” over the Strait of Hormuz. He called the election “our last best chance to at least rein in some of the foolishness of this administration”.

Dive deeper
The charge is more pointed than the usual campaign fare, because it is a former president arguing that the sitting administration has handed a hostile state leverage over a chokepoint through which a large share of the world’s seaborne oil passes — and it lands while Brent sits at a six-month high and Washington draws down embassy staff across the region. In Cleveland he reached for a longer indictment: after “two years of chaos and cruelty and corruption and incompetence”, voters should decide that “enough is enough”. He also found room for ridicule, asking of the refurbished White House: “What’s with all the gold?” President Trump was in Georgia the same day, telling supporters that “we’re in the golden age of America”, describing oil prices as temporarily high and pledging a payment of $5,000; he voted in West Palm Beach. The midterms fall on 3 November, which is also the date after which reported American and Israeli plans for renewed strikes on Iran are said to begin — reporting that rests on unnamed officials.

Dive deeper

Andy Burnham’s framing was characteristically blunt: “I’m calling time on local leaders going cap in hand to Westminster. That is no way to run a country.” Also in the package: £150m to build capacity in strategic authorities, councils able to borrow from government at preferential rates for housebuilding, and from 2028 a share of income tax revenue handed directly to local areas. The Mayor of London says the borrowing could unlock the West London Orbital and extensions to the Bakerloo line. Burnham Wants an “Honest Discussion” on EU Free Movement

The prime minister has told a newspaper that Britain needs “a very factual, quite evidence-based debate” about free movement, saying the balance of migration has changed. He is declining to rule it out rather than proposing it: the manifesto red lines stand and he says fundamental change would need an election mandate.

Dive deeper
What he actually said is narrower than the headlines it has produced. Free movement “had very significant challenges associated with it”, but was “a type of immigration that perhaps wasn’t as difficult” as now — “revolving door migration where people would come and then go back is different”, mostly younger people going home after a few years. He set one hard limit: “I can’t see myself ever recommending anything that would involve joining the euro or Schengen.” His wider argument is about what was lost beyond movement, including access to European migration databases, which Britain’s own border security commander told members of parliament this week he would like to rejoin. The attack writes itself and both opposition parties have made it: the Conservatives say the public “voted to end free movement, and there can be no return to it”, while Reform calls it “a total betrayal”. The sharpest fact is biographical. Mr Burnham won Makerfield at a by-election in June, a seat that voted to leave in 2016, having promised not to re-run the argument. No Rosebank Decision as a Reporter Reaches the Field at Last

No decision on the Rosebank oil field has been published, and nothing on the government’s own service post-dates July. Hebron Sealed and Razed as Israel Votes on Tuesday

Dive deeper
The displacement figures do not agree and should not be averaged: the municipality puts it at about 300 families, some 1,500 people, with 35 hectares destroyed or marked for destruction, while the mayor is quoted by one outlet at more than a thousand displaced and by another at 2,200. 400,000 March in France With Almost 1,000 Arrested

The interior ministry counted 410,000 demonstrators across France, where unions claim about 800,000. Almost a thousand people were arrested nationally. The Paris march was moved at the last minute from Bastille to the city’s southern and eastern rim, and organisers complied rather than defy the ban.

Dive deeper
That compliance is the correction to this morning’s framing: the route was relocated, not fought over. In Paris the official count was 110,000 against the unions’ 250,000, and the prosecutor reports 64 arrests including six minors, with more than 3,000 people stopped and searched. The interior minister said the marches themselves “passed off well” before police met “an extremely elevated level of violence” from several thousand radical activists at the rear, among them about 500 he identified as rioters; clashes ran roughly two hours. Looting happened but on a far smaller scale than last weekend. Two people were badly hurt, one in Clermont-Ferrand and a twenty-year-old in Toulouse who lost fingers. Something new was recorded too: in Cannes and Montpellier the authorities saw far-right activists claiming to protect businesses or seeking confrontation, which the minister said had not been witnessed before. The movement began with schoolchildren demanding teachers and repairs to leaking buildings, and has widened to the cost of living and the banning of protests.

Ukraine Says Starlink Speeds Are Back to Pre-Restriction Levels

President Zelensky said Starlink and Starshield have “resumed operations at their pre-restriction speeds”, and his defence minister said equipment moving faster than 400km/h now works again. Nobody has said the restrictions were formally lifted, and nobody has yet accepted responsibility for imposing them.

Dive deeper
The distinction matters. Both men describe performance recovering, not an order being rescinded, and the recovery reportedly began overnight. While the cap was in place — about 100mph, for roughly ten days — Ukraine says combat missions failed, drone interception was degraded, strikes behind Russian lines were cancelled and ballistic missiles arrived without American warning. Responsibility remains unclaimed and the denials now form a circle. The American president says “it wasn’t me, and I don’t think it was Elon”; the secretary of state says the administration cannot direct a private company; Elon Musk says the systems are “owned and controlled by the US government” and any limits are decided by the military “and ultimately Potus”. Mr Zelensky blames both, noting that no equivalent limit applied to terminals in European Union countries, only to Ukraine: “In other words, this is a planned operation.” Negotiators from Ukraine, Russia and the United States are expected in Abu Dhabi in the first half of November.

Saudi Arabia Intercepts Missiles Aimed at Its Red Sea Oil Hub

The Saudi-led coalition says it shot down two Houthi missiles heading for Taif and Yanbu, and civil defence briefly warned four western regions including Mecca to seek shelter before rescinding the alerts. Yanbu is the Red Sea terminal through which Riyadh bypasses the Strait of Hormuz.

Dive deeper
That is what gives the attempt its significance: with Hormuz effectively closed to much Saudi crude, the east-west pipeline to Yanbu has become the workaround, and the company’s chief executive says its capacity has been raised from two million barrels a day to seven. A missile aimed at Yanbu is aimed at the detour. Everything here is one party’s account, and the claims run both ways. The interception is the coalition’s own, unverified independently. The Houthis separately claim to have struck a naval base and a camp at Aden, and their health ministry claims nine people were killed in Yemen over two days, which it blames on Saudi Arabia; Riyadh did not respond and the figures could not be verified. Iran’s president, meanwhile, spoke unusually plainly about the cost at home: “There are many people in this society who are struggling to put food on the table because of the war.” He called for an end to a state of “neither war nor peace” without saying how.

Serbia and Brazil Both Vote Tomorrow in Tight Contests

Dive deeper

Its 250 candidates were chosen by university assemblies, none has held office, and it has deliberately refused a leader, so the cardiologist at the top of the list would not automatically become prime minister. Aleksandar Vucic resigned the presidency, being barred from a third term, to stand for prime minister instead. In Brazil the run-off follows a first round on 4 October in which Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of the former president, led Lula da Silva by 47% to 45%. Lula is eighty and seeking a fourth non-consecutive term; most analysts make his opponent a narrow favourite, and since democracy returned in the 1980s no candidate who lost the first round has come back to win. Tens of thousands of his supporters filled Copacabana beach today. Both counts should report on Sunday evening.

Banks Lobby Against a Budget Levy With Nothing Yet Official

Dive deeper
The omission doing the work in that ranking is Paris, which the industry body says it could not model on a like-for-like basis. The Trades Union Congress put it more bluntly: “It’s little surprise that some in the finance lobby are cherrypicking jurisdictions to aid their argument… it tells us nothing about the actual taxes paid by Britain’s big banks.” It estimates that raising the bank surcharge from 3% to 8% would yield £2.25bn a year, and the chief executive of one large bank said on Thursday that a rise would be “bearable”. Police Hunt Prisoner Who Forced His Way Out of Lewisham Unit

And although a prisoner leaving custody from a medical facility invites the question of whether such failures are becoming common, no outlet reports it as part of a pattern, no source prison is identified, and neither the Ministry of Justice nor the prison service appears in any account. On the evidence available tonight this is an incident, not yet a trend.

315 Datacentres Wait for the Grid as Objections Mount

Some 315 hyperscale datacentres are queuing to connect to the national grid, and more than a hundred other planned sites intend to burn gas instead rather than wait. Dive deeper

Five of the largest new and proposed Welsh datacentres would between them consume more electricity than every home in Wales; Scotland has imposed a moratorium on new ones. The scale is visible on the ground in Newport, where a 23-metre building carries 36 air blast coolers and 60 back-up diesel generators. The politics cut across parties rather than along them: a Plaid Cymru member of the Senedd suspects the developer “could be a front for someone else: Google, Microsoft, whoever” and calls the implications for the 2040 net-zero target “massively problematic”, while a Labour councillor backing the scheme says “some of the information being spread is scaremongering”. The communities secretary has separately overturned a council decision blocking a datacentre in east London. This is the other half of the artificial intelligence package the chancellor announced this morning: the compute has to be built somewhere.

Green Councillors’ Deadline Falls Tomorrow With Nothing on Record

Dive deeper

Nine councils have Greens as power-sharing partners, three of them in direct coalition, and the consequences have already begun landing: Green councillors have left the ruling alliance at Runnymede, and the Labour group leader on Lewes District Council has resigned from his own party to sit as an independent cabinet member. In Stafford, where a Green holds the climate portfolio, Labour has confirmed a process would be triggered to remove him if the policy stands. The Stafford group leader said on Friday that his members were “formulating a response” they would want to put to the membership first, and that remains the most recent thing anyone in the party has said. Four of the Greens’ five members of parliament have criticised the motion, which was passed by members at conference last month and backs a single state in all of historic Palestine. One distinction worth keeping clear: the Scottish Greens are a separate party with their own leadership, and held their own conference today.
