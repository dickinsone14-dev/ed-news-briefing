Week of 2–8 November 2026

The Week In Numbers

  • Democrats took both houses of Congress on Tuesday, flipping seven Senate seats including Texas, Kansas, Ohio, Maine and South Carolina. They lead the House by at least 253 seats to 157, and the Senate stands at 54 to 44 with Alaska and Mississippi still counting, where Scott Colom led Cindy Hyde-Smith by fewer than 2,500 votes on Friday. In an election-day voter poll 63% said the United States was wrong to attack Iran. In Jerusalem, 67 members of the Knesset recommended Gadi Eisenkot to form a government against 46 for Benjamin Netanyahu, and Paraguay’s Senate removed Santiago Peña by 44 votes to nil.
  • The Bank of England raised Bank Rate to 4% on Thursday by eight votes to one, its first increase since August 2023, and now expects inflation, 3.5% in September, to peak at 4.9% next spring and return to the 2% target only in the third quarter of 2028. The rise adds about £31 a month to a typical tracker mortgage; 1.4 million households come off fixed deals in the next year, and the Resolution Foundation puts the average worker £390 worse off in real terms in 2027 than in 2025. House prices fell 0.6% in October to £296,285, the sharpest monthly drop since August 2023, with the average two-year fix at 5.97%. Sterling reached its strongest level since June 2016 on the Bank’s trade-weighted index.
  • The Nasdaq fell 5.5% on Thursday, its worst day since 2020, and about $2.5tn was wiped from global technology shares in a week in which the index lost 3.4% and the S&P 500 2.7%; Taiwan’s market had its worst week since 1991. Brent fell 16% to $98.32, below $100 for the first time since 7 October, while US payrolls rose 165,000 against a forecast of 59,000. More than 50 European flights were disrupted by hoax bomb threats; Russia’s strike on Komyshuvakha killed 53; a gunman killed six in Kraków, among them a British citizen; and Russell Brand was convicted of rape and three sexual assaults.

What Moved Forward

Democrats Sweep Congress and Trump Reaches for the Veto Pen

Geopolitical

Tuesday’s midterms ended Republican control of both chambers, something a midterm has done only three times since the Second World War. Kansas elected its first Democratic senator since 1932, James Talarico ended a 38-year drought in Texas and Susan Collins lost Maine after three decades; Hakeem Jeffries is set to be the first black Speaker in January. JD Vance said flatly that “the reason we lost is the war”, and Donald Trump, who called the assessment “largely true”, said he would “do it again”, posted a cap reading “VETO” and, on Friday, replaced Susie Wiles with Stephen Miller as chief of staff. Democrats are mapping oversight hearings and a war powers resolution. The president keeps his veto and his command of the armed forces, so this is a check rather than a change of course, but his judges will stop being confirmed and his nominees now need Democratic votes.

The Bank Moves First, and Says It May Not Be the Last

Markets

After eleven months on hold, the Monetary Policy Committee raised Bank Rate to 4% on Thursday, with Catherine Mann alone wanting half a point. The minutes attribute the move to the Middle East conflict, which pushed Brent to an average of $108 and UK gas to 216p a therm in the fortnight to 27 October, 38% and 81% above July’s assumptions. Andrew Bailey said the rise “should not be seen as the first in an inevitable long sequence of hikes”, but that without progress on the war he could “see the argument for a further increase”; Megan Greene said “additional hikes will likely be required”. The next decision is on 17 December. For the 87% of borrowers on fixed deals nothing changes until they remortgage; for 1.3 million on variable rates and 510,000 on trackers it changed on Thursday. Nationwide answered with a 5.3% members’ bond, a sign that savers, at least, are being competed for.

Herzog Passes Over Netanyahu and Hands Eisenkot the Mandate

Geopolitical

Israel’s election committee certified the result on Wednesday, rejecting Likud’s claim that Arab-led parties had “stolen” the vote, and the consultations that followed went decisively one way: 67 members of the Knesset, including the Joint List and Ra’am, recommended Gadi Eisenkot against 46 for Benjamin Netanyahu. President Herzog told Mr Eisenkot on Saturday night that he would receive the mandate, conferred at a ceremony today. He has 28 days, extendable by 14, to build a coalition, and a majority on paper is not a government: every Jewish party but one pledged before the vote not to govern with the Arab lists, and Mansour Abbas is still declining to call Hamas a terrorist organisation. Donald Trump’s polling-day announcement that American aid to Israel will end this year, which Mr Eisenkot learned of “through the media”, adds urgency. If he fails, the mandate passes back to Mr Netanyahu for four weeks.

Burnham’s Reset Week: North Sea, Settlement and a Draft Deal With Brussels

Domestic

The government legislated steadily while the prime minister was in Antigua. Shabana Mahmood’s settlement rules doubled the default wait for indefinite leave to remain to ten years, with a five-year route for those earning over £50,271 and for doctors, nurses and teachers; care workers already here were spared the fifteen-year wait after Angela Rayner’s objections, a concession the Conservatives called a surrender and Ms Mahmood declined to cost. The Energy Independence Bill removes the North Sea Transition Authority’s power to grant new oil and gas licences, scraps gas standing charges, which ministers say saves 60% of gas account holders about £45 a year, and seeds a £2bn energy wealth fund. Britain and the EU agreed a 200-page draft removing post-Brexit food checks, alongside texts on emissions trading and youth mobility for a summit on 20 November. Each is a bill or a draft rather than a law, but the direction is now set.

What Stalled

Hormuz: Strikes, Mines and a Qatari Plan Nobody Has Signed

Geopolitical

The week began with American “defensive” strikes on Qeshm island and ended with more: a “minor” attack on Friday, Task Force Iron Gate on Saturday extending the blockade to Iran’s land borders and the Caspian, and a US aircraft disabling the cargo ship Huda in the Gulf of Oman. Tehran answered by lifting its self-imposed 2,000km missile-range limit and restating its seven conditions. The Revolutionary Guard says tankers keep striking mines on the southern route, and UKMTO counted at least 11 ships attacked in the week to 6 November, three of them sunk, with transits roughly 65% below pre-war levels. Qatar’s “bridge deal”, under which Washington would lift its blockade and release part of $8bn in frozen assets while Iran reopened the strait and readmitted inspectors, is on the table but unaccepted, and satellite images show rebuilding at the Garmdareh and Shahroud missile sites. Oil fell 16% on the week anyway, on a bet that a chastened White House wants out. Donald Trump promised a decision “very soon”; he has said that before.

The AI Trade Cracks on a Vote About Data Centres

Markets

The rout was political before it was financial. Texas elected a governor, Gina Hinojosa, who promised “a moratorium, a ban, on all data center approvals”, 56% of voters told CNBC that AI and data centres shaped their vote, and Bernie Sanders demanded a national moratorium. On Thursday the Nasdaq lost 5.5%, the S&P 500 4.36% and the Dow 3.44%; Nvidia fell more than 9%, AMD and Micron more than 17%, and the seven largest technology companies shed a trillion dollars between them. Japan’s Nikkei dropped 6%, Seoul halted trading, and Taiwan had its worst week since 1991. A strong jobs report steadied Wall Street on Friday, but London did not join the relief: the FTSE 100 closed 1.01% lower at 10,673.94, more than 300 points off July’s record. The Nasdaq has now fallen four weeks running and sits more than 10% below its peak. Microsoft trimming its “neocloud” partners suggests the build-out is being rationed, not abandoned. For British savers in global trackers, the concentration risk flagged here last week has now been priced.

After Oleshky, the War Returns to Kyiv’s Power Grid

Geopolitical

The Kherson truce ended last Sunday and the week that followed showed what it had interrupted. On Monday nine 3,000kg glide bombs hit a school used as an aid point in Komyshuvakha, a second strike landing as survivors sheltered; the toll reached 53. On Friday night Russia fired 36 ballistic missiles, including North Korean KN-23s, and 172 drones at the Kyiv grid, and Ukraine intercepted four of the missiles. Diplomacy continued in parallel. Melania Trump hosted Russian and Ukrainian envoys at the White House, the first time a first lady has done so, Volodymyr Zelensky awarded her Ukraine’s highest honour for women, and the Kremlin confirmed it will attend US-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday and Wednesday, the first three-way round since February. Mr Zelensky’s verdict was pointed: “When Putin wants the fighting to stop, it stops.” Britain and France will rehearse a post-ceasefire deployment in Poland this month. The humanitarian corridor worked; the war it paused did not.

Decisions Deferred: Police Mergers, Parliament’s Repairs and the Ten-Year Plan

Domestic

Several large domestic questions moved no closer to an answer. Sir Mark Rowley warned that policing is “heading for failure” unless 43 forces become eight to ten, and England’s regional mayors turned on him within a day, Steve Rotheram calling it “the wolf of centralisation dressed up in sheep’s clothing”; Lord Hogan-Howe’s review remains unpublished and Andy Burnham, who ran Greater Manchester’s police as mayor, has not said where he stands. The Public Accounts Committee warned of a Notre-Dame-style fire at Westminster, with restoration now costed at up to £40bn and each year of delay adding £420m, yet the whips have set no date for a vote. Mr Burnham told The Economist his ten-year plan “may go into the new year” and that the Chagos deal must be “recast” to satisfy Washington. At PMQs Louise Haigh could not say when welfare spending will be cut, and the Commonwealth communiqué produced a “time-bound” dialogue on slavery with no date attached. The BMA’s window for averting a consultants’ strike on 25 November also ran down without a deal.

What To Watch Next Week

Abu Dhabi: The First Three-Way Talks Since February

Geopolitical

Moscow arrives having just struck Kyiv’s grid with 36 ballistic missiles; Kyiv arrives asking for written security guarantees and Patriot interceptors, and believing the midterms “may now grow” its chances of American help. Markets

GEO Geopolitical

US extends Iran blockade to land routes; aircraft disables cargo ship

Central Command announced Task Force Iron Gate on Saturday “to prevent Iran from using ground routes to transport advanced weapons and other materials”, extending the months-long naval blockade to Iran’s land borders and the Caspian Sea. Hours later a US aircraft fired “precision munitions at the stern” of the St Kitts-flagged M/V Huda in the Gulf of Oman after it tried repeatedly to run the blockade. Iranian state media reported explosions on Qeshm island and at Jask, Sirik and Bandar Abbas overnight.

Dive deeper
Admiral Brad Cooper said the Tampa-based task force would reinforce “the success of our ironclad blockade against Iran at sea”; CENTCOM says enforcement since July has redirected 147 compliant vessels and disabled five that refused. The Huda is the first confirmed strike on a merchant ship in nearly a month. UKMTO counted at least 11 ships attacked in and around Hormuz in the week to 6 November, three of them sunk, with transits roughly 65% below pre-conflict levels; Iran’s IRGC Navy said two tankers hit mines on Saturday and the UAE rescued 22 crew from a burning cargo vessel. Qatar’s two-day “bridge deal” — blockade lifted and funds released in exchange for IAEA access and a reopened strait — remains on the table but unaccepted.

IRGC lifts self-imposed 2,000km limit on ballistic missile range

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said on Saturday that the range restriction it had long cited to reassure Europe no longer applies. “This limitation has been lifted for us,” its spokesman Hossein Mohebbi told reporters in Tehran, adding that Iran would “demonstrate these capabilities in practice” rather than make statements. The announcement followed Mojtaba Khamenei’s order for an unrestrained response to any future US or Israeli threat.

Dive deeper
The 2,000km envelope already covered the Arabian Peninsula, the Levant, most of Egypt and parts of Greece and the Balkans; Iran is reported to have attempted a strike on Diego Garcia, some 4,000km away, in March. Mr Mohebbi said the IRGC would soon unveil another underground “missile city” and that preparations would be made if Iran chose to alter its nuclear doctrine. Foreign minister Abbas Araghchi said he had spoken to 12 counterparts in 48 hours, including Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, restating Tehran’s seven conditions for reopening Hormuz. The IAEA puts Iran’s stock of 60%-enriched uranium at 440.9kg.

Gunman kills six in Kraków square, including a British citizen

A 25-year-old Polish man opened fire with an automatic weapon from a moving car in Kraków’s Main Market Square at about 3pm local time on Saturday, killing four women and two men and wounding 16, among them two police officers and five foreigners. Polish police said one of the dead was a British citizen. Interior minister Marcin Kierwiński said: “Everything indicates he was fascinated by far-right ideologies.”

Dive deeper
Poland’s defence minister called it an “act of terror”. Late on Saturday Donald Tusk confirmed three bodies, believed to be the suspect’s parents and sister, had been found at his home in Ostrowiec Šwiętokrzyski. The suspect was shot by police and taken to hospital; further weapons and ammunition were found in his vehicle. Mr Tusk praised the two wounded officers as “true heroes” who “likely saved lots of lives while risking their own lives”. The injured include nationals of Austria, Germany, Portugal, Russia and Britain. Kraków has declared mourning until 11 November; legally held firearms in Poland passed one million this year after a record 50,700 permits in 2025.

Russia fires 36 ballistic missiles at Kyiv energy grid before Abu Dhabi talks

Russia struck power infrastructure around Kyiv overnight on Friday with 36 ballistic missiles, including North Korean KN-23s, and 172 drones; Ukraine’s air force intercepted only four of the missiles. The Northern Bridge over the Dnipro was damaged and at least two people were killed across the country. “The capital is facing serious difficulties with its electricity, water, and heat supplies,” Volodymyr Zelenskyy said, with temperatures near freezing.

Dive deeper
The energy ministry said lines carrying power from two nuclear plants to Kyiv and the central regions were hit, with substations struck in five other regions. Mr Zelenskyy accused Moscow of “preparing for trilateral talks by launching ballistic missiles at Ukraine’s critical infrastructure” — US-brokered talks between Moscow and Kyiv are due in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday — and appealed for Patriot interceptors, saying “the United States is in the best position to help address it”. Moscow said it targeted military-industrial, energy and transport sites. Air defences downed 149 of the 172 drones; the four-in-36 missile intercept rate is the watchpoint for the winter.

Saudi coalition downs three Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh

The Saudi-led coalition said on Sunday its air defences destroyed three ballistic missiles fired by Yemen’s Houthis, two of them towards Riyadh and one towards the Eastern Province, according to spokesman Major-General Turki al-Maliki. The Houthi-run health ministry said 33 people, including 13 children, were killed by coalition strikes on the village of al-Shariyah near Sanaa on Saturday; the claim could not be independently verified.

Dive deeper
Fighting has intensified since Yemen’s government launched its Dawn of Yemen offensive last month to retake Houthi-held territory including Sanaa. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree claimed 105 coalition strikes in 24 hours across eight governorates and 4,369 since the escalation began. The UN says more than 1,600 people have been killed and almost 300,000 displaced since the 2022 ceasefire collapsed in July after the Houthis declared a blockade of Saudi ports. The missiles on Riyadh open a second Gulf front at the moment Washington is tightening its own blockade on Iran, the Houthis’ patron.

UK UK Domestic Politics

King leads two-minute silence at the Cenotaph on Remembrance Sunday

The King leads the nation in the two-minute silence at the National Service of Remembrance this morning, joined by the Royal Family, the Prime Minister and senior politicians. About 10,000 veterans from more than 300 organisations take part in the Royal British Legion’s March Past. Andy Burnham said Remembrance Sunday “gives us an opportunity to mark sacrifice and offer gratitude”.

Dive deeper
Culture secretary Lisa Nandy said: “It is our duty to ensure that the immense courage of those who have served is never forgotten.” Defence secretary Wes Streeting added: “We have a moral duty to stand by our veterans and military families who sacrifice so much.” The service is broadcast live on the BBC, ITV and Sky News, with about 10,000 members of the public expected on Whitehall. It is the first Remembrance Sunday of the Burnham premiership and comes with British forces committed in the Gulf for a ninth month.

Burnham writes to councils to house rough-sleeping veterans by Christmas

The Prime Minister is writing personally to every council in England asking for specific plans to offer accommodation to veterans sleeping rough before Christmas. “No one who has served our country should be left to sleep rough on our streets,” Mr Burnham said. The Reducing Veteran Homelessness programme allocates £8.7m to eight organisations, including Veteran Housing Scotland, SSAFA, Launchpad and Wigan Council.

Dive deeper
Downing Street links the drive to the £874m extra for rough sleeping announced in last week’s Budget, taking total funding to a record £4.4bn, and to the Armed Forces Covenant. The veterans’ helpline is said to have already supported more than 1,250 ex-service personnel into housing. The pledge lands the weekend after the Resolution Foundation warned that the Bank’s forecast of falling real pay would hit renters and social tenants hardest, and amid Guardian reporting yesterday that early-release prisoners are swelling street homelessness; councils will be asked to deliver with money already committed.

Opinium: Labour 28, Reform 21, Conservatives 20 as Greens climb to 11

Labour holds a seven-point lead over Reform UK in Opinium’s poll for the Observer, though both fell — Labour to 28% and Reform to 21% — with the Conservatives unchanged on 20% and the Greens up three to 11%. Andy Burnham’s net approval slipped two points to +5; Kemi Badenoch stands at -1, Nigel Farage at -27 and Zack Polanski at -30.

Dive deeper
Asked to choose a prime minister, respondents prefer Mr Burnham to Ms Badenoch by 41% to 22% and to Mr Farage by 47% to 22%. The fieldwork, 4–6 November among 2,050 adults, straddles Thursday’s rate rise. Ipsos’s October poll had Labour on 32% with the Conservatives second on 24% and Reform on 20% — its largest Labour lead since June 2025 — yet only 30% there thought the government deserved re-election against 47% who did not. Keiran Pedley of Ipsos cautioned that “the lesson of the last year is the British politics is volatile”.

Resolution Foundation: Bank forecast means £390 real pay cut by 2027

The Bank of England’s new outlook, not Thursday’s quarter-point rise to 4%, is where “the real danger lies”, the Resolution Foundation says. Inflation is forecast to peak at 4.9% next spring and not return to target until the third quarter of 2028; private-sector real earnings fall 0.5% next year, leaving the average worker £390 worse off in 2027 than in 2025. Some 1.4 million households roll off fixed mortgages over the next year.

Dive deeper
The Bank’s MPC voted 8–1 on 4 November, with Catherine Mann preferring a half-point rise; its summary says CPI, 3.5% in September, “is now expected to rise to around 5% by the middle of next year” and that policy “is not on a pre-set path”. Andrew Bailey said that without “real progress towards settling the conflict in the Middle East” he could “see the argument for a further increase in rates”. Fixed mortgage rates rose about a percentage point before the Bank moved, costing a typical first-time buyer £140 a month; the national mortgage interest bill is projected at £72bn in 2027. Growth is expected to average 1.1% over two years.

Consultants’ 48-hour strike on 25 November nears as BMA deadline runs

Senior doctors in England will walk out from 06:59 on 25 November to 06:59 on 27 November unless Yvette Cooper intervenes, in the first consultants’ action since 2023. The BMA consultants committee co-chairs, Helen Neary and Shanu Datta, said ministers had “kicked the can down the road” and that “that road has now run out”. Hospitals would provide Christmas Day-level cover for the two days.

Dive deeper
The dispute dates from May 2025 and the strike mandate from a July ballot; the dates were set after the government recommended a 2.4% rise for all doctors in 2027/28 to the pay review body, below forecast inflation. The co-chairs said the government had “four weeks to avoid any action taking place” — a window now in its final fortnight. Consultants and SAS doctors in Scotland are being balloted, and Northern Ireland faces consultant strikes on 23–24 November and resident doctors’ action on 20–21 November. With the Bank forecasting inflation near 5%, the pay-review offer is the pressure point.

Trump orders ‘minor’ strikes on Iran as Qatar pushes Hormuz deal

American forces struck an Iranian air-defence site on Qeshm island on Friday, the first attack since the midterms. “They built a little bit of an anti-aircraft device and we knocked it out in about 15 minutes, maybe five,” Donald Trump told reporters in West Palm Beach, adding: “We are talking to Iran. Trump makes Stephen Miller chief of staff as Susie Wiles departs

Donald Trump has replaced Susie Wiles with Stephen Miller, the architect of his immigration crackdown, as White House chief of staff three days after the midterm defeat. “Sometimes in life you have to make moves,” the president said; Mr Miller, 41, starts on Monday and Ms Wiles moves to his political operation. Hakeem Jeffries called the appointment “a dangerous xenophobe and reckless extremist who has American blood on his hands”; Chuck Schumer said Mr Trump “learned nothing from his historic defeat”.

Dive deeper
Mr Trump announced the change on Truth Social, calling Mr Miller “one of my most trusted advisors” and praising Ms Wiles, 69, the longest-serving of the five chiefs of staff he has had, for “an outstanding job”; she is reported to have resigned on Thursday night after the president chafed at stories of her allies landing consulting work. “Stephen’s been with me from the beginning. He gets it,” he said. Mr Miller, who designed the first-term travel ban and family separations and called a protester an “assassin” before retracting it, wrote that he was “honored beyond words” and that the mission was to “fight with all of our soul”. The choice cuts against the post-election mood: 58% of voters told pollsters the administration’s immigration enforcement had “gone too far”, and JD Vance said on Wednesday he was open to shifting enforcement towards employers. Republican dissent is audible — Thom Tillis has called Mr Miller “incompetent” and Congressman Marlin Stutzman said he should have been fired — while Mike Lee hailed “a true conservative warrior”. Democrats who take over the oversight committees in January told him to “lawyer up”. Watch the first clash with the new Congress over immigration funding.

Paraguay’s Peña impeached 44–0 and placed under house arrest

Paraguay’s Senate voted 44 to nil on Friday to remove Santiago Peña from office over the collapse of Ueno bank, and prosecutors arrested him at his home minutes later on suspicion of illicit enrichment, money laundering and influence peddling. Pedro Alliana, the vice-president, was sworn in to serve until 2028 and promised depositors would be “fully compensated”. Around three million people — about 60% of adults — remain unable to reach savings and pensions frozen in the bank.

Dive deeper
Mr Peña, a 47-year-old economist, resigned on Thursday but senators impeached him anyway, finding he had tolerated conflicts of interest, manipulated regulators, diverted pension funds and joined a cover-up involving the central bank, whose governor — a former Ueno chief executive — is also named in the arrest warrant. The lower house had voted 78–0; the Senate refused him 72 more hours by 39 votes to three. Ueno was the country’s fifth-largest bank by deposits and held some $800m of public money, with estimates of state and pension exposure running to $860m; at least 27 people face charges, six directors are in pre-trial detention and nine luxury cars were towed from the lead shareholder’s home. Mr Peña, who held about $1m of the bank’s shares until April 2025, told supporters: “It was never my intention to betray the trust you placed in me”; his lawyer called the case “irresponsible and opportunistic”. The original proposal would have made some lower-paid migrants wait fifteen years and applied the change to people already here, which Angela Rayner, before rejoining the Cabinet as housing secretary, called “un-British”. The consultation drew 200,000 responses. The ten-year wait could be shortened for those with higher incomes or who contribute to their communities. The Home Office will not comment until the statement is made, so the details remain briefed rather than announced, and no start date has been given. The concession on care workers matters because the sector relies heavily on overseas recruits: a fifteen-year wait would have weighed on retention in a service already stretched.
