Morning Briefing

Tuesday 17 November 2026 — 07:55 GMT

What It Means For You

  • Employers of 21 to 24-year-olds: the Milburn report published today is expected to propose doubling the threshold at which you start paying National Insurance on those workers from £5,000 to £10,000, beginning with retail and hospitality.
  • Fuel and energy costs: Brent slipped 0.7% to about $91.8 a barrel in Asian trading this morning, easing rather than spiking despite a fresh tanker strike in the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Exporters, young people and households: agreements with the EU on food, energy and youth mobility are due to be signed at Friday’s summit in Brussels, with talks on the longer-term relationship to follow.

GEO Geopolitical

Ukraine critically damages Kerch bridge as Russia hits Kyiv crossings

The bridge linking occupied Crimea to Russia is closed after an overnight Ukrainian missile strike; photographs suggest a 227-metre road arch has fallen into the Kerch strait. “The enemy has once again attacked the Crimean Bridge. At the moment, traffic has been suspended,” said Sergei Aksyonov, the Moscow-installed governor. Russian ballistic missiles closed Kyiv’s Northern and Southern bridges over the Dnipro the same night, the mayor, Vitali Klitschko, said.

Dive deeper
The strike followed an attack on the bridge’s central arch on Monday; the BBC has not independently verified the images of the collapse, and Russian state media reported a meeting on the situation for Tuesday. Traffic from Crimea must now use the land corridor through occupied southern Ukraine, which is within reach of Ukrainian drones. Volodymyr Zelensky described Monday’s online talks with Steve Witkoff, Jared Kushner and European representatives as “not easy”; participants agreed to meet in Berlin. The EU sanctioned nearly 50 Russian banks and financial institutions. “What we need to see is a ceasefire,” said Kaja Kallas, the bloc’s foreign policy chief. “We see that Russia is really escalating.”

Tanker struck in Hormuz as Iran threatens shipping; third US carrier arrives

On Day 263 of the war, UK Maritime Trade Operations reported a tanker hit by an unidentified projectile in the Strait of Hormuz and set on fire. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards navy warned shipping companies off the US-backed southern route, calling it a “minefield of fire”. The USS Theodore Roosevelt has joined two other American carriers in the region. Brent slipped 0.7% to $91.8 in Asian trading.

Dive deeper
Brigadier General Mohammad Akrami-Nia, the Iranian army spokesman, said the strait would not reopen until “the enemy accepts Iran’s conditions”. Iran’s claims that tankers had struck mines could not be independently verified. The Guards also claimed a drone attack on a US radar at Bahrain’s Isa airbase; Bahraini state television said the attack was thwarted. The American flotilla in the Arabian Sea now includes 13 destroyers and two cruisers alongside the three carriers, maintaining a blockade of Iranian shipping. After CNN reported US intelligence on Iranian planning against officials in Washington, the White House said: “at this time there are no credible threats to the homeland.”

Trump heads for first three-way summit with Xi and Putin

China has confirmed that Xi Jinping will host Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen on Thursday, on the sidelines of the Apec meeting — the first gathering of the three leaders. The White House says Mr Trump will raise nuclear arms reduction and the Iran war but is not expected to bring up Ukraine, the subject highest on Moscow’s agenda. He stops first in Manila, where nearly 300 protesters have been arrested.

Dive deeper
“He will discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world, whose proliferation President Trump believes is very dangerous,” said Olivia Wales, a White House spokeswoman. Neo Wang of Evercore ISI said the main hurdle now appeared to be “a disagreement over the agenda”. For Mr Xi, said Peter Alexander of Z-Ben Advisors, “just the ability to bring Putin and Trump to the table, a first among equals scenario, will deliver much of the political benefit even before the discussions begin”. Mr Trump travels after midterm losses that may weaken his bargaining position. Mr Zelensky had warned of major attacks in the days before the meeting.

Five EU states to open migrant return hub in Rwanda

Germany, Denmark, Austria, Greece and the Netherlands will open a “return hub” in Rwanda, ready to accept transfers in 2027, for people who have exhausted every legal avenue to remain. They are the first to use the EU’s new returns law. Downing Street has not ruled out Britain joining, saying it will watch “with interest”. Spain’s Pedro Sánchez objected: “I don’t see any future or humanity in that.”

Dive deeper
Unlike the scrapped British scheme, which would have sent people to Rwanda to have claims processed and was abandoned by Labour in 2024, this applies only after appeals are exhausted. Officials hope to send 100 people from early next year to test the model; the Dutch government said only single men would be returned in the first phase. Deportees may integrate in Rwanda or return home. The Austrian chancellor, Christian Stocker, said the aim was to relieve European asylum systems and stop traffickers. The five say the plan complies with EU and international law; critics quoted by The Guardian call it “a grave moral failing” and legal challenges are expected.

Washington tightens chip exports to block China’s remote access

The US Bureau of Industry and Security has designated Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil as carrying an “unacceptable risk of diversion”, requiring an export licence for advanced chips shipped there. The rule is aimed at Chinese AI developers who sidestep controls by remotely using data centres in third countries. It lands amid the alleged spy scandal at Nvidia and days before Mr Trump meets Mr Xi.

Dive deeper
The bureau called the change a “logical extension” of existing licensing for exports to China-based firms. Separately, sources told CNBC that Nvidia had warned customers in recent months that the proposed Remote Access Security Act could expose both it and them to US “legal action”, by giving Washington authority over cloud access comparable to its control of physical hardware. The backdrop is the resignation of Jensen Huang as chief executive after reports, not independently confirmed by NPR, of an affair with a woman US intelligence believes was recruited by Chinese intelligence. Elon Musk, rather than Mr Huang, accompanies Mr Trump to Shenzhen this week.

UK UK Domestic Politics

Burnham to open talks on long-term EU relationship after summit

Andy Burnham told the Lord Mayor’s Banquet that “the Brexit we were given has not worked. We have lost more than we have gained.” After Friday’s UK–EU summit, where agreements on food, energy and youth mobility are due to be signed, he will begin discussions on “our long-term relationship”, insisting that “at every stage the British people will be the boss”. The Conservatives accuse Labour of entering the EU “via the back door”.

Dive deeper
The Prime Minister set three tests for any deal: consistency with the 2024 manifesto’s red lines on the single market, customs union and free movement; improvement on the original settlement; and a “Makerfield test” of tangible benefit to everyday life. He called Britain’s isolation “what our enemies wanted”. The Guardian reports he is signalling that the red lines could be dropped in the next manifesto, while some ministers want him to “press the brakes”; Brexit supporters have founded two campaign groups. The Times reports that negotiators have agreed a clause in the food deal requiring compensation from whichever side withdraws, designed to bind a future government.

Milburn report urges NI cut and benefit curbs for under-25s

Alan Milburn’s year-long review of youth unemployment, published today, is expected to recommend doubling to £10,000 the threshold at which employers pay National Insurance on 21 to 24-year-olds, starting with retail and hospitality. Under-25s would lose access to the health element of universal credit and Pip, alongside a legal guarantee of a job, training or support. Nearly one million 16 to 24-year-olds are not in work, education or training.

Dive deeper
Mr Milburn has warned that “there is no such thing as a free lunch” and that the task will take ten years. Employers currently pay 15% on earnings above £5,000 for non-apprentices aged 21 to 24; abolishing the charge at once would cost £5.1bn, according to the Resolution Foundation, which Mr Milburn judges a poor use of money. The Telegraph reports the plan rejects “crude benefit cuts” and yields no savings for a decade; the Metro highlights a proposed Youth Opportunities Service in the 250-page report. Helen Whately, the shadow work and pensions secretary, said ministers should “just stop doing the taxing”. The i reports that business will press for the cuts sooner.

Failed asylum seekers to lose five-day notice of removal

Shabana Mahmood will amend the Immigration and Asylum Bill so that people who have exhausted their appeals can be removed without warning. The current five working days’ notice, which ministers believe invites last-minute legal challenges, would be replaced by a six-month window for immediate removal. “Removing people at such short notice will cause unnecessary distress and anxiety,” said Imran Hussain of the Refugee Council.

Dive deeper
The model is Denmark, where the Home Office says the number awaiting removal more than halved over five years to under 1,000 after a similar change; it has not said how many additional removals it expects here. Mr Hussain argued that “voluntary removals are far more effective at removing people than enforced returns” and cheaper for the taxpayer. In June 2026 there were 40,168 people awaiting an initial asylum decision and a further 90,341 awaiting the outcome of an appeal. The amendments would also create an Independent Immigration Appeals Authority. The change comes as five EU states announce a Rwanda return hub that Downing Street says it will watch “with interest”.

Miliband says he will not resign if Rosebank is approved

Ed Miliband, the Foreign Secretary, said he would “definitely not” resign if the Government approves the Rosebank oil field, which he called “climate vandalism” in 2023. “I’m a member of the government and I will abide by collective responsibility on this,” he told the Today programme. The decision rests with Miatta Fahnbulleh, his successor as energy secretary. At least 75 Labour MPs are reported to have signed a letter opposing the project.

Dive deeper
Mr Miliband stressed that the ruling is quasi-judicial: the energy secretary “looks at all the evidence and then reaches a conclusion”. He backed her recent approval of the Jackdaw gas field while arguing that Ukraine and Iran show “we can’t get economic and energy security from fossil fuels”. Catherine Fookes, the Monmouthshire MP, said drilling would “make climate change worse”; Steve Yemm, MP for Mansfield, called Rosebank a “test” of whether Britain can support North Sea investment while meeting its climate obligations. Rosebank, about 80 miles north-west of Shetland, is Britain’s largest untapped oil field; The Sunday Times has reported a parliamentary private secretary is weighing resignation.

Ministers to end Palantir’s NHS contract — but not until 2029

The Department of Health will extend Palantir’s contract to run the NHS federated data platform by two years rather than use a break clause next spring, The Guardian reports. “The current contract will remain for two years, before coming to an end,” James Frith, the health minister, told MPs, citing public trust and “over-dependence” on one supplier. British firms will be invited to bid to replace it.

Dive deeper
Chi Onwurah, who chairs the Commons science and technology committee, called the decision “deeply disappointing”, her committee having repeatedly urged ministers to exercise the 2027 break clause. Martin Wrigley, a Liberal Democrat MP, said ministers had “kicked the can down the road to March 2029”. NHS England agreed the contract in 2023; last week it deleted a claim that 137 trusts were benefiting from the platform after The Guardian disclosed that 29 used it never or rarely. Amnesty International and Just Treatment had campaigned for termination. The question now is whether a domestic supplier can be procured and ready within the two-year extension.

Estonia to shut Russian border after GRU-linked arson plot on drone maker

Estonia will close its three border crossings with Russia from 1 December after eight people were detained over an attempted arson attack on the drone manufacturer Threod Systems, which Estonian security services say was ordered by Russia’s GRU military intelligence. “Russia must understand that hostile actions against Estonia come with consequences,” said the foreign minister, Margus Tsahkna. No one was injured and no reopening date has been given.

Dive deeper
The prime minister, Kristen Michal, said that “if Russia continues its hybrid operations in Estonia, and we know that Russian intelligence services exploit border crossings to exert influence over people, we must be prepared to reduce the risks we face”, adding that closure would “send a clear message that attacks on Estonia’s defence industry and defence capabilities are unacceptable”. The crossings affected are Narva, Luhamaa and Koidula; Mr Tsahkna said the move would also strengthen efforts to prevent sanctions circumvention. Latvia’s interior minister, Janis Dombrava, confirmed Latvian citizens and non-citizens were among those arrested. Marko Mihkelson, who chairs the Estonian parliament’s foreign affairs committee, called it part of Russia’s “shadow war” against Europe and urged allies to “respond with asymmetric measures of their own”. Moscow’s Maria Zakharova dismissed the measures as “aggressive and unrestrained Russophobia” and said Russia would “check all this for veracity”. Watch for Baltic neighbours following suit.

Europe promised a seat at Ukraine talks; Kremlin stays evasive

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner left Moscow saying “both sides” had agreed to a European presence at the next round of Russia–Ukraine negotiations, something Kyiv and European capitals have sought for nearly two years. Mr Witkoff called it “a very big deal” and reported “progress” with Vladimir Putin. The Kremlin has neither endorsed nor rejected the idea: “Putin has never refused to engage with the Europeans,” said Dmitry Peskov.

Dive deeper
Mr Peskov set conditions: “If the Europeans truly have someone who is authorised to hold discussions … then, of course, we would be prepared to talk with them,” and only “once we understand their actual intentions and their willingness to contribute to the peace process” would Moscow decide on “a dialogue in an expanded format”. António Costa said the European team would be represented by the EU institutions. Volodymyr Zelensky welcomed the shift, saying “it is important that Europeans, next time, next meeting” are at the table, as Ukraine grows ever more dependent on European military, diplomatic and financial support. The envoys met Mr Zelensky and Mr Putin on the same day; the analysis from Moscow is that Mr Putin is playing for time while Donald Trump remains keen on a diplomatic win in a conflict he once said he would end in a day. Separately, Mr Zelensky says Ukrainian units have recorded “systematic speed restrictions” on SpaceX’s Starshield service along the entire front line: interceptor drones lose contact with operators above 161kph, leaving them unable to catch Russian Shaheds. “We are counting on these restrictions to be lifted,” he said on Sunday; SpaceX declined to comment. Watch whether a date and a European delegation are confirmed this week.

Duffy deflects blame for US airspace meltdown as funding fight looms

Sean Duffy, the US transportation secretary, declined to say he was accountable for Friday’s nationwide ground stop that disrupted weekend air travel across America. “I take responsibility for what was given to me by prior administrations,” he told CNN, repeating his call for tens of billions of dollars to modernise dated aviation systems. Donald Trump backed him, saying more money was needed “FAST!” Cancellations and delays lingered on Sunday but fewer flights were disrupted.

Dive deeper
Officials are analysing two routers installed in recent weeks at Federal Aviation Administration facilities in Arkansas and Texas; “we’re going to figure out what was the root cause and then move forward from there,” Mr Duffy said across five Sunday television appearances. Senior Democrats on the two House panels overseeing aviation say federal officials will brief them on Monday on his funding request. Democrats have signalled willingness to fund the department but scepticism about how existing allocations have been spent, setting up a confrontation over whether the breakdown was a question of money or management. For British travellers the practical risk is transatlantic knock-on: airlines were still resuming normal schedules on Sunday. The episode lands as the Trump administration is simultaneously pressing for Congressional backing on Ukraine diplomacy and Iran, with the House narrowly divided. Watch whether Monday’s briefing produces a bipartisan figure or a blame exercise.

Trident boat home after record 217 days submerged as fleet strain shows

HMS Vengeance returned to Faslane after 217 days submerged, the longest undersea deployment by a British vessel, having left on 12 April. Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, welcomed the 140-strong crew at HM Naval Base Clyde and said it “doesn’t sit lightly” with him that patrols have stretched so far. Typical Vanguard-class patrols have more than doubled in recent years, with six-month tours now routine because of a shortage of seaworthy vessels.

Dive deeper
The boat came up Gare Loch encrusted in marine growth with some of its sound-absorbing tiles missing, and its return had been expected the weekend before but was delayed when the submarine relieving it had to return to base to fix a technical fault. The First Sea Lord, General Sir Gwyn Jenkins, praised submariners for keeping Nato safe at a conference in Oslo last week but said the UK must do better at maintaining the fleet. The continuous at-sea deterrent depends on four ageing Vanguard boats until the Dreadnought class arrives, and every extended patrol narrows the margin for refits. The record is a measure of crew endurance and of industrial strain in equal part. Watch for Mr Streeting’s plans on submarine availability.

Suspected bird flu at County Tyrone farm; results due today

A suspected case of avian influenza at a commercial poultry farm in County Tyrone is under investigation after “significant” mortality and clinical signs consistent with the disease. Samples have gone to the national reference laboratory in England with results expected on Monday, movement restrictions are in place and the flock will be culled if the disease is confirmed. It would be Northern Ireland’s first case this season.

Dive deeper
Andrew Muir, the agriculture and environment minister, said: “While the disease has not yet been confirmed, we are treating this situation with the utmost seriousness,” adding that “robust contingency plans” were ready should highly pathogenic avian influenza be confirmed. The chief veterinary officer, Brian Dooher, urged all flock keepers to observe strict biosecurity. A housing order was introduced in England and Scotland last week because of the heightened seasonal risk and a series of outbreaks. Bird flu presents little risk to human health but can be catastrophic for flocks, and the commercial poultry sector is an important part of Northern Ireland’s economy. Watch Monday’s laboratory result and any extension of housing orders.

UK UK Domestic Politics

Burnham: ‘The Brexit we were given has not worked’

Andy Burnham will tell the Lord Mayor’s Banquet on Monday that “the Brexit we were given has not worked. We have lost more than we have gained,” likening the deal to “the emperor’s new clothes” and declaring that “the emperor has nothing on”. He goes to Brussels on Friday seeking agreements on food, energy and youth mobility that he says could raise GDP by 0.4%, about £17bn. The Conservatives accuse Labour of “dragging us into the European Union via the back door”.

Dive deeper
The prime minister’s argument is that Brexit was not wrong but that “a needlessly hard Brexit” created barriers for business and that Boris Johnson’s promise to “take back control” was never delivered; he first used the “lost more than we gained” line at the Welsh Labour conference on Saturday. The summit package covers food and farming, emissions trading and a youth mobility scheme, and he says he will sign only a “win-win” deal. Monday’s papers show the counter-attack forming: the Telegraph carries Mr Johnson insisting “Britain would back Brexit again” if put to a second vote, and the Express warns of the EU’s asylum solidarity scheme. The City is conspicuously quiet: “We’re not going to be lobbying or aggravating or pushing it our side,” one banking lobbyist said, after a decade spent rewiring for life outside the bloc. Food exporters hope the deal eases checks that have throttled trade since 2021. Watch the speech text tonight and the Conservative response ahead of Friday.

Milburn to urge National Insurance cut for under-25s and fewer benefits

Alan Milburn’s year-long review of youth unemployment, published on Tuesday, will recommend gradually lowering employers’ National Insurance on workers aged 21 to 24 over five years, starting in retail and hospitality, alongside moving under-25s off the health element of universal credit and personal independence payment. Nearly one million 16 to 24-year-olds are not in work, education or training, at a cost Milburn puts at £22bn a year. “There is no such thing as a free lunch,” he said.

Dive deeper
Under the plan, under-25s would receive an allowance at the basic universal credit rate, with a new “additional cost allowance” for some to cover costs related to their conditions; Milburn says the changes would affect existing and new claimants and would not be “pain-free”. He also wants a “legal guarantee” of work, training or employment support, says GPs should no longer be a “route into the welfare system” via fit notes, and warns it will take ten years to reach a “reasonable level” of youth unemployment. The politics are hazardous for Andy Burnham: last year 120 Labour MPs threatened to derail Sir Keir Starmer’s welfare reforms and 49 still voted against even after existing Pip claimants were protected. Helen Whately, the shadow work and pensions secretary, said the government should “just stop doing the taxing”. Watch Tuesday’s publication and the Labour backbench reaction.

Reform lands ‘gobsmacking’ £36m gift, with a second donor matching it

Nigel Farage’s party has received a £36m donation, a sum one veteran campaign chief called “gobsmacking”, and a second donor has pledged to match it with another £36m, saying the first had provoked his “competitive spirit”. The money arrives as Andy Burnham and Kemi Badenoch revive a sharper red-versus-blue contest, with one cabinet minister calling the new divide “clarifying”.

Dive deeper
A member of Ms Badenoch’s top team says “normal jogging is reasserting itself” after years in which smaller parties shoved the traditional duopoly aside, and the cabinet minister sees a clear gap in “sense of purpose and values” between Labour and the Tories. The scale of Reform’s war chest is without precedent in British party funding and lands days before the UK–EU summit that Mr Farage will seek to frame as a Brexit betrayal. Elsewhere on the right, the Conservatives pledged on Sunday to halt screen-based exams and cut coursework to curb pupils’ use of AI, with Laura Trott saying “everything in schools must be safe for children and improve standards”. Watch the Electoral Commission filings and how the two main parties respond to being outspent.

Ethics watchdog chief steps back over past sexual misconduct findings

Stuart Sterling, the first chief executive of the Ethics and Integrity Commission, has stepped back after the Sunday Times revealed he was investigated in a previous job over alleged sexual misconduct involving female colleagues and in one case sanctioned. “These are serious allegations. Stuart Sterling has stepped back from his role while we swiftly establish the facts,” a government spokesperson said. He was appointed in October 2025.

Dive deeper
The Sunday Times reported that in 2003 Mr Sterling faced a “full misconduct hearing” as a junior officer with Northamptonshire Police in a case treated as sexual harassment. The commission was a 2024 Labour manifesto pledge to “ensure probity in government” after the scandals of the Covid years, and its chief executive advises the prime minister on tackling misconduct in public life, which makes the embarrassment acute. The question for Downing Street is what vetting was done before the appointment and who signed it off. Watch whether the inquiry is handed to an external figure.

Jackdaw gas starts flowing as Rosebank decision still hangs

Gas has begun flowing from the Jackdaw field, 150 miles east of Aberdeen, a month after ministers approved it following years of uncertainty and a court case. “Last month, I gave Jackdaw the green light and today its gas will start flowing into Britain, thanks to the hard work of the skilled North Sea workers,” said Miatta Fahnbulleh, the energy secretary. The field is operated by Adura, the Shell–Equinor joint venture. No decision has yet been taken on Rosebank.

Dive deeper
Ms Fahnbulleh says the “homegrown gas” will help heat millions of homes and businesses this winter, an argument that carries extra weight with Brent near $96 on Monday morning and the Iran conflict in its ninth month. Rosebank, the UK’s largest known untapped oil field off Shetland, is the harder call: Labour MPs are already warning of a rebellion if Andy Burnham approves it, and the Jackdaw decision was itself contested on environmental grounds. The government is trying to hold a line between energy security and its climate commitments. Watch for the Rosebank timetable and the backbench letter count.

Abu Dhabi day one ‘constructive’, but Zelensky rejects Donbas-for-security trade

Four hours of US-mediated talks between Russia and Ukraine ended on Tuesday with Volodymyr Zelensky calling them “constructive and quite substantive” and Marco Rubio “very productive”, though Sergei Lavrov ruled out a ceasefire: “We will not agree to it.” Mr Zelensky said the Americans had asked whether Kyiv would “trade the Donbas for security”, with Nato membership off the table and “something similar” offered instead. “No one believes that Putin will stop if we give up the Donbas,” he said.

Dive deeper
“He might pause — but then he will return in a year, two years or five years. And who will be to blame then?” the Ukrainian president wrote, while reporting that the delegations “discussed in detail the substance of security guarantees for Ukraine and the foundations for their long-term implementation”. Jared Kushner opened by saying Washington had “narrowed the gaps”; a US official described a guarantee “effectively very similar to Nato” and insisted “it really is a final end to the war” that is sought, with the Donetsk question unresolvable “until they know what the security architecture is going to be”. Mr Rubio, in Paris, answered Europe’s complaint directly: “Well, Europe’s at the table. They’re with us,” though the European envoys met the Americans separately and issued a statement demanding any deal “respect sovereignty, territorial integrity, independent decision-making, and provide credible security guarantees”. The money row surfaced too: Robert Fico called Friedrich Merz’s demand that every member state fund Ukraine “typical blackmail”, with Kyiv seeking €70bn against €45bn pledged and €210bn of Russian assets frozen. Watch whether Wednesday produces a text or another date.

Hegseth calls impeachment ‘a farce and a stunt’ as House vote looms

Pete Hegseth used his first Pentagon briefing in six months to attack Thomas Massie’s impeachment articles: “What one disgruntled and defeated congressman is attempting is not oversight, and it’s not accountability. It’s a political spectacle, a personal manifesto driven by ego and resentment.” “It’s a farce, and it’s a stunt, and everybody knows it,” he said. The House was due to take up the resolution on Tuesday evening, with Mike Johnson promising to “dispense with the impeachment gambit” by a motion to table or refer.

Dive deeper
“None of these are high crimes or misdemeanors. They’re partisan policy disagreements from people who want to politicize our military,” the defence secretary said, adding: “This is not patriotism. This is sabotage.” He said every operation had been “ordered by the commander-in-chief, then ordered by myself”. Mr Trump awarded him “an ‘A’ PLUS, PLUS, PLUS” and called Mr Massie “a TOTAL LOSER”; JD Vance said the question of impeachable crimes was “absurd and the answer is: obviously not”; Mr Rubio said Mr Hegseth had executed “a direct order from the commander-in-chief … quite effectively”. In a 218–214 House Mr Massie needs at least three Republicans; French Hill wants “to hear the debate” and Don Bacon is “wrestling”, while Mr Johnson said: “I expect that we’re going to defeat that one way or the other.” Ted Cruz, by contrast, judged it “extremely likely, if not certain” that the House will eventually impeach and that Mr Hegseth will “step down voluntarily, or Trump will fire him”. A referral to committee would let the articles die on 3 January; no result had been recorded by 19:30 GMT. Watch the tabling vote.

Brent dips below $90 as Rubio sees ‘no need’ for Iran combat

Brent crude fell to $89.03 on Tuesday, its lowest since 31 August, after Marco Rubio told G7 counterparts in Paris that “the economic campaign against Iran is so devastating that right now there is no need for major combat operations”, before settling near $92. Pump prices follow: America’s average dipped below $4 for the first time since July and the AA says UK petrol, at 169.7p, should fall another 10p. Iran dismissed France’s fridge-sabotage accusation as “baseless and hostile”.

Dive deeper
Mr Rubio said Donald Trump “has concluded that for the time being there is no need for major combat operations because we are achieving the same objective by economic means”, while warning that the military “stands ready” to exact “a terrible price” if red lines are crossed; the president had said on Monday that “Iran militarily is effectively over”. Oil has now unwound most of the war premium that took Brent from about $60 in January to $130 in the spring, and analysts at ING describe traders stripping out geopolitical risk, though FGE’s Fereidun Fesharaki “just can’t see a deal being made” and gives the diplomacy a two-week window. On the cyber front, Iran’s foreign ministry issued its first response to Jean-Noël Barrot’s attribution of October’s supermarket attacks to the Revolutionary Guard’s Shahid Kaveh unit; Paris has summoned Tehran’s ambassador and is pursuing EU sanctions and arrest warrants for the unit’s head and two deputies. Esmaeil Baghaei says Iran “remains committed to the NPT” but “we cannot act like normal circumstances”; the IAEA says requested access has not been granted. Watch the Qatari channel and Friday’s weekly oil close.

Anthropic seeks up to $1.4tn in record flotation as Nvidia falls again

Anthropic, the maker of the Claude chatbot, set a price range of $70 to $78 for 825 million shares, raising up to $64.4bn and valuing the company at up to $1.4tn fully diluted — about $1.1tn to $1.3tn on the basic share count — in what would be the largest listing in history, trading on Nasdaq as ANT from 19 November. Nvidia fell a further 6% as its board hired Wachtell Lipton and a former Manhattan prosecutor to run the Huang investigation.

Dive deeper
