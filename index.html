Morning Briefing

Wednesday 28 October 2026 — 08:42 GMT

What It Means For You

  • If you heat your home with gas: the typical annual bill is forecast to rise by £276 in January. The Chancellor has said standing charges, which average £108 a year, will go for about 23 million homes, but unit prices will rise to cover them. The Treasury estimates three-fifths of households save about £45 a year on average; heavier users will pay more.
  • If your only income is the state pension: the full state pension is set to rise to £13,036.40, above the £12,570 tax-free allowance, which would draw about a million people into income tax. Today’s Budget is expected to set out a new personal allowance that keeps them out of it.
  • If you are saving for a first home in England: more detail is due today on the Your First Home scheme — a 2.5% deposit on a new-build and a loan of 20% of the price, for joint incomes up to £110,000, or £140,000 in London. Likud Leads the Count, but Neither Bloc Reaches 61 Seats

Exit polls make Likud the largest party, on 27 seats to Yashar’s 21, while Arab-led parties are projected to win a record combined 19 on turnout above 77%.

Dive deeper
The count is early and skewed towards right-wing areas, and many absentee ballots, mostly soldiers’, are yet to be counted; a clear result may take a day or two, and tallies differ between outlets as it moves. Both men claimed the night. “We must form a strong, national, right-wing government,” Netanyahu said, having warned followers all day about high Arab turnout. “The coalition of failure has lost the people’s trust,” Eisenkot replied. That leaves the Arab parties, which most Jewish opposition parties pledged not to work with, as possible kingmakers. Dive deeper
The claim is so far only the Houthis’ own, and no damage assessment has appeared in approved reporting. It cuts across Tuesday’s more hopeful signal. In Yemen, nearly 100 fighters have been killed in clashes near the Bab el-Mandeb, and a Central Command estimate puts the war’s cost to the US at $47bn. UBS expects Brent to stay near $110 until the year-end, as global inventories have fallen by about 500 million barrels since February. “Vessels remain exposed to attacks,” its analyst Giovanni Staunovo noted.

John Healey presents his first Budget at about 12:30 today, the first of Andy Burnham’s premiership, conceding that Britain faces “a difficult moment”. Gas standing charges are to go for about 23 million homes and fuel duty is expected to stay frozen. Dive deeper

Debt Interest Nears £100bn as War Shrinks the Chancellor’s Headroom

About £100bn of the roughly £1.42tn Healey will allocate this year goes on debt interest, with government borrowing costs at multidecade highs since the war on Iran began in February. The Resolution Foundation estimates the £24bn buffer built at the last tax-raising Budget has halved to about £12bn. Manifesto pledges rule out raising income tax, National Insurance or VAT.

Dive deeper
The Institute for Fiscal Studies calls the backdrop “unquestionably bad news for the economy”. It estimates that higher debt-servicing costs from the conflict will add £10bn–£15bn to borrowing in 2029-30, and that spending plans which already looked tight will get tighter with inflation. “We have a relatively new government with seemingly big ambitions but facing a long list of constraints,” said its director, Helen Miller. Healey is spending about £1bn a month more than forecast. A £4.7bn gap in the defence investment plan is to be plugged largely by cuts elsewhere. The roadmap to 3% of GDP by 2030 waits for next year’s spending review. As Healey himself said last month, “Britain has it harder now.”

Dive deeper

The climate researcher Greg Muttitt, who opposes the field, agrees on the legal point: “the UK government is not going to find itself in any international court if it approves Rosebank.” Campaigners argue that the case against was always about leadership, not direct emissions. The field would produce around seven times the emissions of the Jackdaw gasfield approved last week. Greenpeace and Friends of the Earth have written to the Prime Minister urging him to weigh North Sea drilling, Heathrow and heat-pump grants together. Rejection would leave a hole in the Treasury’s sums on Budget day.

Record Israeli Turnout, and a Fight Over Closing Time

Two-thirds of Israel’s electorate had voted by six o’clock this evening, 66.7%, the highest since such things were first measured in the early 1970s. The prime minister’s party and its coalition partners have asked the electoral authorities to keep polls open past ten; the opposition says that is groundwork for challenging the result.

Dive deeper
Polls close at eight this evening by our clock, so nothing here is a result and no projection appears in this edition. The electoral committee’s own figure was almost 45% by two in the afternoon, six points above the same hour in 2022; official hourly updates put it at 66.7% by six, a nine-point increase. In Nazareth, the largest Palestinian-majority town in Israel, turnout passed 32% by two o’clock against 28% four years ago, still below the national Jewish average but rising; the prime minister spent the day posting about high Arab turnout. The extension request is the live dispute, with the committee convening to rule on it and several complaints already filed by parties in the anti-Netanyahu bloc, though the committee says there is no evidence of systematic tampering with ballot slips. The echo is uncomfortable and worth naming: in Brazil on Sunday the losing candidate’s stated ground for seeking annulment was a judge’s decision to extend voting by two hours. At least 62 people were killed and 52 wounded when gunmen attacked the Binnari community in Taraba state, north-eastern Nigeria, on Friday, according to the state head of the national emergency agency. Thousands have been displaced. Fifty-two bodies have been recovered so far, fifteen of them from the Benue River.

Dive deeper
We declined this story on Sunday, and the reason is worth revisiting now that it has resolved. Two days ago the figures in circulation were irreconcilable — police had recovered at least ten bodies, survivors told a news agency at least twenty, and a human rights organisation’s country director said up to fifty — and publishing any of them would have meant choosing one. Nordic Leaders Hold a Rare Joint Call With Putin

The president of Finland and the prime minister of Norway spoke to President Putin for ninety minutes from Helsinki, in a call they initiated to discuss ending the war in Ukraine. Dive deeper

Ukraine’s president spoke to both leaders afterwards and thanked them for their “active support”, which is the detail that matters most — this was not negotiation over Kyiv’s head. It lands the same day as a local truce holding in the south, brokered through an unusual channel, and a week after Kyiv said a three-way meeting with Washington and Moscow would happen in November, now reported for Abu Dhabi. Denmark’s prime minister called the Nordic initiative one she was “very sceptical” of, and said it “was not agreed with any of us”.

Turkish Committee Clears the Mecca Pact for a Full Vote

Turkey’s parliamentary foreign affairs committee has approved the mutual defence agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, carried by the governing party and its allies over opposition objections. It now goes to the full assembly for debate and a vote. The published text binds the parties for ten years.

Dive deeper
This completes a thread we have followed for a week. When the pact was signed in August it committed each party to treat an armed attack on one as an attack on all, using “all necessary means”; Pakistan ratified it, and Turkey began and had not finished. A committee is not the chamber, so ratification is still incomplete, but the direction is now clear and the ten-year term is on the record. The committee’s chairman, from the governing party, made the case in terms designed to reassure rather than deter: the agreement “aims to increase deterrence and cooperation, does not target other countries and is likely to contribute to peace and stability”. That last clause is doing a lot of work in a week when the three signatories’ defence and intelligence chiefs sat in Tehran pressing Iran to rein in the Houthis, and when attacks on Saudi Arabia have continued. Watch the full assembly vote, and whether Iran reads a ten-year commitment as reassurance or as encirclement.

Minimum Wage to Rise, but the Age Gap Stops Closing

Tomorrow’s Budget is expected to raise the minimum wage by 3.9% for every adult band. That is the point of contention: because younger workers get the same percentage rather than a larger one, the gap between them and older workers stops narrowing, and a manifesto promise to abolish age bands altogether recedes.

Dive deeper
The 2024 manifesto promised to remove “discriminatory age bands” so that all adults were entitled to the same minimum wage, and for two years the government delivered on it by raising the youth rate faster — 26% since 2024. An equal percentage rise does the opposite: in cash terms the gap widens. The business secretary’s defence is that timing was never promised: “The manifesto pledge on equalisation still applies but we were always clear it could happen over time.” A union leader called it “deeply unfair” to keep paying different rates for the same job. Two cautions belong with this. The specific rates in circulation — the 18-to-20 band going from £10.85 to about £11.27, and the rate for those 21 and over from £12.71 to £13.20 — originate with a newspaper that is not on our approved list and reach us only second-hand, so they are reported rather than confirmed. And the whole package remains a Budget expectation until the chancellor stands up tomorrow.

Warm Home Loans Open for Heat Pumps and Solar Panels

A government-backed loan scheme opened today offering households interest-free or low-interest borrowing from £7,500 to £35,000 to fit heat pumps, batteries and solar panels with no money upfront. The government says the full combination can cut a bill by up to £550 a year, and by a further £300 on a time-of-use tariff.

Dive deeper
This one is already live rather than trailed, which separates it from most of what is circulating this week. The borrowing is offered through commercial lenders at a discount to high-street rates: up to £10,000 for an air-source heat pump and up to £35,000 for a ground-source system, and it can be stacked on top of the existing grant of up to £9,000 towards replacing a fossil-fuel boiler. The case for it rests on bills rather than carbon, and there is a trial behind the claim — fuel-poor households in Coventry saved more than £250 a year on average by pairing a home battery with a tariff that charges less outside peak hours. The obvious caveat is that a loan is a debt: the saving has to outrun the repayment, and the published figures are the government’s own. Set it against gas at a four-year high and a forecast 16% rise in bills in January, and the arithmetic matters more than usual.

The business secretary says officials will begin discussing the range of future options for the relationship with the European Union, free movement among them, before the summit in Brussels on 20 November. Dive deeper
That contradiction is tonight’s story and it is not a matter of emphasis. Pressed on whether officials are talking to Brussels about free movement, the business secretary said “they will be talking to them about what the future options for our relationship might be”, while insisting “here and now, the focus should be on the summit”. The spokesman’s formulation was that the government is working “on the areas set out and agreed ahead of the UK-EU Summit and within the manifesto red lines” — the summit agenda being food, youth mobility, electricity and emissions trading. A second ambiguity needs owning, because it touches what we published last night. Accounts of the prime minister’s own position differ: he answered “no” when a broadcaster asked whether he could bring in free movement without a referendum, which is what we reported, but a fuller account of his weekend interview has him saying a referendum would be needed only to rejoin the Union outright. Both renderings are in approved sources and we are not choosing between them. One hard thing did happen: ministers confirmed to the Commons that Britain rejoins the Erasmus exchange scheme in January.

Dive deeper

The wider context is a run of incidents in Britain that police have attributed to Iranian direction, and the timing sits oddly alongside this week’s diplomacy, with Gulf states in Tehran pressing for a deal between Iran and Washington. A third man arrested in October, a dual national, was released without charge. Because these are live proceedings we are reporting the charges, the court dates and nothing more.

Western Powers Warn of Famine in Sudan as Nyala Is Struck
