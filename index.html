UAE Says the Flydubai Co-Pilot Attempted a Terrorist Act

The United Arab Emirates’ prosecutor general has said the co-pilot of Wednesday’s Dubai to Tel Aviv flight “attempted to carry out a terrorist act”, using a crash axe against the captain inside the cockpit and trying to take control of the aircraft. Hamad Saif al-Shamsi said the investigation continues into “the circumstances of the incident, its motives, and related connections”.

Dive deeper
This is the first official characterisation from an authority that actually holds the case. Everything said about motive until now came from Israeli ministers: the prime minister described “Islamist radical indoctrination” and called the attacker suicidal, the defence minister called it an “attempted jihadist terror attack”, and two Israeli security sources said they had not found Iranian involvement. The Emirates claims jurisdiction because the aircraft is registered there, and the suspect was transferred from Saudi Arabia to Abu Dhabi on Thursday along with the injured captain, the Indian national Smit Machchhar. Saudi Arabia’s own preliminary finding said only that there had been “an assault on the captain by the co-pilot”. So the sequence has run from Israeli assertion to Saudi description to an Emirati legal conclusion — and the crash axe, a tool carried in every cockpit, is the first physical detail any authority has confirmed. Final results will follow, Mr al-Shamsi said. Watch whether “related connections” produces a state.

Russia Strikes a Second Major Bridge in Kyiv

A Russian strike on Saturday morning damaged the road surface and the trolleybus overhead wires on Kyiv’s Northern Bridge, the mayor said — the second major crossing to be hit in days. The Southern Bridge has been targeted repeatedly since Thursday, with all metro and vehicle traffic suspended across it. Vitali Klitschko has said such attacks are “tearing Kyiv apart”.

Dive deeper
Kyiv sits on both banks of the Dnipro with only a handful of crossings, so attacking them is an attempt to cut a city of three million in half rather than merely to darken it. The Northern Bridge carries trolleybuses as well as cars, which is why damage to overhead wires matters as much as damage to the road. Mr Klitschko told the city’s defence council this week that “we’re talking about survival”. The defensive problem is specific: an acute shortage of Patriot interceptors, against a newer generation of jet-powered drones that are harder to bring down. About half a million people commute from the left bank to the right every day, which is the number that makes this an economic attack as well as a military one. On the ground the war is moving the other way: Putin told the Valdai forum that Russia seized 1,301 square kilometres in September, while the Institute for the Study of War assesses a net Russian loss of 80.81 — a gap it describes as so far from reality as to resemble hallucination. Watch whether a third crossing is hit, because two is an inconvenience and three begins to look like a siege.

Riyadh is preparing an offensive against the Houthis, weighing either a coastal push to secure the Red Sea shipping route or an assault on several fronts at once, regional and Western officials have said. Dive deeper
The logic runs through the chokepoints. With Hormuz effectively closed by the war on Iran, Bab el-Mandeb is the alternative route, and the Houthis took it last month — so Riyadh has concluded it cannot return to negotiations while they hold it. The former American ambassador Michael Ratney framed the realistic objective modestly: “Ideally, it would be a fairly limited operation to retake coastal areas and get back to normal maritime traffic.” Whether the ground forces are capable of it is the open question. Estimates of timing run from within a week to after the American midterms. Watch Saudi oil infrastructure.

G7 Unlocks 100 Million Barrels and Rules Out Export Bans

The Group of Seven will release up to 100 million barrels of emergency crude and diesel reserves over four months, coordinated by the International Energy Agency, with “a frontloaded substantial diesel release within the first 20 days”. In return the leaders committed to “refrain from export restrictions on energy and energy products between G7 countries” — which kills the American export ban Donald Trump had been threatening all week.

Dive deeper
That reciprocal clause is the deal. Mr Trump posted that Europe had “just agreed to release a massive amount of their heavily stocked Diesel Oil” moments before the statement appeared, but Emmanuel Macron, chairing the meeting, set out both halves: “We have all committed together to releasing these strategic reserves in the proportions I mentioned, with a focus on diesel, and we are all committed to ensuring there are no export bans, and President Trump, in particular, was very clear on this point.” Ursula von der Leyen welcomed “the decision of G7 countries not to impose any export bans on allies”. So Europe supplied barrels and Washington withdrew a threat, each able to present it as a win. The scepticism is in the small print: the statement also “requests the IEA to monitor the immediate and full implementation of the March 2026 commitments”, a 400 million-barrel release that officials concede has still not been completed. Watch whether the twenty-day diesel tranche actually moves.

Dive deeper

Spain's Right Sinks Sánchez's Housing Decrees

Congress rejected both of the government’s emergency housing decrees, the conservative People’s Party, Vox and the Catalan pro-independence Junts voting them down. Dive deeper
Pedro Sánchez put it as a plea rather than an argument: “I don’t ask you, I beg you to be brave and consistent.” The People’s Party answer was the orthodox one, from its lawmaker Juan Bravo: “Housing doesn’t become more affordable through decrees; it becomes more affordable by building more homes.” Because the decrees were already operative and required ratification, the votes do not block something prospective — they remove protections that existed this morning. The trigger was the eviction of Maricarmen Abascal, 87, a wheelchair user, from the Madrid flat she had lived in for seventy years, after the investment firm that bought the building raised her rent from €500 to €2,650 against a pension of €1,350. Congo's Ebola Epidemic Passes 4,000 Deaths

The World Health Organisation warns the epidemic is “far from over” and on course to surpass the 2014 to 2016 West African outbreak, which killed more than eleven thousand people.

Dive deeper
What makes this one so hard to stop is that it is being fought inside a war. The United Nations reported on Friday that soldiers searching for weapons burned a camp housing a transit centre for infected patients on the outskirts of Bunia, the capital of Ituri province and the epicentre of the outbreak, forcing nineteen thousand people to flee. Displacing that many people from an epicentre is the opposite of containment, and it was done by the state’s own forces. The medical problem is as unforgiving: this is the Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment, with several still in trials — so the tools that eventually brought earlier Congolese outbreaks under control do not exist here. It is the country’s seventeenth outbreak and was declared in May. Watch whether the trial vaccines are authorised for emergency use, because nothing else is likely to change the trajectory.

Burnham Will Legislate to End the Triple Lock This Parliament

The government will pass legislation scrapping the state pension triple lock during this parliament, even though the guarantee itself remains in place until 2030. Guidance published alongside the Prime Minister’s conference speech shows MPs will be required to vote on the change before the election. In future the earnings element will be adjusted to avoid very large increases in years when wages spike.

Dive deeper
The timing is the whole of it. Keeping the guarantee until 2030 honours the manifesto; legislating now puts the change on the statute book before anyone has voted on it, and obliges every party to say whether it would keep Labour’s version or repeal it. That converts an announcement into a dividing line, and forces the Conservatives and Reform — both of whom spent this week promising to protect the lock — to show how a reversal would be paid for. It also means a Commons vote in a parliament that has already seen a welfare rebellion. The mechanism smooths rather than severs: the pension still rises by prices or 2.5%, and the earnings link returns only if its value falls behind. The earnings element becomes a floor instead: officials put it at no less than 30% of average earnings, and the saving at about £15bn a year by the end of the 2030s and £50bn by 2050 — but the Resolution Foundation’s Ruth Curtice has shown how conditional that is: it “saves most in a volatile world, and so saves nothing in the 90s or 00s, and £24bn in the 10s”. Since the care offer is costed against it, that range matters. Watch when the bill is introduced.

Dive deeper

Thousands of potentially relevant files have been identified, including images from operations under investigation. The ministry’s counsel said it “apologises that it fell short” and called the failure “one of communication, not application”.

Dive deeper
The inquiry has now run for nearly four years, cost £58m and published no findings, with its evidence deadline set at April 2027 — and it has just been told the department under investigation was sitting on a document archive of almost a hundred million items. Whether that is obstruction or institutional chaos matters less, for now, than the consequence: every conclusion reached so far has to be re-tested against material nobody has read. Families of Afghan civilians who have waited years for findings are the ones who absorb the delay. A further hearing is set for next month. Watch whether the chairman moves the deadline, and whether anyone at the ministry is held responsible for a disclosure failure on this scale.

Kyle Says the Government Is Not Acting With Enough Urgency on Growth

Peter Kyle, the business secretary sacked when Sir Keir Starmer left office, broke cover as delegates travelled to Liverpool. Asked whether there is enough focus on growth, he said: “I don’t think there is… I think we need to up our ambition, I think that we need to act with more urgency when it comes to economic growth.” He suggested delaying parts of the Employment Rights Act and applying a “tax growth test”. The OECD cut its forecast for next year this week to 1%.

Dive deeper
The specific proposal is the awkward part. Kyle wants “honest conversations with unions about how we sequence some of the rights going forward” — in other words, slowing a flagship act passed only last December, some of whose provisions do not take effect until next year. That is a direct invitation to the fight the leadership least wants at a conference where Unison already plans one demonstration and the unions are pressing for a bank windfall tax. He is not alone in the discontent: Darren Jones told the New Statesman this week that Starmer should have been given more time. Discontent from the sacked is easy to dismiss; discontent that names a policy is harder. Watch whether anyone on the platform answers him.

Burnham Pledges £210m to Reopen Boarded-Up High Streets

The Prime Minister will announce £210m for high streets: £125m for councils to tackle derelict buildings, £65m for communities to rescue at-risk pubs and clubs, and £20m split between co-operative ownership and council-run rental auctions. The money is redirected from existing regeneration budgets and sits on top of the £5.8bn pride-in-place programme. “People don’t measure growth by looking at a spreadsheet,” he said. “They measure it by looking at their local high street. Are shops opening or are the shutters down?”

Dive deeper
The quote is the strategy: a government whose macroeconomic numbers are poor, offering voters a metric they can see from the bus. On the substance, £210m redirected rather than new is modest against the scale of empty units, and the co-operative element is the one genuinely novel piece — Labour pledged to double co-operatives, and the number grew by 0.4% last year to 8,005, which is not doubling. The pride-in-place programme behind it is the real money, giving 284 communities up to £2m a year for a decade. A full high streets strategy is promised later this year, which is where business rates, the actual lever, would have to appear. Watch whether the strategy arrives before the Budget or after it.

Labour Members Move to Cancel Palantir's £330m NHS Contract

An emergency motion submitted by the Socialist Health Association and at least three constituency parties asks the health department to trigger the break clause in Palantir’s Federated Data Platform contract. A conference committee decides today whether it is debated. The motion cites the appointment of the Labour peer Tom Watson as a senior vice-president at Palantir UK. Both the science and health select committees, the British Medical Association and Unison have called for the February 2027 termination clause to be used.

Dive deeper
This is the kind of motion that is usually kept off the floor, which is why today’s committee decision matters more than the motion itself. The coalition behind it is unusually broad for a conference emergency motion — Momentum and the Palestine Solidarity Campaign alongside two parliamentary select committees, the doctors’ union and the largest public-sector union. Lord Watson’s own description of his new employer does the opposition’s work for it: “Palantir is the most interesting company on the planet, doing work of real consequence.” The department’s defence is operational: “The benefits of the FDP service are clear, with thousands more patients benefitting every month.” The underlying question is who holds NHS patient data and on what terms. Watch whether it reaches the floor.
