UAE Halts All Trade With Iran Over Missile Strike

The United Arab Emirates has halted all trade, commercial and financial dealings with Iran indefinitely, a dramatic escalation after it accused Tehran of firing two ballistic missiles at its territory. Dubai has quietly become Iran’s most important trading partner, supplying around a third of its imports, so the embargo may bite harder than years of US sanctions. Iran denied launching the missiles, calling the episode a “false flag”.

Dive deeper
The move turns a military incident into economic warfare and closes one of the last big openings in Iran’s battered economy, which is already shrinking and gripped by inflation near 70 per cent. For decades Dubai has been the discreet conduit through which sanctioned Iranian goods and money flowed; severing it, one former US general said, could hurt more than anything Washington has done. The rupture also signals that Gulf states once wary of confronting Tehran are now willing to act, hardening the regional coalition against it. Iran’s denial, and its charge of a “false flag”, leaves the facts contested even as the consequences mount. Watch whether other Gulf states follow and how Tehran retaliates.

Israeli Strikes Kill Gazans as Ceasefire Push Falters

At least 10 Palestinians were killed in Israeli strikes across Gaza on Wednesday, hospitals and the Palestinian Red Crescent said, two days after US negotiators urged Israel to scale back its attacks. Israeli troops also crossed the ceasefire line in southern Gaza at dawn and detained a Hamas police colonel. The strikes cast fresh doubt on the American-led effort to secure a truce, which President Trump has pressed Israel to support.

Dive deeper
The killings come just days after Jared Kushner asked Benjamin Netanyahu “not to create obstacles” to a deal, and they underline how little the diplomacy has changed on the ground. Israel says its strikes target Hamas commanders, including one linked to the 7 October 2023 attack, but the mounting civilian toll and a fresh ground incursion suggest a military campaign running well ahead of any ceasefire. For Washington, the gap between its public appeals and Israel’s continued operations is becoming harder to explain. Israel’s military separately said it would open criminal inquiries into some troop conduct during the war. Watch whether the US applies real pressure or the strikes go on.

Ukraine Approves New Defence Minister Amid Reshuffle Crisis

Ukraine’s parliament approved Yevhenii Khmara as defence minister, with 312 votes, filling a post left vacant since the popular Mykhailo Fedorov was ousted in July. Mr Khmara, a special-forces major general, had served as acting minister and says Russia will have “no safe zones”. The vote came hours after Mr Fedorov called for wartime elections, hinting at political ambitions and exposing tensions at the top of Kyiv’s war effort.

Dive deeper
The reshuffle lays bare a domestic strain Kyiv has largely kept hidden, as anti-corruption agencies probe figures close to the presidency and a sacked, popular minister positions himself as a rival. President Zelensky’s move to install a trusted security officer signals a desire for control at the defence ministry at a perilous moment, with Russian barrages intensifying and no ceasefire in sight. Mr Fedorov’s call for elections, constitutionally barred under martial law, is a pointed challenge that touches the legitimacy question Moscow likes to exploit. For a country at war, open political rivalry is both a luxury and a risk. Watch how Mr Zelensky manages the discontent and whether the graft inquiries widen.

Trump Pauses 50% Canada Tariffs, Citing a Deal

President Trump paused threatened 50 per cent tariffs on Canadian goods for three days, about 90 minutes before they were due to take effect, saying the two sides had reached a deal in principle. The tariffs would have hit dozens of products. Canada’s prime minister, Mark Carney, said “substantial progress” had been made but that important work remained. Dive deeper
The last-minute reprieve fits a now-familiar pattern in which Mr Trump sets a punishing tariff deadline, extracts concessions and then pauses at the brink, keeping trading partners perpetually off balance. For Canada, whose economy is deeply tied to the United States, even a three-day extension is a relief, though the lack of a signed agreement leaves businesses in limbo. The revival of Keystone XL, floated without detail, hints at what Washington may be seeking in return. A durable deal would steady the continent’s largest trading relationship; failure by Friday would reopen the threat. Watch whether the documents are finalised and what Canada gives up in exchange.

Up to 100 Feared Dead in Central African Gold-Mine Collapse

As many as 100 people are feared dead after an illegal gold mine collapsed in the Central African Republic, officials and local sources said, though only about 30 bodies had been recovered by Wednesday and authorities cautioned that the toll was not yet confirmed. The landslide, blamed on the collapse of underground tunnels, struck the Zamboye mine near the Cameroon border. A judicial inquiry has been opened.

Dive deeper
Artisanal mining, largely unregulated and often illegal, draws thousands of desperate labourers into unstable pits across central Africa, where collapses are common and death tolls hard to establish. That the recovered dead number about 30 while local estimates run to 100 reflects both the scale of the disaster and the difficulty of counting victims in a remote area with little official presence. Several of the dead were Cameroonian, underscoring how such mines pull in migrant workers from across the region. The tragedy will renew questions about oversight of a trade that enriches armed groups and traders while the diggers bear the risk. Watch whether the final toll is established and what enforcement follows.

Home Office Issues Conduct Guide for Asylum Seekers

The Home Office has published a guide setting out how asylum seekers are expected to behave while living in the UK, covering respect in public, sex and consent, gender equality and domestic abuse. The nine-page booklet, “Understanding behaviours and expectations in the UK”, directs people to support services and stresses what is expected of them. It lands amid a fraught national debate over asylum accommodation and small-boat crossings.

Dive deeper
The guide is a small document with an outsized political charge, published as ministers try to show they are gripping a system that dominates the news and drives support for Reform UK. Supporters will see a common-sense statement of British norms; critics will call it tokenistic, or an implicit slur that casts asylum seekers as a behavioural problem. Either reading underlines how far migration has moved to the centre of politics, with the government caught between a public demanding control and campaigners warning against stigma. The practical effect of a booklet is limited; the signal it sends is the point. Watch how it is received and whether it forms part of a wider package on asylum accommodation.

Watchdog Probes Trainline and Virgin Atlantic Over Hidden Fees

The competition watchdog opened investigations into Trainline, Virgin Atlantic Holidays and a driving school over “drip pricing”, where mandatory fees are added late in the booking process. The Competition and Markets Authority pointed to booking fees of up to £2.79 on train tickets and resort charges on holidays that can run to hundreds of pounds. It said the first price a customer sees “should be the price they pay”.

Dive deeper
The cases are among the first under new powers that let the regulator rule on breaches itself and impose fines of up to a tenth of a company’s global turnover, without going to court. Drip pricing, the trickle of compulsory fees revealed only at checkout, is estimated to cost consumers billions a year and has become a priority target, after the AA was fined £4.2m in April in the first such penalty. For shoppers, the practical effect could be clearer headline prices on some of the most-used booking sites. For the firms, the threat of large fines and forced refunds is a powerful incentive to change. Watch which companies settle and whether the crackdown widens.

HMRC Sends 81,000 Crypto Tax Warning Letters

HM Revenue and Customs sent more than 81,000 letters over the past year warning cryptocurrency holders they may owe capital gains tax, a figure that has almost tripled since 2024, according to figures obtained by the BBC. Investors risk fines or prosecution for undeclared profits, including from swapping one cryptocurrency for another. From 2027, overseas crypto platforms will be required to share UK customer data with the tax authorities.

Dive deeper
Crypto investors have become the tax authority’s single biggest target for capital gains enforcement, overtaking shares and property, as HMRC uses platform data to chase gains that many holders do not realise are taxable. The surge in “nudge” letters, from under 28,000 two years ago to more than 81,000, reflects both the spread of crypto ownership and a Treasury hunting for revenue ahead of a difficult autumn budget. Coming data-sharing rules will make undeclared gains far harder to hide. For the many Britons who bought crypto casually, the message is that selling, or even swapping, can trigger a tax bill. Watch how much the crackdown raises and whether small investors are caught out.

UK House-Price Growth Halves to 2% as Market Cools

Annual house-price growth in the UK slowed to 2 per cent in the year to June, down sharply from 3 per cent the month before, official figures showed, leaving the average home at £272,000. Prices barely moved over the month. The North West led with growth of 4.7 per cent while London lagged at 2.5 per cent; Northern Ireland continued to rise fastest over the quarter.

Dive deeper
The cooling reflects a market losing the momentum it drew from last year’s rush to beat stamp-duty changes, as higher borrowing costs and stretched affordability weigh on buyers. For homeowners, near-flat prices mean little of the paper wealth of recent years; for would-be buyers, it offers only modest relief when mortgage rates remain high and gilt yields sit near multi-year peaks. The regional gap, a buoyant North against a sluggish London and South, continues a slow rebalancing of the housing map. With the Bank of England’s next rate decision weeks away, the direction of prices will hinge on whether borrowing costs finally ease. Watch the autumn budget and the mortgage market for the next signal.

Dive deeper
The reshuffle follows sustained anger over the downgrading of services at Causeway Hospital, one of a series of painful decisions forced by a health system that is chronically underfunded and overstretched. Northern Ireland’s waiting lists are proportionally the worst in the United Kingdom, and successive ministers have found the politics of closing or centralising services almost impossible, with every change resisted locally. Installing a new minister signals a desire for fresh momentum, but the underlying problems — money, workforce and a divided Executive — will not shift with a change of face. Watch who is appointed and whether they can carry reforms the last minister could not.

Israel carried out eight air strikes on the Abu al-Duhur airbase in Syria’s Idlib province, saying the operation was intended to stop Turkey deploying troops there; Syrian media reported no casualties. Dive deeper
The strikes open a perilous new front, setting Israel against Turkey, a NATO member with the alliance’s second-largest army, over the future of post-war Syria. Israel casts the move as pre-emption against a Turkish foothold on its northern flank; Ankara sees a bid to legitimise unlawful bombing and keep Syria weak and divided. The rare American rebuke is the more striking element, suggesting Washington’s patience with Israeli unilateralism is fraying even as it shields it elsewhere. For a Syrian government still finding its feet after the fall of Assad, becoming the arena for an Israel-Turkey contest is the last thing it needs. The strike lands amid one of the war’s deadliest recent stretches, with Russian barrages killing scores across Ukraine and no diplomatic track in motion. Zambia's Hichilema Wins Second Term Amid Observer Concerns

Dive deeper

The observers’ concerns, over rushed legal changes and a tilted playing field, echo a wider pattern across the region, where incumbents increasingly bend the rules of the contest without openly breaking them. For Zambia, long seen as one of Africa’s more stable democracies, the critique reads as a caution rather than a crisis. Watch whether the opposition mounts a legal challenge and how Mr Hichilema handles the debt-and-copper economy in his second term.

Inflation Climbs to 2.9% as Energy Bills Jump

Inflation rose to 2.9 per cent in the year to July, up from 2.6 per cent and its highest since March, official figures showed. The increase was driven almost entirely by the 13 per cent rise in the energy price cap on 1 July, which added £221 to the typical annual bill. Food inflation eased to 1.3 per cent and petrol prices fell, but the figures complicate the Bank of England’s path to further interest-rate cuts.

Dive deeper
The jump is uncomfortable but largely mechanical, the direct result of a single step-change in energy bills rather than broad-based price pressure, and services inflation actually eased. That distinction matters to the Bank of England, which cut Bank Rate to 3.75 per cent last month and must now judge whether the rise is a one-off bump or the start of something stickier. For households the relief in food and fuel is real but is being swamped by heating and power costs. The Chancellor, John Healey, pinned part of the pressure on the Iran war’s effect on energy prices. Watch the Bank’s next decision and whether inflation falls back as the cap’s effect washes through.

Burnham Pledges Route Off Streets for Rough Sleepers by Christmas

The prime minister, Andrew Burnham, launched a national drive to offer every rough sleeper “a route off the streets” by Christmas, backed by an extra £102m for medical care, addiction support and charities. He called for a “Covid-style” winter campaign and a first-of-its-kind national summit in the autumn. Homelessness charities welcomed the money but questioned whether the timescale and funding matched the scale of the problem.

Dive deeper
The pledge revives the “Everyone In” approach of the pandemic, when emergency action briefly cleared the streets, and stakes the government’s credibility on a visible, hard-to-fake outcome by a fixed date. Rough sleeping has risen sharply in recent years, and Mr Burnham’s framing, that it has come to “feel like part of the scenery”, is a deliberate bid to make it politically unacceptable again. The difficulty is that the causes, from addiction and mental illness to a chronic shortage of housing, outlast any winter blitz. The extra money is modest against a total programme of £442m. Watch whether councils can hit the Christmas target and what happens to those housed once the campaign ends.

The hospitality entries, from festival tickets to a day at the races, are the kind of small detail that tends to travel further than the six-figure sums. None of it breaches the rules. Watch whether the opposition presses the point and how the government handles questions about its donors.

Davey Urges UK to Join EU Migration Pact on Small Boats

The Liberal Democrat leader, Sir Ed Davey, called for the UK to join the European Union’s Migration and Asylum Pact, arguing that being outside it leaves Britain “vulnerable to traffickers” and unable to return small-boat arrivals. Dive deeper
The intervention stakes out clear Liberal Democrat territory, unashamedly pro-European, on an issue the government would rather handle quietly and Reform exploits loudly. Rejoining the pact would indeed restore practical tools lost after Brexit, chiefly shared biometric data and a mechanism to return asylum seekers to the first safe country they entered. But it would also mean accepting EU rules and a share of the bloc’s own asylum burden, a hard sell to voters told for years that leaving meant taking back control. With small-boat crossings still high, the politics of migration remain treacherous for every party. Watch whether Labour engages with the idea or steers clear of anything that reopens the Brexit argument.

Demand for Degree Apprenticeships Quadruples in Three Years

Demand for degree apprenticeships in England has quadrupled in three years, according to official figures, as school leavers weigh the cost of university against earning while they learn. Around 40 per cent of undergraduate applicants, some 430,000 people, now say they are interested in apprenticeships, a number projected to pass half a million by 2030. Rising tuition costs and strong employer demand are driving the shift.

Dive deeper
The surge reflects a quiet rethinking of the post-18 choice, as higher fees, student debt and a tighter graduate job market push more young people towards routes that pay from day one and end without a loan. Degree apprenticeships, which combine a full degree with paid work, remain scarce relative to demand, and competition for places is fierce. For the government the trend is convenient, easing pressure on student finance while feeding employers’ skills needs, but supply lags far behind interest. Whether the places materialise depends on employers willing to fund them. Watch whether ministers expand the programme and how universities respond to the shift.

Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan Sign the “Mecca Pact”

Geopolitical

The agreement recasts a region already convulsed by the US–Iran war and the Hormuz blockade, offering Riyadh a security guarantee beyond Washington and giving Ankara its deepest foothold yet in Gulf affairs. Analysts called it the most significant realignment since the Abraham Accords — and one pointed squarely away from the American-led order.

Whether tougher sanctions bite quickly enough to matter on the battlefield is another question — but after months of congressional paralysis, the legislative logjam in Washington has at last broken.
