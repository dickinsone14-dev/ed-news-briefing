GEO Geopolitical

Britain Keeps Its Jerusalem Building and Loses Most of Its Staff

The consulate general in East Jerusalem has not closed. From today it is the UK Mission, Jerusalem, after talks with Israel. But twenty diplomats and the Consul General are ending their assignments and leaving, with a task force of seven permitted to remain on what Israel calls “a very limited scope of activity”.

Dive deeper
Ed Miliband’s statement concedes nothing on the law and a great deal on the ground. “We have been clear that we do not accept that the Government of Israel has the legal right to close the British Consulate General in Jerusalem, which has been there for nearly 200 years,” he said, restating that East Jerusalem is occupied territory. The building stays, renamed, “independent, operational, and will continue to deliver vital services and our work with the Palestinian people”. The reason for the departures is bluntly put: “Without an understanding, we would not have the necessary assurances for the security and protections of our staff and premises.” Britain has “temporarily withdrawn a number of staff and families” and will “work in the coming months to build back up our presence”. Crucially, it “will not take reciprocal action while such discussions are underway and credible” — so the twenty-seven Israeli diplomats that Israeli media reported Britain might expel have not been expelled, and that figure was never confirmed. Gideon Saar, who ordered the closure on 8 September in answer to Britain’s sanctions on settlements and its ban on settlement goods, said: “We did not want it to come to this,” and “no action can be taken against Israel without Israel taking appropriate retaliatory measures.” Workers removed the “British Consulate General” plaque this morning. The union flag was still flying. The embassy in Tel Aviv is untouched; what has been thinned is the mission that serves Palestinians in East Jerusalem, the West Bank and Gaza.

Pakistani Forces Are in Saudi Arabia, and Three Die at Two Airports

“The forces of Pakistan are already there,” the chief of the Pakistani military’s media wing has confirmed, declining to give numbers. It follows a mutual defence treaty signed with Saudi Arabia and Turkey. Houthi missile and drone strikes killed two at Abha airport and one at King Khalid in Riyadh, wounding thirty-six.

Dive deeper
Lieutenant General Ahmed Sharif Chaudhry gave the confirmation in an interview, treating numbers and capabilities as classified. A nuclear-armed state has put soldiers on Saudi soil in a war Britain sits adjacent to through the Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz; that is a structural change in Gulf security rather than another day of exchanges. The casualties come from Saudi Arabia’s General Authority of Civil Aviation: two women, a Moroccan and an Algerian, killed with twenty-eight wounded at Abha International near the Yemeni border, and a Sudanese national killed with eight wounded at King Khalid International in Riyadh, on 6 and 7 October. The Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree claimed both, calling them a response to sixty Saudi air strikes and missile attacks in twenty-four hours, and also claimed a strike on Aden International. The Saudi-led coalition said the attacks “will not go unpunished” and reported strikes on more than eighty Houthi military sites in Saada, Al Hudaydah, Al Jawf and Ma’rib. The American mission in Saudi Arabia has issued an updated security alert warning the conflict “has the potential to escalate”. António Guterres condemned the cross-border attacks and said he was deeply concerned by the sharp escalation. The United Nations now counts more than 200,000 people displaced by the fighting in Yemen.

The Pensioner Whose Eviction Brought Down Spain’s Housing Truce Has Died

María del Carmen Abascal, known as Maricarmen, has died in hospital at 87, a fortnight after being carried on a stretcher from the Madrid flat she had lived in for more than seventy years while police teargassed protesters outside. Her landlord had sought to raise her rent from €500 a month to €2,650.

Dive deeper
Her lawyer Beatriz Duro did not give a cause of death, saying the case “leaves us the image of the shameful violence of these types of evictions and the impact they can have on people’s health”. The Madrid Tenants’ Union said she died reportedly hours before she was due to sign a new lease: “Although a historic agreement was reached that would have allowed her to return home, this solution came too late. It was an entirely avoidable situation following a harrowing ordeal.” She used a wheelchair, had worked as a secretary for a pharmaceutical company board, and both her parents died in that flat. Pedro Sánchez wrote: “All my love to Maricarmen’s family and loved ones. A hug as well to all of us who feel this loss as our own today.” Protesters outside the Spanish parliament chanted “Maricarmen lives, the fight goes on!” and those camped in Puerta del Sol held a minute’s silence. The politics had already moved: Sánchez called a snap general election on Monday, for November, after right-wing and far-right parties voted down two emergency housing decrees. The numbers behind the anger will be familiar to a British reader. Spanish rents have roughly doubled in a decade and house prices are up about 63%; the government says 26% of Spanish renters spend an average of 40% of their income on rent and utilities, against a European Union average of 18%.

Lecornu Offers France’s Schools €64bn and the Students Say No

In his address to the nation, Sébastien Lecornu said parliament would debate a budget on 12 November containing €64bn for education, a slight increase, along with school renovation and better timetables. The main student association says he did not answer their demands. Hundreds of schools remain blockaded and more protests were called for today.

Dive deeper
“We have a duty to listen to them,” Mr Lecornu said. “Taking young people seriously means supporting their engagement just as much as sharing the difficulties, but also the constraints, with them.” Anaïs Ella, speaking for Les Lycéens, was unimpressed: one of his proposals, filling teaching posts in exam-year classes, “was the basics of the education system”. Money would meet some demands, she said, but what is needed is a reset between government and students. On policing, the interior minister Laurent Nuñez, himself a former chief of the Paris police, confirmed he had ordered forces to stop using the type of stun grenade linked to a fifteen-year-old losing his hand, after an emergency ministerial meeting at which Emmanuel Macron demanded “empathy” and more “pedagogy”: “We don’t deal with a movement of highschoolers like we deal with a movement of adults.” Eighteen investigations into possible police brutality are under way, including one into a fourteen-year-old whose jaw was shattered. The genuinely new element arrived on Wednesday evening, and it connects this to the wider war: the French foreign ministry summoned the Iranian ambassador over what it called a “disinformation campaign conducted by the Iranian authorities” about the protests, saying misleading content put out online by official Iranian accounts was “absolutely unacceptable”. Tehran had summoned the French ambassador the day before, alleging “violent treatment” of protesters; Paris called that baseless.

Congress Counts Eighty-One American Aircraft Lost in the Iran War

A Congressional Research Service report released this week puts the number of American aircraft destroyed or damaged at eighty-one, against forty-two in the same service’s count six months ago. Replacing them would cost between $1.9bn and $3.3bn on Congressional Budget Office figures. More than half are MQ-9 Reaper drones.

Dive deeper
The losses are not confined to drones. The list includes an F-35A and an E-3 Sentry, the airborne early warning aircraft that are few in number, old, and in demand everywhere at once. Eight F-15E Strike Eagles lightly damaged in an Iranian strike on Muwaffaq Salti air base in Jordan in September may be back in service. Sources put the total cost of the war to the United States at at least $45bn, before counting damage to base infrastructure. The report’s sharpest observation is about its own subject: Pentagon reporting on aircraft losses “has, to date, been vague”, to the point that the Department of Defence’s own inspector general was citing earlier figures from this same congressional service in September rather than departmental ones. The question it puts to Congress is whether losses in “high-demand platforms that are aging and limited in number, such as the E-3 Sentry, create capability gaps or increase risk in other theaters” — which is the polite form of asking what is left for a crisis somewhere else. For a British reader the relevance is direct: these are the capabilities the United Kingdom assumes will be available to an ally, and the doubling of the count in six months is the clearest public measure yet of what this war is consuming.

UK Domestic

Holborn and St Pancras Votes in Starmer’s Old Seat

Polls opened at seven this morning and close at ten tonight, when counting begins. Fifteen candidates are standing for the seat Keir Starmer vacated after being forced out as prime minister by his own MPs in July. Labour’s Sagal Abdi-Wali is defending a majority of 11,500 against the Green leader Zack Polanski.

Dive deeper
The contest tests two things at once. For Labour it is whether Andy Burnham’s government can hold inner-city seats against a party that won several London councils in May; a narrow win would end the honeymoon rather than confirm it. For the Greens it is whether Mr Polanski can still take votes from Labour’s left after his party voted to define Zionism as racism, a policy four of his five MPs condemned and on which he has not said how he voted. The arithmetic has moved a long way in eight months. After February’s Gorton and Denton by-election YouGov briefly put the Greens above Labour on 21%; the most recent equivalent poll has them on 11%, sixteen points behind. Sir Keir took nearly 28,000 votes in 2019 and 11,500 in 2024. The seat is young and international: roughly 45% of constituents were born overseas and 48% are aged between eighteen and thirty-four. Ms Abdi-Wali, 42, has led Camden council since May and arrived in London in the early 1990s speaking no English, having fled Somalia with her mother and four brothers. Mr Polanski calls it “looking close” and asks whether it is “time for a Green voice for Camden to push Labour to do better” on rent controls and water ownership. Voters need photo identification, and about thirty polling stations are open in churches and community centres.

Live Fuel Prices Arrive on Google Maps as Diesel Tops £2

From today Google Maps shows live petrol and diesel prices, drawn from the government’s Fuel Finder feed, which forecourts have been legally required to update within thirty minutes of any change since February. Diesel passed £2 a litre for the first time days ago and has since fallen about 2p. Ofcom puts Google Maps in the hands of 77% of British adults.

Dive deeper
The timing is not comfortable for the government. Brent crude is up about 3% this morning to $103 after a tanker was hit off Qatar, and diesel is more than 40% dearer than in late February. John Healey framed it as doing what can be done: “Getting the best price at the pump is now easier. This doesn’t fix everything; bills have gone up because of events in the Middle East. But we’re not powerless.” The AA puts a 55-litre diesel tank at about £110 and petrol at about £96, and its president Edmund King calls the change “a massive step forward”. His colleague Luke Bosdet is more interesting on why it might matter: publishing the data could “signal trouble for the fuel trade by revealing the forecourt pricing tactics which keep local prices artificially high in their full horror”. The Competition and Markets Authority warned retailers in August that many were still slow to pass on falls in wholesale diesel. Two caveats are worth carrying. The government’s claim of near-complete coverage is about 99% of fuel sold, not 99% of forecourts, and Clare Lafferty of PetrolPrices says some sites have never registered while others register and then do not update; her firm identified about 200 forecourts in September it believed had not updated diesel prices in time. The saving the scheme was sold on, when it launched in February, was £40 a year.

Badenoch’s Inheritance Tax Pledge Meets Its Costed Critics

Fewer than 5% of families currently pay inheritance tax, and Kemi Badenoch says her plan would cut that number by more than half. The Institute for Fiscal Studies says the biggest beneficiaries “would be those with the highest wealth and the most valuable homes”, mostly in London and the south-east. Reform says the plans touch only 2% of houses.

Dive deeper
Those two percentages are not measuring the same thing — estates that pay the tax against the housing stock — and neither has been independently verified, so both are worth holding lightly. Stuart Adam of the Institute for Fiscal Studies is precise about who gains: “These will mostly be people living in London and the south-east.” Tax Justice UK calls it “a massive inheritance tax giveaway to some of the wealthiest people in Britain… a reckless policy that will deprive public services of cash when they most need them”. Robert Jenrick, Reform’s finance spokesman, attacks from the same direction for opposite reasons: “The Tory party is looking after its own in Kensington, Reform is looking after the country.” Labour’s chair Bridget Phillipson says the party is “prioritising the wealthiest instead”. The argument with the widest reach is not about fairness but about housing supply: exempting the family home whatever its value gives older owners a reason to stay in large houses rather than downsize, which is where the stock for everyone else comes from. Mrs Badenoch also ruled out any arrangement with Reform — “to those who say ‘unite the right’, we are not uniting with that” — and rejected Suella Braverman’s remarks on ethnicity and Englishness, saying she did not want to explain to her son “that some people don’t think our black players are English”.

Burnham and Merz Ratify a Treaty Against Hybrid Attacks

Andy Burnham makes his first official visit to Germany today and meets Friedrich Merz for the first time, received with military honours at the Chancellery. The two will formally ratify the 2025 Kensington Treaty, which covers science, technology, defence, security and growth. Mr Burnham says the two countries are “joining forces to find, expose and disrupt” threats.

Dive deeper
His argument is that this is not a specialist concern: “Attacks on our infrastructure, our businesses and our democracies are not a distant threat. They reach into people’s homes and workplaces, and they are designed to make us weaker and more divided.” The immediate context is Leipzig-Halle airport, a freight hub used to supply Ukraine, where Germany last month accused Russia of launching a drone fitted with explosives; a senior Russian diplomat was summoned in London over it and Moscow denies involvement. Two complications sit underneath the ceremony. August Hanning, who ran Germany’s foreign intelligence service from 1998 to 2005, has been arrested on suspicion of espionage, treason and obtaining classified information for a foreign power, which raises an awkward question about the partner Britain is binding itself to. And Mr Merz is weak at home after poor approval ratings and regional setbacks. Running alongside all of this is a second European conversation. France’s finance minister Roland Lescure told the BBC “come back, anytime” and “come all the way”, after Mr Burnham said a second referendum was “possible” in a future manifesto and that Britain should consider the options. Mr Lescure’s practical point concerned Made in Europe, the scheme that could shut British supply chains out of EU public contracts: “Come back, and you’ll be at the table.”

Labour’s Special Needs Overhaul Is at Risk of Unravelling

The Institute for Government says the plan to replace most education, health and care plans with mainstream support by 2030 may be too optimistic, because schools and parents have an incentive to secure a plan before they are phased out. Several councils project that nearly one pupil in ten will hold one by 2029.

Dive deeper
The report’s author Amber Dellar names the trap precisely: “There’s a ‘use it before you lose it’ dynamic, as schools and parents have an incentive to secure EHCPs before they are phased out.” A reform designed to reduce reliance on the plans is, in the interval before it takes effect, giving every family a reason to apply for one. The number that should concern anyone with a child in an English state school, whether or not they have special needs, is what this does to budgets: the institute calculates that the extra costs could absorb around two-thirds of the schools funding uplift over this parliament. That is money that would otherwise reach every classroom. The Department for Education pushes back on the record and without hedging: “The figures in this report are speculative and there is no evidence that proposed reforms are driving additional EHCP demand... we are completely committed to the implementation timelines set out in the schools white paper.” The work was funded by the Nuffield Foundation. Both things can be true at once — the projection is a projection, and the incentive it describes is real and already visible in council forecasts. What would settle it is the next set of EHCP application figures, broken down by the year the reform was announced. Watch for those, and for whether the 2030 date starts to move.
