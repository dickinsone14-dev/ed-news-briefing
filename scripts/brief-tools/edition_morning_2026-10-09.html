Nobel Peace Prize Goes to Navi Pillay, and to the Courts

The Norwegian Nobel Committee has given the 2026 Peace Prize to Navi Pillay, 85, the South African judge who defended Nelson Mandela, helped establish rape as a component of genocide at the Rwanda tribunal, and served as United Nations human rights chief. Asked whether the award was a message to states attacking international courts, the committee chair said it was.

Dive deeper
Jørgen Watne Frydnes did not soften it: “This is definitely a message to those who are attacking, undermin[ing] the international system, the international rules-based system and the institutions.” His citation made the same argument at length — “The system of international law is under tremendous pressure, and its institutions are under attack. We see a shift towards power politics at the expense of legal frameworks… Whenever might overtakes right, it undermines stability, trust and peaceful coexistence.” The context is the United States sanctioning International Criminal Court staff after the court sought an arrest warrant for Benjamin Netanyahu. Pillay was unreachable when the committee rang; Frydnes appealed on air: “If you see a Norwegian number calling, please pick up.” Filmed at the Palace of Justice in Nuremberg, she joked, “If you clap a little bit louder I may share this prize with Trump,” then added the lawyer’s caveat: “being a lawyer and a judge I still want to verify this thing”. She is the first Black woman to have served on South Africa’s High Court and now sits as an ad hoc judge at the International Court of Justice in the Rohingya genocide case. The award is not uncontested: she chaired the United Nations inquiry that found Israel’s conduct in Gaza amounted to genocide, a report Israel called “scandalous”. No government had responded to the prize itself by this edition. There were 287 nominations; Donald Trump was among them.

Three Saudis Killed at Riyadh Airport as Britain Warns Travellers

Saudi Arabia’s civil aviation authority says three Saudi nationals were killed in two Houthi attacks on King Khalid International Airport on Thursday, the second striking a parked Saudia aircraft. The airline says one of its employees was among the dead. The Foreign Office updated its travel advice last night to warn against all but essential travel to Riyadh province.

Dive deeper
This is the first time the Houthi campaign against Saudi airports has killed people at Riyadh itself, and it changes what the war means for anyone with a reason to fly there. The Foreign Office now advises against all travel within ten kilometres of the Yemeni border and against all but essential travel to Riyadh, Aseer, Najran and Jizan provinces and to Taif. Its notice is explicit about the consequence: “Your travel insurance could be invalidated if you travel against advice.” It records a heightened threat to airports, military bases, ports and energy infrastructure across the kingdom, including Jeddah and Yanbu, as well as to Red Sea shipping. The Houthis say they used two cruise missiles and also claimed strikes at Najran and the Khamis Mushait airbase, which the Saudis have not confirmed; the coalition says it destroyed three rocket-launch platforms and that attacks on civilians “will be met with resolve”. Airlines have drawn their own conclusions: Air India has cancelled Riyadh flights to at least the weekend and Lufthansa has suspended them through 16 October. Multinationals in the King Abdullah Financial District told staff to work from home, and some schools were told to shelter in place with no public alert. Turkey is sending defensive and technical support and Pakistan has deployed troops; the mutual defence pact the two signed with Riyadh this summer has still not been ratified in Ankara.

Volodymyr Zelensky says Thursday’s strike on two public buses killed at least thirty-five, up from the thirty reported when it happened, and puts the two-day national toll at seventy-eight killed and 215 wounded. He accused the United States of being unwilling to help while pursuing economic ties with Moscow. Ukrainian negotiators fly to Florida today.

And then Putin will sit down at the negotiating table.” He wants expanded access in order to target Russian ballistic-missile launchers. One correction worth making plainly: this edition and others described the weapon on Thursday as a glide bomb, and the approved record does not support that — four sources give three different descriptions, so it is reported here simply as a Russian strike. Volker Türk, the United Nations human rights commissioner, said he was “appalled by the wave of attacks killing civilians across Ukraine over the past four days… The scale and manner of these attacks — killing and injuring people on buses, at their homes and work in different areas of the country — is reprehensible.” Talks begin in Florida today and tomorrow with Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, covering security guarantees and a post-war plan. Britain moved yesterday: Ed Miliband, on his first visit to Ukraine as Foreign Secretary, announced about forty new sanctions designations across Russian finance, oil and technology, £54m in energy and humanitarian funding and £6.3m for war-crimes accountability, telling Moscow “You cannot wait us out.”

Trump Rules Out an Iran Strike and Tehran Says It Will Reply

“We will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the midterm elections to be held in the United States on November 3rd,” Donald Trump wrote, saying discussions were productive. Iran’s foreign minister Abbas Araghchi says Tehran is reviewing the American response to its seven-day plan for reopening the Strait of Hormuz and will answer within days. Brent has fallen back below $103.

Dive deeper
The sequence is the interesting part. Brent closed Thursday up 4% at $104.28 on reports the White House had asked the Pentagon for strike options; it gave that back after the post and fell again this morning, which some analysts read as the point of the exercise. On Monday President Masoud Pezeshkian had called talks with Washington “futile”, accusing the United States of using negotiations as cover for an attack, so Araghchi’s undertaking to respond is a shift inside a week. JD Vance said on Tuesday that Iran must make a “meaningful” reduction in enrichment capacity, which reads as a retreat from the earlier demand that it abandon enrichment altogether. The underlying numbers have not improved: nine tankers were attacked in and around the strait in the past week, and throughput was 9.5 million barrels a day in the week to Tuesday, about 30% below the pre-war norm, with total Middle East flows near normal only because of pipeline workarounds. Ryan McKay of TD Securities explains why the price stays elevated anyway: the need for naval escorts, higher costs, logistical friction and “constant risk of being attacked” together “warrant a sticky risk premium to remain in pricing”. Vladimir Putin told Mr Pezeshkian on Thursday that Russia would “do everything” to help end the war.

Vehicles Torched in South Africa as a Court Ruling Meets the Street

At least twenty-four vehicles were set alight, fourteen in Durban and ten in Soweto, and foreign-owned shops looted as anti-immigration protests turned violent on Thursday. Police fired rubber bullets outside a refugee reception centre in Durban where protesters demanded it stop accepting asylum applications. The Department of Home Affairs now says it will change how it complies with a Constitutional Court ruling.

Dive deeper
The ruling, handed down in July, struck down restrictions that barred some people from seeking asylum because of how they entered the country or because they had missed immigration deadlines — meaning applications must now be accepted even from those who arrived without documents. Long queues followed at immigration centres. On Friday the department said staff had been “overwhelmed” and that “serious threats to public safety and order had emerged”, and that it would change its approach while still abiding by the court; it gave no details. A government spokesman, William Baloyi, said people had deliberately spread false claims that undocumented migrants would automatically be granted legal status, and that applications will still be assessed individually. A senior police officer, Xolani Fihla, reported clashes between South African and foreign nationals with no injuries; three foreign nationals were arrested over an alleged assault on a protester, which is what tipped Thursday’s demonstration over. Unemployment runs at roughly a third of the workforce, and researchers say the claims made against migrants are not supported by evidence. The xenophobic attacks of 2008 killed more than sixty people. The retired South African constitutional court justice Edwin Cameron, reacting to this morning’s Nobel award to his compatriot, named the same wound: his country has been “wrecked by anguish about cross-border migration” and by movements “distributing toxic untruths”.

Labour Holds Holborn and St Pancras With Its Majority Cut by 71%

Sagal Abdi-Wali took 12,292 votes to Zack Polanski’s 8,928, a Labour majority of 3,364 against the 11,572 Keir Starmer won in 2024. The swing from Labour to Green was 13.06 points. Turnout fell 15.5 points to 38.6%, the lowest at any English by-election this parliament.

Dive deeper
Chris Mason’s reading is the right one: this “falls in the column marked ‘relief’ for Downing Street rather than ‘jubilation’”. Ms Abdi-Wali, leader of Camden council and a child refugee from Somalia in the 1990s, used her declaration speech to answer a Reform UK spokesman who had questioned whether she was British: “I hope this moment also says something about who gets to belong… This is Camden, and this is Britain.” She won, she said, “not by making people afraid of each other, but by bringing people together”, and named homelessness as the thing that drove her into politics and Thames Water as a company that “failed residents up and down our constituency”. Two numbers are being spun in opposite directions and both are true. The Green share more than tripled, from 10.44% to 32.81%; the Green vote count did not, rising from 4,030 to 8,928, slightly more than double. The sharper comparison is that in 2024 the Greens and the pro-Gaza independent Andrew Feinstein together took 11,342 votes in this seat — 2,414 more than Mr Polanski managed alone. The Greens consolidated an existing bloc rather than growing one. One other result deserves recording: the Conservatives finished third on 1,922, beating Reform UK for the first time at an English by-election this parliament, though Reform and Restore Britain between them would have been ahead of them.

All ADHD and Autism Assessments to Be Regulated After Review

An independent review chaired by Professor Peter Fonagy has found that diagnosis has become “a very narrow pathway to finding support”, with referrals rising sharply while underlying prevalence stays broadly stable. The health secretary Yvette Cooper will make every diagnostic assessment regulated by the Care Quality Commission, whether NHS, private or self-funded.

Dive deeper
Fonagy is careful about what he is and is not saying: “Our findings show a system under immense strain, where diagnosis has increasingly become a very narrow pathway to finding support… Diagnosis remains essential and must be protected — what has to change is that it is no longer the only door through which help can be reached.” That distinction matters, because the review has been reported both as a warning about over-diagnosis and as a warning about system failure, and the reconcilable fact underneath is that diagnoses and referrals have risen steeply while the share of the population with these conditions has not. The numbers around it are stark: the proportion of sixteen to sixty-four-year-olds with a common mental disorder rose from 15.5% in 1993 to 22.6% in 2024, and children diagnosed with autism or ADHD before seventeen are around four times more likely to be out of education, employment and training between sixteen and twenty-four. Ms Cooper’s regulatory change is aimed squarely at the private market that has grown in the gap: assessments will be inspected “so people can be confident that assessments are high quality and that the NHS is not being overcharged by independent or private providers”. The review also recommends banning direct-to-public advertising of NHS-funded assessments. A Mental Health Strategy follows, alongside a £4bn package for special educational needs.

Awaab’s Law to Be Extended to England’s Private Renters

The government has opened a consultation on extending Awaab’s Law, which sets legal deadlines for landlords to fix severe damp and mould, from social housing to the private rented sector, temporary accommodation for homeless families, and housing for care leavers. It would more than double the homes covered, from about four million to nine million. The consultation closes on 18 December.

Dive deeper
The law is named for Awaab Ishak, who died in 2020 at the age of two after prolonged exposure to damp and mould in a Rochdale flat his parents had repeatedly complained about. England has eleven million private renters and around one in ten privately rented homes has what the housing health and safety system classes as a category one hazard — a risk capable of causing death or serious harm. Matthew Pennycook, the housing minister, puts the principle plainly: “Irrespective of tenure, all renters have the right to live in a safe and decent home… We’re now moving to extend it to private landlords so that England’s 11 million private renters can enjoy the same protections.” Holly Williamson of Generation Rent describes what the deadlines are meant to fix: “too many renters live in homes that are harmful to our health and often wait months for landlords to act.” The Renters’ Rights Act already raised the maximum fine for the worst offences to £7,000. The practical point for anyone affected is that this is a consultation, not a commencement — the duty does not exist for private tenants yet, and the window to respond runs to 18 December.

Tory Net Zero Costing Overstates the Bill by £479bn

Kemi Badenoch’s hundred-page conference document says the Climate Change Committee put the cost of reaching net zero at £957bn. The committee’s own figures total £478bn — half that. The apparent cause is a spreadsheet tally that counted the row of column totals as well as the costs. The Conservatives had not responded.

Dive deeper
The committee’s response, on the record, is drier than the error deserves: “We don’t recognise this number. The committee has previously offered to meet with Ms Badenoch and talk her through our advice. That offer is, of course, still open.” Its most recent estimate of the net cost is about £110bn, or roughly £4bn a year, which is 0.2% of GDP — a different order of magnitude from the figure in the document. The mistake was spotted by the research outfit Carbon Brief, and the number appears to have travelled into the document from a think tank report and a magazine article rather than from the committee itself. The reason this is more than a clerical matter is that the figure is doing work: it is the headline cost used to justify scrapping the 2050 target, and it is wrong by a factor of two. There is also an unfortunate symmetry with the document’s own framing. Mrs Badenoch’s foreword notes that “as an engineer” she is used to correctly diagnosing problems, and the text argues that “to fix a problem we must first have an accurate understanding of what is causing it”. This rests on one outlet’s reporting, but the committee’s quotation is on the record and the arithmetic is checkable against its published budget.
