GEO Geopolitical

At Least Thirty Killed as a Glide Bomb Hits Two Buses

A Russian aerial glide bomb struck two passenger buses on Yuvileina Street in Kramatorsk at ten this morning, about twenty kilometres from the front. The Donetsk regional prosecutor’s office puts the toll at thirty killed and eighteen wounded. All public transport in Kramatorsk and neighbouring Sloviansk has been suspended.

Dive deeper
Volodymyr Zelensky says the number may rise, because some of the dead cannot be identified from the severity of their injuries. Three more people were killed by shelling at Bilenke, a village in the same community, which is a separate incident and should not be added to the bus toll. Vadym Filashkin, who heads the Donetsk regional military administration, was explicit about why buses: “For them, a bus full of passengers is an attractive target precisely because a single strike can kill the maximum number of people.” He has suspended public transport across both towns and asked remaining civilians to leave. Moscow has not commented. The pattern matters as much as the strike. Volodymyr Fesenko of the Penta think tank reads it as a change of method: “They used to focus on depriving Ukraine of power, on switching off electricity and heating; now their task is also to immobilise the infrastructure, create a transportation collapse.” The state railway counted 521 locomotives hit since 2022, three-fifths of them this year alone, and trains carry almost two-thirds of Ukraine’s freight. United Nations figures record 15,280 civilians killed or injured in the first eight months of this year, 55% up on the same period last year. Ed Miliband, standing at Wednesday’s Pryluky strike site, called it “Putin’s evil, evil intent and strategy”.

Trump Rules Out Striking Iran Before the Midterms, and Oil Falls

Brent had climbed 5% to $105.30 by midday on a report that the White House had asked the Pentagon for options to strike Iran before the 3 November elections. Donald Trump then wrote that “we will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the Midterm Elections”, and the price gave back more than a dollar.

Dive deeper
The full post keeps the pressure on while removing the threat: Iran is “in very bad condition, both Economically and Militarily”, he wrote, and “the Blockade will remain in full force and effect”. His claim that 22 million barrels passed through Hormuz in a single night is a presidential assertion rather than a verified figure and is not treated as one here. What can be checked is harsher: Kpler counts nine tankers attacked in and around the strait in the past week, and 9.5 million barrels a day leaving Hormuz in the week to Tuesday, about 30% below the pre-war norm, with total Middle East flows near normal only because of pipeline workarounds. A further 500,000 barrels a day of Gulf of Mexico production is shut in for a hurricane, which is weather rather than war but prices the same. Iran’s foreign minister Abbas Araghchi says messages are still moving through intermediaries: “We presented our plan, which was the plan referred to as the seven-day plan. We heard the Americans’ views in response. We are now reviewing them. I think we will respond within a few days.” The British consequence arrived through the bond market, and is the subject of the first item in the column alongside this one.

Riyadh’s Airport Closes for the Fourth Time This Week

The Saudi-led coalition says it intercepted two ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and one at Khamis Mushait. The Houthi spokesman claims a missile struck King Khalid International and caused “damage and disruption to air traffic”. Saudi civil defence says falling interception debris damaged a kindergarten and a medical clinic in the capital.

Dive deeper
The admission about the kindergarten is unusual: Saudi authorities rarely concede civilian damage from the air war. Flight tracking showed the airport closed with no landing for more than eighty minutes and no departure for more than ninety; a witness described passengers being told to abandon checked luggage and leave. No approved source has published a count of cancelled flights, so none is given here. Air India has cancelled Riyadh services until at least the weekend and the Lufthansa group has suspended them through 16 October. The Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree went further than aviation, warning oil workers directly: the armed forces warn “all employees, including experts, engineers and workers at all Saudi oil facilities, against being present in locations that represent targets for our forces, so as not to put their lives at risk”. Turkey and Pakistan are assessing air defence, electronic warfare and technical help for the kingdom under their trilateral agreement, though Turkey’s foreign minister Hakan Fidan drew a line: “Turkey will not be sending troops into another country’s territory to carry out an attack.” Three people were killed and thirty-six injured in the strikes on two Saudi airports earlier this week.

Ethiopian Drones Strike Eritrean Troops Inside Tigray

Military vehicles crossed from Eritrea into Tigray on Wednesday and reached Adigrat, forty kilometres south, according to witnesses, a militia commander and the monitoring group Acled. Ethiopian government drones then struck the Eritrean troops on Wednesday evening and again on Thursday morning. Eritrea has neither confirmed nor denied the incursion.

Dive deeper
Eritrea’s information minister Yemane Ghebremeskel answered the question by refusing it: “We are not in the business of refuting wild allegations or narratives.” He had already accused Ethiopia of “incessantly beating the drums of war” over what Addis Ababa calls sovereign access to the sea, and of repackaging that agenda as self-defence. Ethiopia’s foreign ministry says it “stands ready to defend its sovereignty, territorial integrity, and core national interests” and that any discussion of regional maritime security “must account for Ethiopia’s legitimate interests and its enduring connection to the Red Sea”. Magnus Taylor of Crisis Group sets out the worst case plainly: Abiy Ahmed “might try to solve all his problems at the same time, by invading Eritrea, possibly a regime change move and also a land grab for the Red Sea port, and also solving his problem with Tigray at the same time. It’s a very ambitious military agenda, though.” The sequence behind this is fast: the Tigray People’s Liberation Front launched its offensive on 23 September and the allied Tigray Peace Force took Mekelle on 4 October. The 2020 to 2022 civil war killed between 300,000 and 800,000 civilians on British government estimates, and Mr Abiy won the Nobel peace prize in 2019 for making peace with Eritrea. With Iran constraining traffic through Hormuz, Red Sea access is worth more than it was.

Maduro and His Wife Charged With Conspiracy to Commit Torture

A superseding indictment filed in Manhattan federal court adds a count of conspiracy to commit torture against Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores, on top of existing drug-trafficking charges. Prosecutors say the conspiracy ran from at least 1999 to 2025 and involved Venezuelan military, intelligence and law enforcement officials. Trial is set for 1 June 2027.

Dive deeper
The indictment alleges that one detainee was given electric shocks and asphyxiated after being arrested following a 2018 drone attack the government called an assassination attempt. Three American citizens are named among the alleged victims; one was arrested in January 2025 in a case of mistaken identity, beaten over two days causing permanent injury to an eye, held for about a year, and warned on release not to disclose what had happened. Prosecutors say Mr Maduro was told a detained military officer remained defiant and instructed officials to “squeeze them harder”, suggesting specific methods; Ms Flores is alleged to have been present at some meetings where the treatment of detainees was discussed. Both have pleaded not guilty to the drug charges and have been held at the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn since their capture on 3 January, after Donald Trump authorised a military raid in Caracas. The torture count is the legally significant addition, because it reaches conduct inside Venezuela against Venezuelans as well as Americans, and because it sets up a trial in which the evidence will be the testimony of people who were held in those cells.

UK Domestic

Bailey Tells the Chancellor That Fiscal Policy Must Be Credible

Speaking in Istanbul as medium-term borrowing costs reached a nineteen-year high, the Bank of England governor said that “whatever the stance of fiscal policy, it must be credible and be seen as such by financial markets”. The ten-year gilt touched 5.515% by lunchtime, the highest since July 2007. Yields eased later in volatile trading.

Dive deeper
Andrew Bailey added that realistic commitments to rein in debt “are needed more than ever when these negative shocks occur”, which in context is a message to one chancellor three weeks before one Budget. The twenty and thirty-year yields reached their highest since 1998 before retreating. The immediate trigger was oil: Brent rose more than 5% to $105.30 on the prospect of American strikes on Iran, and the inflation that implies is what the gilt market was pricing. Economists reckon roughly half of the £24bn buffer against the fiscal rules built by Rachel Reeves at the spring statement has been wiped out, and possibly significantly more. John Healey is expected to raise taxes on 28 October while also funding a six-month VAT cut on electricity bills and support for the poorest households. Andrew Wishart of Berenberg Bank argues against doing it mechanically: raising taxes merely to maintain headroom “would do unnecessary damage to economic incentives”. The round trip is the part worth holding on to. A market that can move the government’s borrowing costs to a nineteen-year high and most of the way back inside a single session is not delivering a verdict on British public finances; it is reacting to a war, and the Budget arrives whatever it does next.

Asos Breach Is Wider Than the Company First Told Customers

Hackers hold detailed profiles of potentially millions of users, Asos has now acknowledged, after the BBC told the retailer the criminals had contacted it and that the breach went beyond the “basic contact details” first disclosed. Names, addresses, phone numbers, emails, customer numbers, dates of birth and site search histories were taken. No bank details or passwords.

Dive deeper
The attackers obtained an employee’s login by impersonating a trusted contact — not a software flaw but a person persuaded to hand over access. Calling themselves Xuanyewen, they told the BBC they used a platform built on top of Snowflake to extract the data and claimed to have compromised the Snowflake instance; Snowflake has previously said its platform was not breached. Asos declined to answer questions about scale. The search histories are the part that should worry people, because they turn a list of names into a profile: someone who can tell you what you have been browsing, your date of birth and your address sounds exactly like a retailer with a genuine query. Asos has told customers to “remain cautious of unexpected messages or calls” and that it “will never ask you to share passwords, security codes or payment details through an unsolicited message or call”. One customer, who has used the site since 2019, put the wider risk well: “What I find particularly worrying is the possibility that stolen data can be used as a tool for future attacks, meaning the impact of a breach could extend well beyond the initial incident.” Asos says customers need take no action; security specialists advise changing passwords anyway.

The Waiting List Rises Again as Corridor Care Climbs 13%

An estimated 7.37 million treatments were waiting at the end of August, covering 6.24 million patients — the highest since October 2025 and a second consecutive monthly rise. In September 50,582 people waited more than twelve hours between a decision to admit and being admitted, up from 44,951 in August. That is 1,686 people every day.

Dive deeper
The health service is not idle: a record 4.7 million planned treatments, procedures and appointments were delivered across June to August, more than in any previous summer. Demand is simply rising faster. Francesca Cavallaro of the Health Foundation puts the arithmetic plainly — rising referrals mean “the service is having to run faster just to stand still and the prospects of meeting the government’s pledge are receding” — and adds a political question that has not been answered: whether Andy Burnham’s government will give the eighteen-week target the priority it had under Keir Starmer. That target has not been met for a decade; the stated aim is 92% within eighteen weeks by 2029. Accident and emergency performance against the four-hour standard fell to 74.4% in September from 75.0% in August. Professor Frankie Swords, NHS England’s national medical director, says staff are seeing “the ominous early signs of pressure, with more patients coming to A&E with flu and other respiratory viruses than usual for this time of year”. Dr Vicky Price of the Society for Acute Medicine is blunter about the twelve-hour figure: a 13% year-on-year rise as winter approaches “should set alarm bells ringing”.

Warrants Used to Search Andrew’s Homes Were Unlawful, Court Rules

The High Court has quashed the search warrants used by Thames Valley Police on two of Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor’s homes at the time of his arrest in February, after the force accepted they were issued under the wrong section of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act. The seized documents and devices remain with police, and the criminal investigation continues.

Dive deeper
The error is technical and the consequence is not. Police applied under sections 8 and 9 of the 1984 Act; the Central Criminal Court decided only section 8 warrants were needed and granted them on that footing. The judicial review found section 9 was required — the route under which the issuing judge must consider legal privilege and special procedure material before anything is taken. In other words, no judge applied that test before documents and electronic devices left the premises, and that protection exists for everyone whose home or business is searched, not for one man in Norfolk. Mr Justice Nicholas Hilliard was careful to limit what he had decided: quashing the warrants “does not bring the investigation to an end or mean that the whole of the investigation is unlawful”, and on whether the material must go back, “there would be little point in handing property back if it could then simply be lawfully seized under some other provision”. Thames Valley Police accepts the court’s position and says proceedings continue over whether it can lawfully retain the material, with a further hearing listed for 2027. The underlying investigation concerns alleged misconduct in public office and arose after American court documents suggested confidential information was passed to Jeffrey Epstein while he was a trade envoy. He denies wrongdoing and was not in court.

The Big Six Are Now the Big Three After E.ON Takes Ovo

E.ON Next has completed its takeover of Ovo Energy with competition clearance, combining 5.6 million households with Ovo’s 4 million. The merged supplier holds about 25% of the market and 13.45 million accounts, second to Octopus. Almost three-quarters of British households are now served by three companies.

Dive deeper
Octopus has about 26% and 14.3 million accounts; British Gas about 23% and 12.5 million. Add EDF and Scottish Power and five firms serve roughly 90% of the country. The number worth setting against that is historical: the old Big Six held about 85% in 2016, when a landmark competition inquiry concluded that customers were overpaying by something between £1.4bn and £1.7bn a year because competition was too weak. A decade of policy aimed at breaking that grip, a crisis that removed about thirty suppliers in 2021 and 2022, and the market has ended up more concentrated than when the inquiry reported. Tom Goswell of Cornwall Insight states the worry directly: “The big six have become the big three”, and “the concern with fewer suppliers is that the pressure to compete eases off, taking with it some of the incentive to keep prices low”. Chris Norbury, chief executive of E.ON UK, rejects that reading, calling the market “fiercely competitive” and arguing that scale lets the company “deliver for customers now and to transform for the energy system to come”. Nothing changes for Ovo’s four million customers today. What changes is how many places they can go if it does.
