GEO Geopolitical

Riyadh Targeted a Third Night as Houthis Threaten America

Saudi Arabia says it intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at the capital and two more fired towards Khamis Mushait. No casualties were reported. Donald Trump said the Houthis were “misbehaving very badly”, and the group replied that American involvement would bring “a swift and painful response”.

Dive deeper
King Khalid International has been shut since Saturday’s strike and the closure now runs to Saturday 17 October, so this is a deepening siege rather than a fresh halt. At least twenty people have been killed in a week, among them an American. The threat is the sharpest development: a Houthi political bureau member, Mohammed Al Bukhaiti, said that if the United States joined the campaign “there will be a swift and painful response targeting the US and all its interests in the region”, and warned workers at Saudi oil facilities and airports to stay away because they had “become legitimate targets”. Washington is still only considering joining, while supplying intelligence and targeting. Airlines are voting with their schedules: Qatar Airways has suspended Riyadh and Dammam, Etihad has cancelled flights from Abu Dhabi, and one American carrier has pushed its Saudi launch to March. An urgent review is under way into why missiles keep penetrating Saudi air defences. The energy congress continued ten kilometres away, where Aramco’s chief executive Amin Nasser observed that markets “can diversify suppliers” but “cannot diversify geography”.

Trump Insists the Ceasefire He Announced Is Holding

Asked how he knew Moscow and Kyiv had signed up, the president said: “Well, they agreed, because I said, ‘You agree’.” Pressed on continued Russian strikes, he said there had been “violations on both sides”. The Kremlin has still not said whether it agreed to anything at all.

Dive deeper
Pressed on why neither capital had committed to the deal, Mr Trump indicated he had known when he announced it on Sunday that he did not yet have agreement from either side. Asked whether he was aware Russia had already violated it, he said: “I’m not sure about that.” Dmitry Peskov’s formula at his daily briefing was unchanged from Monday: “Contacts are continuing. As soon as we have anything to say about this, we will definitely inform you.” Kyiv says it learned of the ceasefire from news reports with no advance warning, and remains open to a mutual energy truce if Moscow accepts one. Volodymyr Zelensky’s summary of his own attempts was blunt: Ukraine has proposed many de-escalatory steps and “Putin had one answer to all of them — nyet”. The president also claimed, without evidence, that at least 32,000 people had been killed this month alone, and said the two leaders “have to get together and end that ridiculous war”. The announcement came two days after Washington eased sanctions to let Russian diesel reach global markets.

Sacked Ukrainian Commander Says Russia Cannot Advance

In his first interview since being removed this summer, General Oleksandr Syrskyi says Ukraine has stabilised the front and broken Russia’s ability to advance. He puts Russian losses at six times Ukraine’s, and says Moscow took a net 61 square kilometres in the first half of this year. The claim is his own.

Dive deeper
The territorial half of the argument is independently supported: the Institute for the Study of War has confirmed the same trend, finding Moscow lost more ground than it took in September. The casualty ratio is a different matter and does not survive comparison with published estimates. He says Russian losses run six times higher, “and if you count only dead, then it’s up to 11 times”; British intelligence estimates around 500,000 Russian dead since 2022, while one Washington institute put Ukrainian deaths at up to 150,000 by June, a ratio closer to three to one. Neither side publishes its own dead. On his removal he aims not at the president but at the former defence minister, saying procurement created an “imbalance” that prioritised drones while the army went short of unmanned ground vehicles, pick-ups and motorbikes, and even on drones received “only half of what they needed”. Drones now account for 80% of Russian losses. His message to European governments was the plainest thing he said: prepare now, and “drop all illusions” that war will not come.

The Only Region in the World Forecast to Shrink

The Middle East and Central Asia will contract 0.3% this year, the International Monetary Fund says, against growth of 1.9% it projected in April. Kuwait is forecast to shrink 16.7%, Iraq 16%, Qatar 15.6% and Bahrain 11.3%. No other region in the world is expected to shrink at all.

Dive deeper
This is the war’s bill, written out country by country, and the cause the fund names is the closure of the Strait of Hormuz — in peacetime a conduit for a fifth of global oil supplies. Iran is forecast to contract 7.6% and Saudi Arabia 3.3%, the Saudi figure cushioned by pushing exports through the east-west pipeline that bypasses the strait. The contrast with everywhere else is what gives the number its force: emerging and developing Asia is forecast to grow 5.1%, sub-Saharan Africa 4.5% and Latin America 2.3%. Kyrgyzstan, at 9%, is the strongest performer in the same region as the four worst. For a British reader the chain is short. These are the economies that set the oil price, and the same fund that published these numbers also upgraded Britain’s growth this morning while warning that higher energy prices will weigh on consumption and investment next year. Watch whether the contraction forecasts move again if the strait reopens; the fund’s assumptions about Hormuz are doing a great deal of the work.

Five Dead as Two Storms Hit Mexico’s Pacific Coast

Hurricane Simon killed a 71-year-old man when a wall collapsed in Chilpancingo, and Tropical Storm Rachel is blamed for the deaths of four members of one family, including two children, found outside Ensenada. Five people are still missing and helicopters have rescued thirty-two.

Dive deeper
Simon did not make landfall, weakening to a category two storm and moving away from the coast, though a rain alert remains in force and infrastructure and homes in Acapulco were damaged. The Guerrero state governor Evelyn Salgado confirmed the first death; the four found near Ensenada in Baja California were reported by the state prosecutor’s office. The Baja California governor Marina del Pilar said officials were helping more than fifty people in shelters. What lifts this above a single weather event is the pattern it completes: Simon caps a Pacific hurricane season of record-breaking intensity, with damaging storms running from Hawaii to the Mexican mainland. Acapulco has now been hit repeatedly in three years, and the rebuilding from each storm is still unfinished when the next arrives. The forecast question is whether the rain alert holds through the week, because the deaths so far have come from collapsing structures and flooding rather than from wind.

UK Domestic

Fund Upgrades Growth and Warns on Inflation

The International Monetary Fund has raised its forecast for British growth this year to 1.3%, from the 1% it expected in July, making the United Kingdom joint second-fastest in the G7. It also expects inflation to average 3.3% this year and 2.8% next, above target, and cut its 2027 growth forecast to 1.2%.

Dive deeper
The upgrade is less generous than it first reads, because the good news and the bad arrive in the same document. Growth next year is revised down, not merely slower, and the fund says it will “edge down” as “higher energy prices and long-term interest rates weigh on consumption and investment”. Britain is singled out, with Japan, as a country where core inflation will not return to 2% until late 2027; the Bank of England is gloomier still, expecting inflation above 4% early next year. One rate-setter, Megan Greene, argued in Bangkok that the upgrade is itself an argument for higher rates. What is unexpectedly warm is the verdict on the public finances: the fund’s economic counsellor Silvana Tenreyro said Britain “stands out” in the G7 for having begun consolidation, and that “the message for the UK is simply, stay the course”. It also advises against across-the-board subsidies, wanting support “temporary, targeted and non-distortionary” — awkward counsel for a chancellor under pressure to soften January’s energy bills. The Budget is on 28 October.

Six Hundred More Prisoners Released Early Today

About 650 offenders serving up to two and a half years leave jails in England and Wales today, freed after a third of their sentence rather than half. It follows roughly 700 released on 1 October, and the scheme escalates in stages, reaching those serving more than twelve years next June.

Dive deeper
Those released are normally tagged electronically for the second third of their sentence, which is where this collides with a finding reported yesterday: more than 5,000 people who should have been monitored in the first four months of the year were never fitted with a tag, about 15% of all orders. The probation service that must absorb both is also in dispute. The union says the releases have placed enormous strain on members and will ballot for strike action next week, having been offered 5% against a claim of 12%; the ministry points to £700m of investment, 1,300 extra officers and new automated tools. Court reform returns to Parliament the same day, with jury trials still to be scrapped for complex fraud, 5,500 more cases kept in magistrates’ courts and magistrates’ sentencing powers doubled to two years. Two things are not yet on the record and are not assumed here: which offences are excluded from this tranche, and what victims’ organisations make of it.

Council Deficits on Special Needs Forecast to Reach £10bn

County councils say deficits on support for children with special educational needs will reach a record £10bn by 2028, describing the position as catastrophic. Ministers recently cleared more than £5bn of historic debt; councils expect to have run that up again by April and doubled it within two years.

Dive deeper
The deadline that makes this urgent is an accounting one. A statutory override currently keeps these deficits off council balance sheets, and it expires in April 2028 — at which point authorities carrying them become insolvent on paper. Hampshire alone forecasts a £200m deficit this year, with Surrey, East Sussex, Kent, Worcestershire and Norfolk each above £100m. The demand behind it is not disputed by anyone: 718,000 children and young people held education, health and care plans in January, a rise of 12.5% in a year and the steepest since 2014, with a further 1.3 million identified as having special needs; 94% of the 105,300 statutory assessments carried out in 2024 ended in a plan, and several councils expect nearly one pupil in ten to hold one by 2029. The councils’ spokesperson Linden Kemkaran was careful to locate the cause: the deficits are not the result of reckless spending but of “having to meet rapidly growing statutory demand… without funding keeping pace”. Ministers say they will set out details before March 2028.

Heating Oil Households Face Winter Without a Cap

The government has accepted that the heating oil market needs tighter regulation, promising clearer compensation, more competition and a review of minimum order sizes. It has not proposed a price cap and has set no date. Prices have close to doubled in a year, with one customer quoted 111p a litre.

Dive deeper
About 1.5 million United Kingdom households heat their homes with oil and none of them is protected by the energy price cap, which covers only mains gas and electricity. The exposure is heavily regional: around 60% of Northern Ireland households use heating oil, and the regulator’s remit there is separate again. The competition authority found that 1,700 households were forced to reorder at significantly higher prices or go without fuel after the war began, at a cost of up to £350 each, and the minimum order of 500 litres is now to be reviewed against tanks that hold between 1,000 and 2,000. A £53m support package announced in March was distributed by councils on their own criteria, so what a household received depended on where it lived. The energy minister Polly Billington said customers “felt they had nowhere to turn” and that energy “is an essential good”. One supplier’s chief executive was blunter about what regulation will not do: it will not reduce the price, and what the trade wants is VAT removed.

Violence Plans Shelved on Cost, Southport Inquiry Told

Four Home Office proposals drawn up after the Southport murders — on online risk, prevention partnerships, a national gateway and strengthening Prevent — were not taken forward because they required more money, the inquiry has heard. The department decided instead to focus on “cost-neutral improvements” before last year’s spending review.

Dive deeper
Counsel to the inquiry, Nicholas Moss KC, set the decision against the prime minister’s statement at the time that “Southport must be a line in the sand”, suggesting the sentiment “doesn’t sit entirely comfortably” with accepting that high-ambition responses would be put on hold. The Home Office witness replied that part of the context was an expectation that the inquiry would make its own recommendations. What the four proposals would have cost cannot be reported, because the figures were redacted in the document shown to the inquiry. A separate departmental review last May found a “confused picture” and a system at risk of “allowing cases to fall between the cracks”, which counsel called bleak but candid and the witness accepted. The inquiry, chaired by Sir Adrian Fulford, is examining how violent fixated individuals are managed; its first phase concluded the attack that killed Alice da Silva Aguiar, Bebe King and Elsie Dot Stancombe could and should have been prevented. The hearings continue.
