Tens of Thousands March in Spain After Housing Measures Fall

Tens of thousands marched in some fifty Spanish cities on Saturday, a day after the opposition struck down two government housing decrees. Authorities put the Madrid crowd at 70,000; the Tenants’ Union claimed 500,000. In Valencia police fired rubber bullets and teargas to break up the protest, and about thirty people got inside the City of Arts and Sciences before being removed. Demonstrators waved posters calling for a general strike.

Dive deeper
The decrees were written in answer to a single eviction. Maricarmen Abascal, 87, was put out of her Madrid flat in September after a sharp rent rise she could not meet; her landlord later negotiated her return on a low rent, and the protests carried on without her. The first decree would have suspended evictions of vulnerable tenants until 2030, extended leases expiring before 2028, raised taxes on tourist apartments and restricted residential buying by investment funds. It fell 178 to 172. The second, automatic renewal of rental contracts, fell 184 to 166, after the centre-right Catalan party JxCat condemned both on the eve of the vote and sided with the People’s Party and Vox. The underlying numbers explain the anger better than the politics: the Bank of Spain puts the country 700,000 homes short, prices are up 12.2% on the year, and rents rose 8.5% in 2025. Marcos Sainz, a 26-year-old librarian, said a one-bedroom flat in Madrid now costs around €1,000 a month, about half the median salary in the capital. Valeria Racu of the Tenants’ Union said eight days of people power had achieved more than eight years of progressive government. Watch for a snap election: Pedro Sánchez was reported on Friday to be weighing a November poll, and to be waiting to see how large Saturday’s crowds were before deciding.

Ethiopian Forces Retake Mekelle Airport as Tigrayan Fighters Pull Out

Ethiopian government troops have regained Alula Aba Nega Airport outside Mekelle, one of three airfields seized by Tigrayan forces when fighting resumed on 23 September. A local journalist said the TPLF’s fighters and leaders were leaving the Tigrayan capital. Pro-government Tigrayan units also took Alamata, a supply town near the Amhara border. The military says about 270 Tigrayan fighters have been killed around North Wollo.

Dive deeper
Ten days of fighting have undone an agreement that held for nearly four years. The Pretoria deal of November 2022 ended the Tigray war; it has now collapsed into three simultaneous fronts, in Tigray, Amhara and Afar, with at least 52 civilians killed in the north and an explosion reported at the defence ministry in Addis Ababa on Thursday, the first time this round reached the capital. The Afar front matters most to the Ethiopian state, because the road from Djibouti carries almost all of the country’s imports, fuel included. The regional dimension hardened on 1 October, when Eritrea severed all diplomatic relations with Ethiopia, its foreign ministry saying it had no option but to reciprocate in kind; Asmara denies arming the TPLF. Laura Hammond, professor of development studies at SOAS, said there is a strong possibility Eritrea becomes more involved on the ground inside Tigray if the fighting continues, and a risk the conflict inflames relations with and inside Sudan. Tigere Chagutah of Amnesty International said the African Union, a guarantor of the ceasefire and headquartered in Addis Ababa, cannot wait for mass atrocities before putting its host country on the agenda. Watch whether the Peace and Security Council convenes at all.

Yemen Shuts the al-Houban Crossing as Fighting Closes on Taiz

Yemen’s internationally recognised government has closed the al-Houban crossing at Taiz, the main civilian link between Houthi-held territory and the government-held city, citing an attempted infiltration and a military build-up. Colonel Majed Abdullah al-Nazili said the army carried out 257 “precision targeting operations” on Saturday and estimated at least 700 Houthi fighters killed and 170 vehicles destroyed. He gave no figure for government losses.

Dive deeper
The crossing is the detail that will be felt. It was shut for nine years and reopened to civilians in 2024 on humanitarian grounds, and closing it again severs the ordinary traffic between al-Houban and Taiz city on which families and traders depend. The casualty claims around it should be read as claims: they come from one side, they are not independently verified, and the day before the same spokesman put the figure at 1,540 killed or wounded in 24 hours from nearly 500 operations. Saturday’s strikes were said to have hit Sanaa, Midi and Baqim and the front lines in al-Jawf, Marib, al-Bayda and Lahij, with the heaviest effort around Taiz. What is verifiable is the direction of the fighting: the 2022 truce collapsed in July, the Houthis swept across parts of western Yemen in mid-September and took Mocha and islands in Bab el-Mandeb, and residents have left the Samaa front in numbers not seen in more than a decade. A shell hit a residential area at Dhubab and killed a baby. Government forces have since taken the Samaa fort, south-east of Taiz. Watch the humanitarian traffic rather than the body counts.

Brazil Votes Tomorrow With Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro Level

Brazilians vote on Sunday in a presidential election the polls put level. Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, 80, is seeking a fourth non-consecutive term against Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, the far-right senator standing because his father is under house arrest for attempting a coup to overturn his 2022 defeat. If neither clears half the vote, a runoff follows on 25 October. Lula has accused Washington of trying to interfere.

Dive deeper
The contest has become a measure of American reach in the hemisphere. Donald Trump told a gathering of Latin American leaders after his UN address last month that everybody he had endorsed had won, and the right has in fact taken all seven presidential elections in the region since his second term began. Washington asked for privileged access to Brazil’s rare earths and critical minerals in return for withholding tariffs; Lula refused, and Flávio Bolsonaro has offered them as the way to break American dependence on China. The same policy has a harder edge: dozens of strikes on boats in the regional campaign against the drug trade have killed more than 230 people. Michael Shifter of Georgetown’s School of Foreign Service called Brazil “a big prize” and said American foreign policy is going very badly elsewhere. Pedro Telles of the School of International Relations at FGV said a Flávio win would be a real blow to the left and a major boost to the far-right agenda globally. At home Lula can point to falling unemployment, poverty and inflation and rising average incomes, against rising household debt; he banned online betting days before the vote. Watch whether it goes to a second round at all.

Somalia Rejects an Israeli Naval Base at Berbera

President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud has said Somalia will not accept an Israeli presence “under any circumstances”, and that Israel is planning a naval base at Berbera and the use of the city’s airport. Berbera lies in Somaliland, the self-declared republic Israel recognised in December and which Mogadishu claims. It faces Yemen across the Gulf of Aden, near the Bab el-Mandeb strait.

Dive deeper
Geography is the whole argument. Berbera sits opposite the strait the Houthis have used to blockade Israel-linked shipping, which is why a base there would matter and why Mogadishu reads it as an attempt to pull Somalia into other people’s wars. Mr Mohamud placed the plan under the “Greater Israel” project, the set of proposals for Israeli control beyond its present borders that Benjamin Netanyahu said in an August 2025 interview he felt connected to, and which Israel has never adopted as policy. He called Israel a destabilising force that does not abide by international law, said Somaliland’s own residents are completely opposed to an Israeli presence, and noted that no state other than Israel recognises Somaliland, which has governed itself since 1991. He was markedly softer on his neighbours: relations with Ethiopia are good, and he does not object to landlocked Addis Ababa gaining sea access through a joint agreement to use Somali ports — a pointed contrast with the memorandum Ethiopia signed with Somaliland in January 2024, which Mogadishu called a violation of its sovereignty. He also accused the Houthis of disrupting navigation at Bab el-Mandeb and said they are not in a position to bring peace to Yemen. Watch whether any Gulf state puts its name to the Berbera plan.

A London Borough Warns of a 150% Council Tax Rise

Hammersmith and Fulham’s cabinet says it may have to impose a 150% rise in its share of council tax, according to a budget report due on Monday. At Band D that is an extra £29.11 a week in 2027/28, about £1,000 a year. Its government funding falls from £150.5 million this year to £72.4 million over the next four years.

Dive deeper
The arithmetic in the report is the argument. A 100% rise would cost £19.40 a week and still leave a £46.1 million shortfall; 125% would cost £24.25 and leave £20.6 million; only 150% — raising an extra £163.9 million by 2030/31 — “broadly balances the budget on current assumptions”. The borough currently has the third-lowest council tax in England, which is precisely why it is exposed: the government’s “fair funding” review, introduced last year, redirects money towards places hit hardest by austerity, and aims to bring historically low-charging councils up towards the national average. Councillor Rowan Ree, the cabinet member for finance and reform, said the reduction “could be equivalent to losing as much as 40 per cent of our total annual budget of £222 million” while demand and costs for essential services keep rising, and that he does not support the policy but must present a legally balanced budget. Six councils have already been allowed to breach the 5% referendum cap, Wandsworth and Westminster among them, and neighbouring boroughs have warned they may need rises of 188% and 200%. The housing ministry says it has made over £78 billion available for council finances next year, mostly unringfenced, and that it is fixing an outdated system “so funding goes where need is greatest”. Watch Monday’s published report.

Court Told of a Homemade Bomb Aimed at Manchester’s Jewish Community

Rahman Salehi, 34, of Salford, and Salam Ahmadyan, 36, from Liverpool, appeared at Westminster Magistrates’ Court accused of preparing an act of terrorism on or before 20 September. The prosecutor said they are accused of targeting Manchester’s Jewish community “through the use of a homemade explosive device”, and of carrying out hostile reconnaissance. Both were remanded in custody until 23 October.

Dive deeper
Mark Luckett, prosecuting, said the two had been observed “identifying attempted targets such as the Jewish Museum and Heaton Park Synagogue”. The court also heard that they were allegedly “directed and co-ordinated” by another Iranian individual “most likely operating within Iran”. Separately, police said the men are alleged to have obtained instructions to make a chlorine bomb, acquired liquids and components for an improvised device, and used the internet to research targets, gathering images, plans and maps. Both arrived by small boat, Salehi in 2020 and Ahmadyan in 2023; Salehi had previously claimed asylum in Germany, was granted asylum here in 2023 and has leave to remain until July 2028, while Ahmadyan’s claim is outstanding. They were arrested in Manchester’s Northern Quarter on 20 September, the eve of Yom Kippur, with searches in Liverpool and Salford. One of the buildings allegedly reconnoitred was Heaton Park Hebrew Synagogue, where Melvin Kravitz, 66, and Adrian Daulby, 53, were killed a year ago when Jihad Al-Shamie drove into the gates and attacked people with a knife while wearing a fake suicide belt. Shabana Mahmood, the Home Secretary, said the charges would be “deeply distressing” as the city marked that anniversary. They appear at the Old Bailey on 23 October. Watch what the Crown says about the chain of direction.

Leaked Remarks Show the Tories Weighing Cuts Beyond the £47bn

Andrew Griffith, now shadow chancellor, told Conservative members last October that the shadow cabinet had discussed finding further savings in “every part of government”, beyond the £47 billion of cuts already announced. “The rate at which the economy is deteriorating, as well as our ambition to cut more taxes, means that we will have to find further savings,” he said. A party spokesman confirmed it will “go further” in Birmingham.

Dive deeper
The £47 billion already on the table is made up mainly of removing welfare from all non-British and non-EU citizens, £8 billion from cutting 132,000 civil service jobs, £1.6 billion from scrapping almost all of Labour’s clean energy scheme and more than £7 billion from international aid. On Friday Kemi Badenoch added £4 billion to that: £2.5 billion more from aid, which would leave it at £1 billion a year, and £1 billion from culture, media and sport, with half the saving paying for 50,000 prison places. For scale, Labour’s own planned reduction, from 0.5% to 0.3% of national income by 2027, still leaves about £9 billion; £1 billion is a cut of roughly 90%. Mr Griffith’s own words were that there are “decisions made and signed up to by everybody to deliver that £47bn” and that “last week at the shadow cabinet, we talked about [how] we will need to go further”, framing it as “a moral mission to get our economy growing”. He was promoted in August, was chief operating officer at Sky before politics, and worked in the Treasury under Kwasi Kwarteng, defending the mini-budget as the markets turned. Mrs Badenoch told ITV Meridian on Friday that privatisation “was a success” and that she does not think “Andy Burnham running the water companies is the answer”. Watch the conference for the named savings.

Greens Would Renationalise Two Water Firms and Guarantee Youth Jobs

The Greens would take Thames Water and South East Water into public ownership before Christmas without compensating shareholders or creditors, their co-deputy leader Rachel Millward told the party’s conference in Brighton, arguing both are insolvent. Her fellow co-deputy, Mothin Ali, set out a guaranteed two-year job for every 18 to 24-year-old within four months of leaving education, with the state paying half the salary.

Dive deeper
“Not a single major reservoir has been built in this country since the beginning of privatisation 35 years ago,” Ms Millward said, promising action “not in 10 years, but before Christmas” and at “no cost to the tax payer”: “Forget compensation for shareholders and creditors, these are insolvent companies.” She said the prime minister was “making a mistake”; his own legislation would repeal the ban on public ownership of water and begin a decade-long process. South East Water said it is investing £1.9 billion in local infrastructure and resilience over the next five years. Mr Ali’s jobs guarantee would sit in the private sector, exempt employers from National Insurance and require in-job training; the party says a pilot would create 9,000 jobs for £110 million in Rotherham, Ceredigion, Newcastle-under-Lyme, Broxtowe and South Staffordshire, though those costings came from party material rather than the speech itself. He put the number of young people out of work, education or training at a million, roughly one in seven of those aged 16 to 24, and said that “if we don’t take action now, we’re losing a generation”; the party estimates each such person costs the state £240,000 in benefits over a lifetime. Green membership now exceeds 200,000. Watch Sunday’s motions.

Man Arrested Over RAF Fairford Released on Bail

A 25-year-old British-Iranian dual national arrested on suspicion of preparing terrorist acts in connection with the RAF Fairford incident has been released on bail. He was detained in Westminster on Thursday, and searches of two properties there have now finished. Five other men, British nationals from London aged 23 to 25, were bailed on Monday. The investigation continues.

Dive deeper
A major incident was declared and dozens of homes evacuated last weekend after police were alerted to three suspicious vehicles near the Gloucestershire airbase. One of the men is understood to have dialled 999 himself about an hour before a local farmer reported a large group of masked men near the vehicles — a detail that sits awkwardly with the gravity of the charge and may be why the five were bailed so quickly, in what police called an “investigative decision” that was “grounded in experience and strategy”. Andy Burnham chaired a Cobra meeting that afternoon and later said there were “strong indications” that Iran had played a part, without giving detail. Tehran denies it: the Iranian embassy in London said it “categorically rejects and strongly condemns” what it called “unfounded and malicious speculations”. The base matters because it has been a United States forward operating location for decades, and in recent months American bombers have flown from it towards the Middle East after Britain approved their use for strikes on Iran; in July Iran said any base used for aggression against it was “a legitimate target”. Donald Trump said he was surprised by the bail decision: “We know everything about them, we would not have released them.” Watch whether anyone is charged.
