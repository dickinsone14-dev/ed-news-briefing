GEO Geopolitical

Trump Lifts Sanctions to Buy Russian Diesel; Kyiv Calls It a Gift

After a call with Vladimir Putin, Donald Trump announced that Russia will supply 300,000 tonnes of diesel immediately, 500,000 more in November and a further million after that, with the Treasury issuing a licence permitting Russian diesel imports until April 2027. Volodymyr Zelensky called it “a weak decision, unfortunately, a weak decision by strong partners”.

Dive deeper
The order of the evening matters. Mr Trump posted the announcement while his envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner were in a room with Ukrainian officials discussing how to end the war, which is why Mr Zelensky’s statement reads as it does: “I believe our team is simply being used as a smokescreen. And that is certainly not fair. It is certainly not how partners should treat each other.” He added that allowing Russia to sell petroleum products “is an investment in a war that must be ended, not prolonged”. The Kremlin then confirmed a readiness to supply oil and products but named no volumes and no dates, and separately used the same call to close a door: the foreign policy aide Yuri Ushakov said Ukrainian drone attacks meant Kyiv had “interrupted the possibility of an immediate resumption of the negotiation process”. Hours later a Ukrainian drone strike set a fuel facility in Russia’s Rostov region alight, on a single report that says more than fifty drones were intercepted and nobody was hurt. A separate meeting in Miami this morning was described as productive, and is worth naming accurately: Mr Witkoff says it involved the E3, the European Commission, Ukraine and NATO. Russia was not there, so it is not the talks Mr Putin had just ruled out. Britain sits at that table as an E3 member. Of the 4.8 million tonnes promised, three million are conditional on refineries Ukraine has been bombing.

A Magnitude 7.7 Earthquake Strikes Panama, and the Canal Keeps Running

The quake struck on Friday afternoon local time at a depth of 12.6 kilometres, about 200 kilometres from Panama City, shaking the capital for the better part of a minute. A second of 6.6 followed two and a half hours later, with 147 aftershocks. Several people have minor injuries; no deaths have been confirmed.

Dive deeper
President José Raúl Mulino called it “a major disaster” while confirming that no deaths or injuries had been documented by Friday evening, and warned against using it “to cause alarm or disorder”. The damage is concentrated in Coclé, Herrera, Los Santos and Veraguas: the cathedral at Penonomé lost a side and a spire, the Church of San Atanasio in Los Santos collapsed, a multi-storey building came down in the resort town of Playa Venao crushing several cars, fissures opened along roads, six provinces lost power and classes were cancelled nationwide. The United States Geological Survey estimates 4.6 million people felt moderate to severe shaking and puts the economic loss at between 1% and 10% of national output. Ricardo Brown, 74, in Las Minas: “We’re scared. It’s just the two of us old folks here. The house has cracked and we’re afraid it could fall on us.” Tsunami warnings went out for eleven countries before being stood down. The detail that matters beyond Panama is the one that did not happen: the canal continued normal operations. With the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed, that waterway is carrying more of the world’s trade stress than usual. Panama was due to hold a national earthquake drill on Monday.

Trump Calls the Nobel an Indelible Stain on Norway

At a rally in Syracuse, the president said he had never heard of the “mystery person” who won the Peace Prize, called the award “an indelible stain on the country of Norway” and added “we will not forget”. The winner, Navi Pillay, is a former judge of the court his administration sanctioned hours after her prize was announced.

Dive deeper
He went further, saying the prize had been “forever discredited by a handful of radical ideologues who are anti-democracy and really enemies of civilisation”, and repeated without evidence that he had settled eight wars. The Nobel committee chair Jørgen Watne Frydnes had said Ms Pillay’s work was “instrumental in ensuring that war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide are prosecuted”. Marco Rubio, announcing sanctions on the International Criminal Court as an institution, said: “Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC,” and called on allied signatories, Britain among them, to rein it in. They have declined in writing. The foreign ministers of Canada, Denmark, Germany, France, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom say they “regret and strongly disagree with the announcement of sanctions”, that the measures “will have a significant impact on the Court’s work, its dedicated staff and their families”, and that they will “continue to work together to support the Court’s vital work”. António Guterres called the sanctions “a serious blow to our collective pursuit of international criminal justice”; Benjamin Netanyahu welcomed them. Ms Pillay, reacting from the Palace of Justice at Nuremberg, said it “must be a joke”, then offered to share the prize with Mr Trump if the applause went on.

Delhi Sealed as the Cockroach Party Marches Over Deleted Voters

Thousands of police and paramilitaries are deployed in central Delhi, 57 metro stations closed from Friday night, long-distance trains cancelled and mobile internet suspended within four kilometres of the protest area. The demand is the resignation of the chief election commissioner over a revision of the electoral roll that has removed more than 130 million names.

Dive deeper
The Election Commission says the revision strips out duplicate and ineligible entries; critics say it has fallen disproportionately on marginalised and minority communities and favours the governing party. The protests escalated after a national newspaper reported that the commissioner’s two deputies had formally raised concerns at least fourteen times in ten months. Police refused permission for the rally and warned of “a forceful law enforcement response”; India’s Supreme Court said on Friday that some trains must still run and that blanket closure of metro stations was unlikely. The party’s chief Abhijeet Dipke: “The dictator would rather shut down the entire national capital and inconvenience millions of citizens than allow a peaceful protest at a designated protest site.” Neha Bora of the All India Students’ Association: “You can detain the people, you cannot detain the movement.” The party says at least ten thousand people have been detained nationwide, a figure its own claim and not independently confirmed. The movement began in May as online satire after a chief justice referred to unemployed young people as “cockroaches” and “parasites”, a characterisation he says was misquoted; in July it forced an education minister to resign over exam paper leaks. State elections follow in early 2027.

Taiwan Raises Defence Spending to a Record to Deter War

“Our aim by increasing investment in national defence is not to provoke; rather, it is to deter war,” President Lai Ching-te said outside the Presidential Office on the island’s national day. The 2027 budget is a record NT$1.1 trillion, about $34.5bn and more than 3% of output. Twenty-one Chinese military aircraft circled in the previous 24 hours.

Dive deeper
He framed it as a limit rather than a challenge: “We are open to exchanges, but will not accept subordination,” and “Peace cannot rely solely on wishes, and tolerance cannot become a pretext for aggressors to encroach ever further.” Thirteen of the twenty-one aircraft crossed the median line of the strait, alongside seven navy vessels. Taiwan is waiting on American sign-off for about $14bn of weapons including Patriot air defence, after an $11bn package in December that was the largest arms sale to the island on record. That wait is the point. In May, after meeting Xi Jinping, Mr Trump suggested arms sales to Taiwan could be a bargaining chip — the same transactional logic that last night traded sanctions on Russian fuel for a promise of diesel. A British reader can see the pattern twice before breakfast. The practical stake is narrower and harder: Taiwan makes the advanced semiconductors inside every British car, phone and data centre, and no alternative supply exists at that quality or scale. This account rests on a single approved source; it is a national day set-piece and will be matched widely later today.

Northern Ireland Secretary Bans the Drumcree Parade

Sir Chris Bryant has banned the Portadown procession under section 11 of the Public Processions Act, acting on a request from the Chief Constable and ending a thirteen-day impasse. “I’ve decided that it’s not in the public interest for it to proceed,” he said, calling for all protests and rallies to be stood down.

Dive deeper
The decision reverses a reversal. Two weeks ago the Parades Commission ruled that a circumscribed march could pass along the Garvaghy Road for the first time in twenty-eight years; police stopped it when thousands of residents, including Sinn Féin leaders, blocked the route. Sir Chris held several rounds of negotiations at Hillsborough Castle last week and said he did not want the stand-off running into another weekend “with the consequent risk of protests spreading to other parts of Northern Ireland”. He thanked Portadown’s Orange Lodge for its “peaceful and law-abiding approach”. The Chief Constable Jon Boutcher said the parade would have carried “an unacceptable risk of serious injury, disorder and a wider contagion effect” and that “time and space is now created for conversations about finding long-term solutions”. Reaction split precisely as expected. Mary Lou McDonald and Michelle O’Neill spoke of “an enormous sense of relief within the community”; the residents’ coalition called it the only “sensible, reasonable and logical outcome”. Gavin Robinson of the DUP called it “a mistake” that sends “profound signals that the country should be run on force of numbers and who shouts the loudest”, leaving the framework for parading and protest “in tatters”. Micheal Martin warned against triumphalism. Separately, the police have written to Ms O’Neill about alleged offences during the protest; unionists are demanding she stand down.

Labour Insiders Fear the Budget Is Too Timid to Matter

John Healey’s first Budget on 28 October is expected to be deliberately low key: fiscal devolution, help for small businesses and a household support package built around cutting energy bills. One Labour MP is quoted as saying that “as things stand, this looks like a Reeves budget with better vibes”.

Dive deeper
The full quotation sets the fear out plainly: “If voters aren’t better off by the next election, we are screwed… I hope this isn’t all there is. But I really fear that actually it is.” A Downing Street aide frames the same date differently — “a moment of peak attention and potentially peak peril… we can’t fix everything all at once, but we’re trying” — while another calls it “our first punch in the face moment”. The constraint is the war: inflation and borrowing costs are reported to have blown a hole of about £12bn in the public finances, a figure the report itself hedges and which no official body has confirmed. Against that, the government is pointing to small, visible measures produced by departmental incubators: rules on car-parking apps, unbranded school uniforms. The pollster More in Common asked voters for a word to describe Andy Burnham and the most prominent answer was “trying”, which is either encouraging or damning depending on the day. This account rests on a single outlet and on unnamed sources within the governing party; the date, the chancellor and the energy focus are solid, and the mood is their characterisation rather than an established fact. The gilt market has spent this week making the arithmetic harder.

Head Teachers Move From No Confidence to Strike Ballots

Having passed a motion of no confidence in Ofsted’s leadership on Friday, the Association of School and College Leaders now says it will seek an indicative ballot on industrial action if change is not forthcoming. The National Association of Head Teachers will consider strike action at a leadership meeting next week.

Dive deeper
The number that gives the unions their ground comes from the inspectorate itself: 88% of schools reported that recent inspections had negatively affected staff wellbeing, in a survey Ofsted published. Pepe Di’Iasio of the ASCL says repeated attempts to resolve concerns through negotiation “have hit a brick wall” and that the system “is not fit for purpose. It is an overbearing mess and urgently needs to be reviewed.” Paul Whiteman of the NAHT is explicit about next week: “Our executive committee will be meeting at the end of next week… We fully expect that debate to include the option of pursuing industrial action.” The ASCL motion names the consequence it fears, which is not disruption but attrition: that “the disproportionate stress and anxiety exerted by the inspection system will lead to school leaders deciding to leave their posts”. The education secretary Lucy Powell has already said she wants Ofsted to go back to the drawing board on the framework introduced last year. The inspectorate acknowledges that workload and wellbeing “remain a challenge”. All of this sits in the shadow of Ruth Perry, the Reading head teacher whose death a coroner found in 2023 had been contributed to by an inspection, and whose case produced the system now facing its second no-confidence vote in a month.

Boots Under New Ownership: What Actually Changes

The Weston family’s Wittington Investments takes on 1,800 stores in the chain’s 178th year, having bought it for about £6.7bn. Retail analysts expect money spent on smaller branches, an expansion of healthcare services including weight-loss drugs, and no early change to the Advantage card.

Dive deeper
The card is the asset nobody expects them to touch. Launched in 1997, it pays three points per pound with each point worth a penny — a rate Jackie Naghten, a retail veteran of Top Shop, Marks and Spencer and Debenhams, calls “the best-value store card in terms of bang for your buck”. Natalie Berg of NBK Retail makes the commercial case for keeping it: it gives Boots “a unique understanding of their customers”, something new owners “will want to double down on”. The estate is the problem. Sofie Willmott of GlobalData Retail says smaller stores “have really lacked investment over time” and that the chain has “a bit of a disconnect” between its best and worst branches. Ms Naghten wants them “more functional” rather than health hubs “squeezed in the corner”. Customers quoted in the reporting are blunter about the immediate irritations: sanitary product prices described as “ridiculously expensive”, and rewards points that only pay out against a full transaction. The Westons owned Selfridges from 2003 to 2021, and a separate branch of the family controls Associated British Foods, which owns Primark — so this is a family that already knows the British high street. One number to hold lightly: some reporting rounds the price to £7bn, which is the headline figure rather than the £6.7bn in the body.

NS&I Pushes Savings Bonds Above 5% for the First Time in Years

National Savings and Investments has raised its British savings bonds to 4.99% for one year, 5.07% for two, 5.10% for three and 5.17% for five — most above 5% for the first time in nearly three years. The minimum is £500, the maximum £1m a person per issue, and there is no early withdrawal.

Dive deeper
The case for taking it is not the rate, because better rates exist. Sarah Coles of AJ Bell puts it directly: “you can still make more money elsewhere” — the best one-year fixed bond pays 5.12% and the best five-year 5.37%. The case is security at scale. NS&I says most banks guarantee savings only up to £120,000 and that it is “the only provider that secures 100% of your savings above this amount”, being Treasury-backed; that framing is NS&I’s own and worth checking against the compensation scheme’s published limit before acting on it. The warning attached is about timing rather than value. Rachel Springall of Moneyfactscompare notes that attractive deals “do not always hang around for long”, because once a provider has drawn in enough money it pulls the product. Elsewhere, Starling pays 5% on its Easy Saver with unlimited penalty-free withdrawals on balances to £25,000, but only for current-account customers who joined on or after 1 October — earlier customers get 4% — and Marcus by Goldman Sachs moved its one-year fix from 4.3% to 4.75% this week. All of it is the same story told from the saver’s side: the gilt yields that have made this week uncomfortable for the chancellor are what is paying for these rates.
