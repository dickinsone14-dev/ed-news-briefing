GEO Geopolitical

Sánchez Calls a Spanish Election for 29 November

Pedro Sánchez has called a snap general election for 29 November, in a televised address three days after parliament threw out his two housing decrees and two days after tens of thousands marched in Madrid, Valencia and Barcelona. Spain was not due to vote until the summer of 2027. He said he needed “a broader progressive majority” able to “overcome the resistance of the elites”.

Dive deeper
It is an unusually frank pitch for a sitting prime minister. “We have made mistakes and have not achieved everything we had set out to do,” Mr Sánchez said, naming inequality, public services and access to housing as the unfinished work. He has governed since June 2018 and, since 2023, as the leader of a minority coalition that finished second: the People’s Party won most seats and fell short, and he survived on Catalan and Basque votes. The decrees that fell on Friday would have suspended evictions of vulnerable tenants until 2030, extended leases expiring before 2028, raised taxes on tourist flats and restricted purchases by investment funds; the second, automatic renewal of rental contracts, was the tenants’ union’s central demand. Behind all of it is one eviction: Maricarmen Abascal, 87, carried out of the Madrid flat she had lived in for more than seventy years on a stretcher on 23 September, after a legal gap let her rent go from €500 to €2,650, later cut to €1,650 — still some €300 above her pension. Alberto Núñez Feijóo welcomed the vote: “At last, Spaniards will be able to decide,” framing it as a choice between “decline or change”. Most polls point to his party emerging largest but needing Vox to govern. Watch whether the left’s junior partner holds together across a 55-day campaign.

Yemen’s Government Turns on Sanaa as Turkey and Pakistan Commit Forces

Saudi-backed government forces have declared a “battle for liberation” and launched an offensive towards the Houthi-held capital, saying they have taken Mocha on the west coast. The claim is not independently confirmed. Separately, a committee of the Mecca defence pact — Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan — agreed to “provide the agreed military forces and capabilities and ensure their rapid deployment” in the kingdom.

Dive deeper
The second half of that is the part with consequences beyond Yemen. Until today the Mecca pact was a signature; activating it commits two non-Arab powers, one of them nuclear-armed and one a NATO member, to putting troops on Saudi soil in answer to what the committee called “heinous” Houthi attacks. That changes the character of the war from a Saudi-led coalition campaign into something closer to a bloc, and it follows Houthi missile and drone claims against Aramco sites at Riyadh and Khurais at the weekend. On the ground the government’s position is more fragile than its language: it declared the offensive only after the Houthis cut the main road linking besieged Taiz to Aden, and taking Mocha, if confirmed, would be the recovery of a port the Houthis seized only last month. Heavy damage was reported at a factory in Sanaa. Every territorial figure in this war is a belligerent claim and almost none of it is independently verified, which is worth holding on to when the numbers get large. The 2022 truce collapsed in July. Watch whether Turkish or Pakistani units actually arrive, because a committee decision and a deployment are not the same thing.

Washington Plays Down the Fairford Withdrawal; No 10 Says Base Is Safe

Twelve B-1 Lancers left RAF Fairford so quickly that the refuelling aircraft needed to get them across the Atlantic were reportedly still being fuelled as crews were ordered to scramble. Marco Rubio declined to connect the move to last month’s security incident — “I wouldn’t just directly tie that to the move that was made” — and JD Vance called it “an abundance of caution”. Downing Street says the base is safe.

Dive deeper
The manner of the departure undercuts the explanation offered for it. These aircraft had flown from Gloucestershire for seven months against Iranian missile launch sites, and they left in a hurry. Mr Rubio called it unremarkable — “It’s not unusual to see movement of assets to different parts of the world based on exercises, redeployments, people changing out” — while the vice-president put it down to caution and said Iran had spent 49 years engaging in acts of terrorism. British officials were blunter. They would not say what the new intelligence was, but told the BBC there is “an ongoing high tempo of threat from Iran to the UK, and it is logical that US airbases would be pretty high on any targeting list”. A NATO military source said it is “still believed that Iran poses an acute threat to US assets in the UK”. Whitehall has not disputed that Washington acted in response to the 27 September incident, and the decision is described as military rather than political, taken at operational level. Ezra Cohen, a defence official in Donald Trump’s first administration, called it prudent on practical grounds: the B-1 is a strategic asset, no longer in production and held in limited numbers. Five British men arrested over three suspicious vehicles near the base have been bailed, as has a sixth, a British-Iranian detained separately in London. Iran said in July that any country providing such a facility would be a legitimate target; Tehran denies any role in the incident. Watch whether the B-52s follow.

Russian Drones Sink a Turkish-Owned Grain Ship in the Black Sea

Two drones struck the Royad Mammadov, a Turkish-owned vessel carrying corn, which sank about thirty nautical miles south of the Romanian port of Sfantu Gheorghe, inside Romania’s exclusive economic zone. Eleven crew, nationals of Azerbaijan and India, were rescued. Volodymyr Zelensky said the captain was killed and called the attack “horrific”; Romania’s interior ministry has identified two dead.

Dive deeper
“Russia consistently chooses terror and sinking civilian vessels instead of normal maritime navigation,” Mr Zelensky said, noting he had repeatedly proposed steps to de-escalate in the Black Sea, and arguing that “pressure is needed to restore global food security”. Moscow has not commented on this ship, though its defence ministry said earlier that it had struck two cargo vessels. The casualty accounts do not yet agree: Kyiv describes a captain killed and one sailor unaccounted for, Bucharest two bodies identified. What makes it more than a shipping incident is the geography — a NATO member’s vessel sunk inside an EU member’s economic zone — and the timing. The head of Germany’s foreign intelligence service, Martin Jaeger, warned on the same day that Germany “is in danger of becoming embroiled in a violent conflict with Russia”, which he said was waging a shadow war, with the threat of attacks on German targets “real and imminent”. Friedrich Merz said last week that Germany was preparing for further hybrid attacks “including serious ones”, after an explosive-armed drone was found in August on the tarmac at Leipzig airport beside Ukrainian cargo aircraft. Russia’s defence ministry said on Sunday it would intensify its strikes. Watch Ankara’s response.

Teenager Loses a Hand as France’s School Protests Enter Week Three

A fifteen-year-old’s hand was blown off during a confrontation between school protesters and police at Lens in Pas-de-Calais, in an incident the prefect called “very serious”; the boy’s life is not in danger. The police watchdog is investigating. The protests over teacher shortages and school conditions are now in their third week, with teachers on strike and other unions threatening to join them.

Dive deeper
What began as pupils protesting about the state of their schools has become a national industrial dispute with the school year itself on hold. Up to five hundred schools were shut on Sunday on the education ministry’s own safety advice, and a high school in Metz was gutted by fire last weekend, though authorities have never established whether that fire was connected to the protests. The watchdog inquiry is the thing to follow, because the central question is what caused the blast: if it is attributed to a police munition, a dispute about staffing and buildings turns into a dispute about policing, which in France has a way of widening very quickly and drawing in unions that have so far only threatened to act. The government has so far framed the closures as a safety measure rather than a concession. Watch whether the other unions move from threatening action to calling it, and whether the watchdog reports before they decide.

The Average Five-Year Fixed Mortgage Rate Hits 6%

The average five-year fix reached 6.00% today, up from 5.98% on Friday and the first time above six in three years. The two-year average is 5.98%, its highest since December 2023. The number of fixed deals priced below 5% has collapsed from 1,494 at the start of September to nine. The Bank rate has not moved since December last year.

Dive deeper
This is not the Bank of England doing it. Rachel Springall of Moneyfacts put the cause plainly: “As wholesale funding costs climb on the back of rising gilt yields, fixed rate adjustments are somewhat inevitable” — and gilt yields rose again today, the ten-year adding about four basis points to 5.41%. Barclays has raised selected fixed rates four times in September alone; HSBC, Lloyds, Nationwide, NatWest, Santander and TSB three rounds each. In cash, a £250,000 loan over twenty-five years costs £1,611 a month at 6%, against £1,453 at February’s average of 4.94% — £158 more a month, or £1,896 a year, on the HomeOwners Alliance’s figures. Around half a million homeowners need a new deal before the year is out, and the Bank expects just over five million to be paying more by the end of 2028. Ms Springall called the three-year highs “disastrous news for borrowers”. Note what has not moved in step: sub-5% variable deals have gone only from 411 to 389, so the squeeze is specific to fixed pricing. It arrives in a month that has already brought a 4% rise in energy prices and diesel at a record £2 a litre, with a forecast 16% rise at the January cap. Watch the Budget on 28 October, and Nationwide’s finding that annual house price growth halved in September.

Griffith Pledges to Scrap the Family Homes Tax and Back Heathrow

The shadow chancellor told the Conservative conference he will abolish Labour’s High Value Council Tax Surcharge, under which English homes worth more than £2 million pay an extra levy from 2028. “They pretend it’s a mansion tax, but it will hit people here in Birmingham, in Manchester,” Andrew Griffith said. He later committed a Conservative government to the Heathrow third runway. No costing has been published for either.

Dive deeper
“Like Nigel Lawson, it’s my ambition to scrap at least one tax every budget,” Mr Griffith said, adding the plastic packaging tax to the list and conceding most people have never heard of it. He was careful on sequencing — “we will cut taxes, but only when we can afford to do so” — and said earlier in the day that he and Kemi Badenoch ultimately want to remove inheritance tax but must find the money first, which is a more guarded position than the weekend headlines suggested. He also blamed his own side for losing the argument: in government, he said, the party “allowed restrictions on everything from the type of car you could buy, how you could heat your home, to banning adverts for certain types of yogurt”. On aviation he promised to “do whatever it takes to end the delays” and legislate to “sweep away the blockers”, which immediately exposed a split: Sir James Cleverly said he had “always been very sceptical” of quick-fix arguments for a third runway, and Andrew Boff, who chairs the London Assembly, called it “the most unambitious scheme”. Andy Burnham has still not said whether he backs it, amid reports the project could slip by up to four years. Meanwhile the party’s claim that deregulation would cut £50,000 from the cost of a new home has been fact-checked as the housebuilding industry’s own estimate. Watch Mrs Badenoch’s own speech later this week.

Shadow Home Office Minister Claims “No-Go Zones” in Luton

Blake Stephenson, the MP for Mid Bedfordshire appointed to the Conservative frontbench a fortnight ago, told a fringe meeting in Birmingham that in parts of Luton “white people won’t go. They are like no-go zones.” He called the town “a very dangerous place” and said some communities “live in ghettoes”. Bedfordshire Police have previously said no-go areas do not exist in the county.

Dive deeper
Mr Stephenson returned to the word “icky” more than once: “this is where it gets icky, but we need to make sure we don’t have ghettoes where there are no-go zones for anybody”. He also told the meeting that “there is a consensus, certainly on the right, that mass migration has been an absolute disaster”, and said he knows the town because he lives ten or fifteen minutes away, in a village over the county border. The specific claim has a history that makes it combustible. Anders Breivik invoked Luton and more than a thousand supposed Islamic no-go zones in a statement to an Oslo court in 2012, and Tommy Robinson founded the English Defence League in the town in 2009; the force’s response then was that no-go areas do not exist in Bedfordshire. A shadow Home Office minister saying it from a conference platform is a different order of claim from a fringe commentator making it, which is why the test now is whether the leadership distances itself. No Conservative response had been issued this evening. Watch for one, and for Luton’s own MPs and council.

Andrew Challenges the Search Warrants; the King’s Money Is Ruled Out

Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor is seeking judicial review of warrants for Sandringham and Royal Lodge, issued at the Central Criminal Court on 18 February, the day before his arrest on his 66th birthday on suspicion of misconduct in public office. A hearing is listed for Thursday afternoon. A royal source says the King’s private allowance must not fund the action.

Dive deeper
The challenge is against the chief constable of Thames Valley Police and against the Central Criminal Court itself. The force said only that it is “engaged in legal proceedings in relation to warrants previously obtained” and that it would be inappropriate to comment further; Thursday’s hearing begins in private, with the judge first considering a police application to keep some documents from publication. The money question is the one with constitutional edge. The King’s private allowance is not the Sovereign Grant, the £138 million of public funding for the monarchy, but his own income including the Duchy of Lancaster — and the sum involved has not been disclosed. At the time of the arrest the King said the law “must take its course”. As for what a win would be worth, Nick Vamos, a criminal lawyer and former head of special crime at the Crown Prosecution Service, offered the first substantive public scepticism since February: even if the warrants are quashed, “that would not necessarily be the victory it might first appear”, because police can apply to the Crown Court to retain the material anyway. Watch Thursday, and whether any of it is heard in open court.

Southport Inquiry Told Violent Material Stays Online for Weeks

A review for the Southport Inquiry found that violent material was still available two weeks after being reported in more than 80% of cases, across X, TikTok, Reddit, Instagram and Discord — the result of a single eight-hour search. Nicholas Moss KC, counsel to the inquiry, set out the finding as its second phase examines violence-fixated individuals and the role of the internet.

Dive deeper
The number that will travel is the eight hours. One search, over a single working day, produced a body of material of which the great majority was still reachable a fortnight after being flagged — which goes directly to the question the Online Safety Act was written to answer, namely whether platforms remove what they are told about. The inquiry’s second phase is not re-examining the attack itself but the ecosystem around it: how a violence-fixated individual accumulates material, and what the companies did or did not do. Alice da Silva Aguiar, nine, Bebe King, six, and Elsie Dot Stancombe, seven, were killed at a Taylor Swift-themed dance class in Southport on 29 July 2024, and twenty-three other children were physically or psychologically injured. The practical consequence of a finding like this is regulatory rather than criminal: it hands the regulator a measured, inquiry-sourced figure to set against the platforms’ own compliance claims, at exactly the point when enforcement powers are being tested for the first time. Watch for Ofcom’s response, and whether the inquiry asks the named companies to account for the specific reports.
