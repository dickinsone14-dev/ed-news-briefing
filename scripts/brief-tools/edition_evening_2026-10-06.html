Trump Says Iran Could “Take Out” Los Angeles and San Diego

Speaking at a rally in Nebraska, Donald Trump said of the war on Iran: “It’s a small price to pay for keeping the world safe, keeping our country safe. They can take out a city. Let them take out Los Angeles, let them take out San Diego.” Parts of the audience cheered. Gavin Newsom called the remarks “deranged and dangerous” and has directed California’s threat assessment centre to monitor for foreign reaction.

Dive deeper
The governor went further than condemnation, calling the words “dangerous — if not treasonous” and instructing the State Threat Assessment Center to watch in case foreign actors read them as an invitation. Karen Bass, the mayor of Los Angeles, said they amounted to threats and an attempt to encourage attacks on American lives; Todd Gloria in San Diego said his city was not collateral damage and asked to be kept out of “reckless war talk”. Both cities carry serious military weight — Naval Base San Diego is the home port of the Pacific Fleet, and Camp Pendleton has some seventy thousand personnel — which is presumably why they came to mind, and exactly why naming them is not cost-free. The White House communications director Stephen Cheung called the reaction a “media freakout” “not rooted in reality”, arguing the president was describing what a nuclear Iran would have threatened rather than inviting anything: “America is safer because of the President. Iran has no nuclear, no navy, no air force, and no money.” That defence rests on a tense the sentence does not obviously have. Mr Trump had not responded to the Californian officials by early evening. Watch whether he repeats it, because a second utterance would settle the question of whether it was a slip.

Germany’s Former Spy Chief Arrested for Treason and Espionage

August Hanning, who ran the BND from 1998 to 2005, has been arrested on suspicion of espionage, attempted treason and acting as an agent for a foreign intelligence service. Prosecutors allege he received around 2,000 classified documents over several years in exchange for payment. A second former officer, his one-time bureau chief, has been arrested for aiding and abetting. Neither foreign service has been named.

Dive deeper
The second man is the more alarming detail. He remained inside the BND until this year and served as chief of staff to Hanning’s successors from 2012, which means the alleged pipeline ran from a serving officer at the top of the service to a retired chief working as a private consultant. The federal prosecutor’s office says that “on one occasion, Hanning used classified intelligence provided by Manfred D to draft an analysis for an officer of a foreign intelligence service”, while conceding it has yet to establish whether the material reached its intended recipient. He is separately accused of years of close contact with a representative of a second foreign service, to whom he is said to have passed political information. Two services are implicated and neither has been identified, which is the gap everything else will be read into. Marc Henrichmann, who chairs the Bundestag’s intelligence oversight committee, said that “whoever allies themselves with the enemies of our liberal democracy will be found out” and that Germany would not tolerate the betrayal of secrets. The documents are reported to have surfaced through an unrelated investigation. It follows the case of a serving BND officer charged in 2023 with passing Ukraine and Wagner material to Russia for around €400,000. Watch the court appearances this week.

A Cargo Ship Is Sunk by Drones Inside Bulgaria’s Waters

Two commercial vessels were hit by air and sea drones about seventy nautical miles off the Bulgarian coast, inside the country’s exclusive economic zone. The Togo-flagged, Turkish-owned Alfa Watan sank at the scene with its crew missing; all eighteen aboard the grain carrier Able were rescued, two of them seriously injured. Nobody has claimed the attack and Bulgaria has not attributed it.

Dive deeper
President Rumen Radev called it “an unacceptable attack in Bulgaria’s exclusive economic zone” and “a blatant violation of international law and maritime law”, and then said the thing that matters more: “if this war is not halted in time, it will continue to expand its geographical reach and increase risks, including the risk of a global conflict”. Note what he did not do, which was name Russia. Ursula von der Leyen said the Black Sea situation was “increasingly concerning” and that attacks on civilian shipping were unacceptable, “with lives already lost at sea”. This is the third civilian vessel struck in three days: a Turkish-owned ship carrying corn was sunk inside Romania’s economic zone on Sunday, which Volodymyr Zelensky attributed to two Russian drones, and a Marshall Islands-flagged ship was hit near Odesa today, killing a crew member. The pattern is a war spreading sideways into the economic zones of EU member states, and the commercial consequence is already visible — Mr Radev noted that the disruption is “driving up insurance costs even further”, which is how a drone strike at sea reaches a European shelf. Of the rescued crew, eleven were Turkish and seven Indian. Watch whether Sofia or Brussels formally attributes the attack.

Nearly 90,000 March in France as Half the Schools Are Blockaded

Some 86,000 people marched this morning in Marseille, Nantes, Lyon and elsewhere, with tens of thousands more expected in Paris, as 1,800 of France’s 3,700 high schools were blockaded or closed. Official figures put the toll of the past week at 715 police officers, 215 teenagers and 85 teaching staff injured, with 6,100 arrests, almost all of minors, and more than 1,800 prosecutions.

Dive deeper
Of those being prosecuted, 1,483 are under eighteen. That number, more than any other, is what has turned a dispute about overcrowded classrooms and decaying buildings into something the government cannot easily settle. Two injuries have become emblems: the fifteen-year-old whose hand was blown off in northern France on Monday, and a sixteen-year-old in Tours who lost the use of his right eye last Wednesday, where the prosecutor Catherine Sorita-Minard said the wounds were “compatible with the impact of a defensive bullet of the blast ball type”. The interior minister Laurent Nuñez says ten internal investigations into police conduct are open. Anne Savinel-Barras of Amnesty France said the images and testimony “raise alarming concerns about the dangerous use of teargas, batons and policing techniques”. A seventeen-year-old in Paris put the asymmetry plainly: “We’re teenagers with wheelie bins, and the police have heavy weapons.” The adult unions are now moving in behind the pupils — the CGT has told members to protect them, and the Green leader asked parents to wear white and come and stand with their children. Twenty-four schools have been burned or ransacked. The placards are consciously quoting 1968. Watch whether the teaching unions call a strike of their own.

The AfD Takes a State Speakership, a First Since the War

Tobias Rausch, 35, has been elected speaker of the Saxony-Anhalt parliament, the first time a far-right party has held the post in post-war Germany. His party fell three seats short of a majority, so he needed nine votes from other benches: five came from the hard-left BSW and four from members who have not been identified. “The days of political firewalls should be a thing of the past,” he said.

Dive deeper
The four unidentified votes are the whole story, because Friedrich Merz has ruled out cooperation with the AfD and fourteen of the Christian Democrats’ fifteen state members took part in the ballot, of whom five were meant to abstain. Whether any of them voted for Mr Rausch is unconfirmed and is now the question in Magdeburg. The post itself is largely ceremonial — chairing debates, controlling access to the building, representing the chamber — but the symbolism is the point, and the party made a related point before the vote by successfully arguing to be moved from the right fringe of the chamber to its centre. The AfD in Saxony-Anhalt is classified as right-wing extremist by Germany’s domestic intelligence service; it is pro-Russian, anti-immigration, and has proposed segregating child refugees in schools. More than three hundred protesters outside carried banners reading “Never again is now”. Susan Sziborra-Seidlitz of the Greens said a line had been crossed and that “we will not sit by when rights are curtailed”. The serious test comes in December, when the local AfD leader Ulrich Siegmund seeks the state premiership and needs 42 of 83 seats. Watch whether the Christian Democrats can account for every one of their votes.

Tories Would Halve Employer National Insurance for Under-25s

Kemi Badenoch will cut employer national insurance on 21 to 24-year-olds from 15% to 7.5%, at a static cost of £3.25 billion falling to £2.3 billion by 2029-30 once extra receipts and lower benefit spending are counted. Earnings above £50,270 would still attract the full rate. A savings plan to pay for it is promised tomorrow.

Dive deeper
The policy fixes a genuine cliff edge: employer national insurance currently jumps from zero to the full rate the day a worker turns 21. In cash, an employer would keep £1,125 a year on a 21-year-old earning £20,000, £1,875 at £30,000 and £3,395 at £50,270 or above, an average of £1,545 a head — so a firm with ten young graduates on £30,000 keeps £18,750. Mrs Badenoch framed it as discipline rather than giveaway: “We are being fiscally credible. We find savings and then we spend it. If I could afford to cut it for everybody, I promise you that I would. But young people have a much worse problem than everybody else,” citing a graduate recruitment crisis. The party calls employer national insurance “a tax on jobs” and says Labour’s £25 billion increase hit young workers hardest. The awkward part is the sequencing. The savings plan does not arrive until Wednesday, which means the leader has announced £2.3 billion of spending the day before explaining where it comes from, and her shadow work and pensions secretary spent this afternoon trailing the answer — Helen Whately said £170 billion a year goes on working-age welfare, “two and a half times more than we spend on defence”. It also sits alongside last week’s plan to bar 16 to 24-year-olds who have not worked for six months from universal credit. Watch tomorrow’s arithmetic.

A Seventh Arrest Over the RAF Fairford Incident

A 22-year-old British man has been arrested in Westminster on suspicion of preparing terrorist acts under section 5 of the Terrorism Act. He is the seventh person held over the 27 September incident at the Gloucestershire airbase and nobody has yet been charged. Five men aged 23 to 25 and a 25-year-old dual Iranian-British national were all previously bailed.

Dive deeper
Vicki Evans, the senior national coordinator for Counter Terrorism Policing, said the investigation remains live and that teams are “working around the clock, and at pace, to identify the motivation behind events in Gloucestershire”. That sentence is the admission underneath today’s arrest: more than a week on, and after the United States pulled every bomber out of the base, the motive is still not established. The original five were detained at Whelford in the early hours of 27 September after three suspicious vehicles were reported heading for the base, a major incident was declared and dozens of homes evacuated; police defended bailing them as “an investigative decision” grounded in experience. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards described Fairford as a legitimate target in July, and ministers have been briefed that Tehran uses proxies, sometimes unwitting ones, to consume resources and create disruption. The Iranian embassy in London “categorically rejects and strongly condemns the recent unfounded and malicious speculations”. United States European Command said today it would be inappropriate to discuss intelligence but that American forces remain at the highest state of readiness. The 28-day bail periods on the first five expire late this month. Watch whether anyone is charged before they do.

Three Iranians on Trial Over a Plot Against London Journalists

Mostafa Sepahvand, 41, Farhad Javadi Manesh, 46, and Shapoor Qalehali Khani Noori, 57, went on trial at Woolwich Crown Court accused of assisting a foreign intelligence service under the National Security Act. The prosecution says at least two journalists at the Persian-language channel Iran International were under surveillance and that one was to be subjected to serious violence.

Dive deeper
Bill Emlyn Jones KC, prosecuting, told the court the aim was retribution and deterrence — “to try to silence and deter others who might dare to voice their criticism” — and that “the defendants were engaged in what is very obviously a form of spying”. Sepahvand is alleged to have been the one who would carry out the attack, with the other two conducting surveillance and reconnaissance; images of the journalist Mojtaba Pourmohsen, his workplace and a hotel where he had lived were said to have been found in a briefing document on his phone. The men are also alleged to have held a briefing sheet on the barrister Jonathan Turner. Sepahvand does not deny involvement but says he acted out of fear, which the prosecution calls “an entirely bogus claim”, arguing he did it for money and to have an Iranian theft conviction fixed; the other two are expected to say they did not know who they were working for. Tehran has designated Iran International, which is headquartered in London, a terrorist organisation. Coming on the same day as a seventh Fairford arrest, it is the second Iran-linked security case to move through the British system in twelve hours. Watch whether the Crown names a tasking agency.

Badenoch Would Rather Fight a Second Election Than Deal With Reform

Asked what she would do in a hung parliament, the Conservative leader said: “If there is a hung parliament, then we can have another election. We had this in 1974.” Pressed on whether she would genuinely prefer a second election to a coalition with Reform, she replied: “I would rather have another election, yes.” She declined to confirm whether tomorrow’s speech will contain an inheritance tax cut.

Dive deeper
The 1974 precedent is doing a lot of work. Both elections that year produced governments without a stable majority, and the second was called within eight months by a prime minister seeking one — which is a thin advertisement for the strategy, but it is at least a real one. The remark matters because it closes off, in public and on the record, the arrangement that most arithmetic currently points to. On inheritance tax she defended a cut against the objection that only one estate in twenty pays it: “Inheritance tax right now has dragged in a lot of people who weren’t covered before. The wealthiest people don’t pay it, they have all these trusts and so on. It really affects the middle class.” On the prime minister she was dismissive — “he’s not applying to be everybody’s boyfriend” — and on reports of a half-empty hall she told Channel 5 she had “absolutely no idea what you’re talking about. This conference is buzzing.” Robert Jenrick, speaking for Reform, spent the afternoon attacking her from the other direction over the state pension. Her leader’s speech is tomorrow, and she gave two different times for it in two different interviews. Watch whether the inheritance tax cut and the savings plan appear in the same speech.

A Million Britons Who Never Smoked Now Vape

Some 1.1 million people who have never smoked regularly now vape, up from about 940,000 last year, according to official figures. For the second year running more people over 16 vape than smoke — 5.4 million against 4.9 million, with smoking down from 5.3 million. The heaviest-vaping group is now 25 to 34-year-olds rather than the young adults below them.

Dive deeper
The shift in age is the interesting part. At 16.4%, those aged 25 to 34 have overtaken 16 to 24-year-olds, which the statistician David Mais said “may reflect existing users getting older, growing popularity among this age group, or a combination of both” — the difference between a habit ageing with a cohort and a habit spreading into a new one. Smoking continues to fall: fewer than one adult in ten now smokes, the steepest decline is among 18 to 24-year-olds, and half of current smokers say they intend to quit. Hazel Cheeseman of Action on Smoking and Health said smoking “remains the biggest driver of preventable death in the UK. Now is the time to finish the job.” Kruti Shrotri of Cancer Research UK put the trade-off carefully: “Although we don’t know the long-term risks of vaping, evidence so far shows that legal vapes are less harmful than tobacco and an effective tool to stop smoking.” The million never-smokers are the group that argument does not cover. Legislation taking effect in the new year permanently bars tobacco sales to anyone born on or after 1 January 2009, so the smoking line will keep falling by law. Watch whether the vaping line keeps rising with it.
