GEO Geopolitical

A Tanker Is Attacked in Hormuz and Eleven Indians Hurt

Oman’s air force has evacuated ten crew from the Panamanian-flagged On Peace after a projectile struck it nine nautical miles off Limah on Tuesday, setting the vessel alight. India’s foreign ministry says at least twelve of the nineteen crew were injured, eleven of them Indian nationals; seventeen of the crew were Indian. No group has claimed the attack.

Dive deeper
The shipping intelligence service Marisks counts at least seven other tanker incidents in the past week. Iran effectively closed the waterway when the war began on 28 February, has repeatedly attacked vessels that do not use its designated route, and says it wants to charge ships for passage. India, the largest single supplier of seafarers to merchant shipping, said in July that at least ten Indian sailors had been killed since late February; another Hormuz attack in September killed one more. The counter-intuitive part is the cargo. Middle East crude exports are back to pre-war levels — a seven-day moving average of 18.3 million barrels a day on 30 September, against about 18 million a day in the twelve months before the war, on provisional Kpler figures. What has not normalised is the price: Brent closed Tuesday at $101.51 against $72.48 the day before the war began. That gap is insurance, detours and risk premium rather than any shortage of barrels. The evacuated crew were treated at Khasab hospital in Musandam. Watch for attribution, because an unclaimed strike on a Panamanian hull with a mostly Indian crew pushes Delhi towards a posture it has spent eight months avoiding.

Three Years On From 7 October, With All Hostages Home

Memorials are being held at the Nova festival site near Kibbutz Reim and at intersections and bomb shelters across southern Israel, three years after the Hamas-led attack that killed about 1,200 people and took 251 hostage. It is the first anniversary with every hostage returned. A flag flew at half mast at Kibbutz Kfar Aza this morning.

Dive deeper
Elkana Bohbot, 37, abducted from the Nova festival and released after 738 days, is marking the day in Israel for the first time. He says he was held fifty metres underground in a dark, damp tunnel, in a small cell with other hostages, fed very little, allowed to bathe only every few weeks, and forced into propaganda videos, one of them a staged call to his family. In August his wife Rivka gave birth to a daughter, Michele, fulfilling a promise he made to himself in the tunnels. Gaza’s health ministry, which does not separate civilians from combatants but says around half the dead were women and children, counts more than 74,000 killed; United Nations agencies and independent experts regard its figures as broadly reliable. Since the ceasefire took effect on 10 October last year the ministry counts 1,145 Palestinians killed, and seven Israeli soldiers have died in the same period. The politics sits underneath the grief. The government held its official service on 5 October, the Hebrew-calendar date, and the split commemorations track the argument over Benjamin Netanyahu’s conduct of the war and his reluctance to order an inquiry into the failures of the day itself. An Israeli election falls at the end of this month.

The Deadline to Shut Britain’s Jerusalem Consulate Falls Tomorrow

Thursday marks thirty days since Israel’s foreign minister Gideon Saar ordered the closure of the British consulate general in East Jerusalem, in retaliation for Britain’s sanctions on illegal West Bank settlements. The national security adviser Jonathan Powell has held undisclosed talks in Israel without reaching an agreement. The Foreign Office did not respond to a request for comment.

Dive deeper
Sir Vincent Fean, British consul general in Jerusalem from 2010 to 2014 and in effect the ambassador to the Palestinian territories, calls it “not the act of a friend or ally” and holds the closure to be unlawful: “Israel does not have the right to interfere with the diplomatic relations between the state of Palestine and the state of the United Kingdom. It is doing so by closing our office, and that is unlawful.” He is particularly concerned for the fifty or so Palestinian staff. A Britain Palestine Project brief endorsed by the former attorney general Dominic Grieve calls the order “an unlawful assertion of sovereignty over occupied Palestinian territory”. Security Council Resolution 478, passed in 1980 with Britain a permanent member, held Israeli attempts to alter the status of Jerusalem “null and void”; Resolution 2334 restated it in 2016. Britain recognised the state of Palestine in September 2025. Israel has also expelled Britain from the American-led coordination centre for post-war Gaza and barred eleven parliamentarians, Jeremy Corbyn and Zarah Sultana among them. Reports on Monday that Britain could expel twenty-seven Israeli diplomats are unverified. Fean’s practical warning is the one to watch: the consulate depends on Israeli electricity and water.

A Quarter of a Million March in France’s School Revolt

The interior ministry counted 266,000 demonstrators across France on Tuesday, 56,000 of them in Paris, in the largest day of student protest in decades. Nearly 500 people were detained, taking the total past 6,500 since the movement began, most of them minors. Water cannon were used in Rennes, tear gas in Paris and Marseille.

Dive deeper
The student union Union Syndicale Lycéenne says 2,000 secondary schools were at a standstill and every university mobilised. What has radicalised the movement is the injuries. A fifteen-year-old lost a hand in Lens on Monday after picking up a police grenade, which a police union source said appeared to be a sting-ball round fired by officers. The interior minister Laurent Nuñez called the police response proportionate in “almost all cases”, said the protests had at times turned into riots, and put the number of officers injured at 715. The prime minister Sébastien Lecornu denounced “ultra violence” threatening schools, pledged that any “accidents” would be investigated, and called the charge that the police are racist a “lie”. The grievances themselves are unglamorous and old. It began at a single school in a Paris suburb on 21 September over staffing and buildings: “In Saint-Denis, just right outside of Paris, high schools see the ceilings falling apart, water in the walls, rats,” one protester said. Fresh protests are called for Thursday, and the movement is now a live issue in a presidential campaign that runs to next spring.

Puigdemont Can Go Home, and May Hold Spain’s Balance

Spain’s Constitutional Court has ruled that the amnesty law for Catalan separatists covers the embezzlement charges against Carles Puigdemont, and a Supreme Court judge lifted his arrest warrant on Tuesday. He fled to Belgium in 2017 after the independence referendum Madrid declared illegal, and has defeated several extradition attempts since. No date has been given for his return.

Dive deeper
Jordi Turull, secretary-general of Junts, put it as a verdict on the state rather than a court ruling: “They held firm, they resisted, they prevailed in Europe, and today they have prevailed against the Spanish state.” Pedro Sánchez says he does not regret the amnesty and welcomed the decision — “the important message is that of coexistence among Catalans” — which is easier to say now than it was in 2023, when Junts votes were the only reason he could form a government, given in exchange for those amnesties. Junts withdrew its support a year ago and was decisive in the government losing two votes this week on emergency housing measures, taken amid street protests over rents; Sánchez responded by calling a snap election for November, betting that housing anger mobilises his own side. The arithmetic is where it becomes interesting. The conservative People’s Party leads the polls but may need Junts and other centre-right parties to govern, so the politician Madrid spent eight years trying to extradite could end up deciding who runs Spain. A Junts official says he is unlikely to stand himself.

UK Domestic

Badenoch Promises a Battle for the Soul of the Nation

In extracts released before her leader’s speech today, Kemi Badenoch casts the next election as “a battle for the soul of this nation” and accuses the government of believing “that your income, your pension and your savings are theirs to take and then hand out as they see fit”. The funding plan for Monday’s £2.3bn national insurance cut is published alongside it.

Dive deeper
What has been trailed and what has been confirmed are not the same thing, which is worth holding on to all day. The Times reports she will announce a cut to the 40% inheritance tax rate, which bites above £325,000, or £500,000 where a home passes to children or grandchildren. The deputy leader Alex Burghart would not confirm it on BBC Breakfast: “Kemi has said she’d like to abolish inheritance tax but it’s very important we can afford any announcement we can make”, with the shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith still working out what is possible. The Times also reports the tax cuts would be funded by £30bn of welfare reductions, a figure today’s costings will either carry or quietly drop. Monday’s measure takes employer national insurance from 15% to 7.5% for 21 to 24-year-olds, against a backdrop of about a million young people not in education, employment or training. Griffith wants to scrap at least one tax every Budget and has the High Value Council Tax Surcharge, due in 2028 on English homes worth over £2m, in his sights. John Healey answers that his own promises are “built on the rock of fiscal discipline”. The Budget is on 28 October.

Healey Lines Up Over £1bn Against January’s Energy Bills

The chancellor is preparing an intervention worth more than £1bn at the 28 October Budget, most of it a bigger discount for households on certain benefits, after forecasts that the war with Iran will push the January price cap up by as much as £442. The most likely route is raising the warm homes discount from £150 by a further £100.

Dive deeper
A £442 rise would entirely erode the VAT cut Andy Burnham announced on becoming prime minister, which is why the Treasury has moved at all. Mr Healey is expected to rebuff the energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, who wants levies stripped from bills altogether — worth up to £120 off every bill, but costing as much as £3.2bn. Officials are working up two options for after the Budget: a social tariff, under which poorer households pay less per unit, and a rising block tariff, with a cheap essential block and a dearer one above it, a design the think tank Fahnbulleh used to run first proposed. Alex Chapman of the New Economics Foundation: “A serious price spike is coming, and without action, bills will exceed the peak of the last crisis.” Alfie Stirling of the Joseph Rowntree Foundation argues support “needs to support the majority of households” and should lower the price itself, thereby bringing down inflation. Against that, the chancellor has £4.7bn of extra defence spending to find and a fiscal buffer to rebuild, and is reported to be eyeing higher bank taxes. The Treasury would not engage. Watch what households above the benefit threshold are offered, because on this plan the answer is nothing.

Holborn and St Pancras Votes Tomorrow With Labour Ahead

Zack Polanski, the Green leader, faces Labour’s Sagal Abdi-Wali in the seat Keir Starmer vacated on 1 September. Labour has held it since its creation in 1983 and is the favourite; Polanski calls the contest “really on a knife-edge” and the deputy Labour leader Lucy Powell says a Green win is “unlikely”. Labour figures are now ruling out ever working with the Greens.

Dive deeper
Green members voted 1,022 to 813 in Brighton on Sunday to treat Zionism as racism and call for a single Palestinian state, against the wishes of four of the party’s five MPs. Labour’s chair Bridget Phillipson called it “shameful”; one minister cannot foresee the parties working together and a government official wants a coalition formally ruled out. Senior Liberal Democrats want their Southwark coalition ended, the peer Lynne Featherstone saying: “I would ditch them. Grow a pair!” The motion’s proposer, the British-Palestinian artist Lubna Speitan, was named in 2015 United States court filings as a “close friend” of Minh Quang Pham, jailed for forty years in 2016 for supporting al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula; she offered to testify for his defence about his mental state. The Greens say she holds no official position and that they take allegations of this nature seriously; she has not responded to the BBC. Ms Abdi-Wali, 42, leads Camden council and came to Britain from Somalia as a child. The local grievance is HS2, which permanently takes 14,404 square metres of green space and playgrounds from the constituency for a line now due to open between 2040 and 2043.

Ten Military Training Sites Face Their First Independent Inquiry

Polly Miller-Perkins, the new Armed Forces Commissioner and a former air commodore, has opened her first investigation: into ten training establishments in England, among them the Army Foundation College at Harrogate, Sandhurst and Britannia Royal Naval College. It follows four young women telling the BBC in August that they were sexually assaulted and harassed at Harrogate.

Dive deeper
One said she was raped by several fellow junior soldiers in an attack that lasted hours. Harrogate trains under-18s, which is what makes the oversight question sharp rather than merely serious. Miller-Perkins calls the allegations “absolutely appalling” and says the reporting “brought into sharp focus” potential problems including predatory behaviour towards women: “I already had concerns, but there’s absolutely no doubt that your report reinforced these concerns.” On the first phase of training she is blunt that “it’s up to the MoD to bring those individuals up to the values and standards that would be expected in a modern armed forces — and that includes respect for women.” She visits all ten sites in November, can arrive unannounced and compel information, and expects early findings in the new year. The defence secretary Wes Streeting says he expects every woman who serves “to be treated with respect, with decency”; the ministry welcomed independent scrutiny and noted the college’s outstanding Ofsted report. The counterweight is a survivor with no confidence in the process at all: “military life is ingrained in you — it’s hard to step away from that and see the faults.” The commissioner’s answer: “I understand trust has to be earned.”

Equinor Asks Whether Britain Is Still Worth Investing In

Anders Opedal, chief executive of the Norwegian state oil company, says that if drilling at Rosebank and Jackdaw is refused, “the question will be: is the UK investable in the future? I hope it will not come to that.” He expects approval, and calls the present limbo “an uncomfortable position to be in”.

Dive deeper
Rosebank is the largest undeveloped field on the United Kingdom continental shelf, eighty miles north-west of Shetland, thought to hold up to 500 million barrels. It was licensed in 2001, discovered in 2004 and given a final investment decision in 2023; both it and Jackdaw were approved by the last government, then stalled by a Scottish court ruling on the need to weigh climate impact. The consultation closed in August and the decision now rests with the energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, despite a manifesto pledge against new licences. Adura, the Equinor and Shell venture that operates both fields, says Jackdaw is 99% complete and could deliver gas this winter if approved soon; Ithaca owns a fifth of Rosebank. Opedal frames the whole thing as politics rather than geology: “the same geology on both sides of the border — several fields actually cross it.” Tessa Khan of Uplift answers that Rosebank “won’t cut our bills” and is a “bad deal for Britain”. Domestic production is forecast to halve by 2035, and Norway already supplies half of Britain’s gas. The timing is its own tell — the decision was reported last month as likely to wait until after tomorrow’s byelection.
