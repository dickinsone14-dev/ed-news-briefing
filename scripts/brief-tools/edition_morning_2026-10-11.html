GEO Geopolitical

Twelve Killed at Riyadh Airport in the Third Strike This Week

Saudi Arabia’s civil aviation authority says twelve people were killed and 309 wounded when a missile hit the terminal complex at King Khalid International at about three on Saturday afternoon. The dead include four Saudis, two Bangladeshis and one national each from America, Jordan, Palestine, Syria, Sudan and Egypt. The coalition calls it a war crime.

Dive deeper
One thing needs saying carefully: the Houthis have not claimed this attack. They claimed Thursday’s, which killed three including a Saudia pilot, and the head of their media office posted that the group had “shut down Riyadh international airport” — but on Saturday’s strike itself they have said nothing. Saudi Arabia and the American State Department attribute it to them. Major General Turki al-Malki, the coalition spokesman, called it a “full-fledged war crime” and promised to “respond decisively… in accordance with the Customary International Humanitarian Law”, and confirmed a projectile also fell near King Fahd International at Dammam, the alternative hub. The BBC’s Middle East correspondent reported hospitals overwhelmed and called it the deadliest single attack on a Gulf Arab state since the war with Iran began in February. Around 580 flights were cancelled on Sunday and Riyadh’s schools move to remote learning for a week. António Guterres is “deeply shocked”; the United Nations envoy for Yemen called it “a grave escalation”; Marco Rubio confirmed an American was among the dead. The detail that bears on Britain: Saudi Arabia is running low on interceptor missiles and has asked Britain, France, Pakistan and Egypt for air defence support, and Britain is reported to be sending an aircraft to refuel Saudi jets.

Trump Says Ukraine Needs a New Leader to Make a Deal

Volodymyr Zelensky “wants to make problems for the world” by authorising strikes on Russian refineries, the president said outside the White House, adding: “He’d better damn well stop.” Ukraine should “get a new leader who can make a deal”. Andy Burnham telephoned Mr Zelensky to offer “complete solidarity”.

Dive deeper
The full remark sets out the bargain he believes was broken: “We said, ‘you can do whatever you want to Russia, don’t hit the refineries’, because that’s a world problem that’s caused… He could have made many deals and for some reason, he never does.” Ukraine has been under martial law since 2022 and elections are suspended, so the call for a new leader has no constitutional route. Russian refinery output is down an estimated 30% after Ukrainian drone strikes, which is the context for Friday’s suspension of American sanctions on Russian diesel until 7 April. Putin’s investment envoy Kirill Dmitriev called the intervention “iconic and historic”. The European Union’s Kaja Kallas took the opposite view — suspending sanctions “provides Moscow with more revenues to wage war… This is not the time to ease pressure on Russia, and Europe won’t” — and European foreign ministers intend to approve their largest sanctions package since the invasion on Monday. Downing Street’s readout of the Burnham call records that the prime minister recognised the last twenty-four hours had been difficult, pointed to new British designations announced this week, and agreed with Mr Zelensky that Russia should accept an immediate energy ceasefire. The two will meet in person within days. On Saturday, Russian glide bombs killed at least twenty people in Zaporizhzhia, four of them children.

Trump Says America May Join Saudi Strikes on the Houthis

Asked on his way to a rally whether the United States would join, the president said: “We may. We’re gonna look at it. We just found out about the recent attack, so we’ll make a decision. We’ll move very quickly.” Asked whether Riyadh changed his pledge not to strike Iran before the midterms, he said only that he would take a look.

Dive deeper
It is worth being precise about what this is, because the wording invites over-reading: an answer to a shouted question on the way to a campaign event in Tennessee, not a prepared statement and not a reported decision. What makes it newsworthy is what it would reverse. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman asked Mr Trump twice last month to strike the Houthis and was refused both times. Washington instead met the group in Oman to protect a separate understanding: the Houthis have said their war is with Saudi Arabia alone and have let American vessels transit the Red Sea unmolested. Joining Saudi strikes would put that at risk, in the same week the administration suspended sanctions on Russian diesel to bring American fuel prices down before an election. On Saudi capability he added: “They’re able to defend. The Saudis are good fighters. But I think the attack was a terrible thing.” He said he would speak to the crown prince soon. The decision, if it comes, lands on a country that has just told its allies it is running short of interceptors.

An American Jet Disables a Cargo Ship in the Gulf of Oman

A United States fighter fired a precision munition into the stern of the Panama-flagged MV Ocean Molica, also known as the Arika Sun, after the crew “ignored repeated warnings and attempted to run the ongoing naval blockade against Iran”. Central Command says the strike disabled the propulsion without harming the crew.

Dive deeper
Since the blockade resumed on 14 July, Central Command says it has disabled four commercial vessels, turned around 135 ships and destroyed ten tankers linked to what it calls the Revolutionary Guard’s multibillion-dollar shadow network. The British maritime trade operations agency acknowledged the incident, reporting “an incident involving a tanker and military forces in the Gulf of Oman”. Senator Chris Murphy put the objection on the record: “What is this all for? Why are taxpayers paying billions to hit commercial ships?… Apparently the goal is to gain leverage in order to convince the Iranians to let us pay them billions to return everything to the way it all was before the war. What a disaster.” A separate incident should not be merged with this one: the Revolutionary Guard says it struck the gas carrier NV Sunshine in the Strait of Hormuz for taking an unauthorised route, and claims Iranian forces have “taken control of the administration of the strait”. The maritime agency logged an outbound tanker hit on the port side by an unknown projectile with a fire aboard and the crew safe. One is America stopping a ship; the other is Iran hitting one. Both happened in the same stretch of water in the same twenty-four hours, which is the point.

Hurricane Isaias Kills Four and Cuts Power to 670,000

The first Atlantic hurricane of the season made landfall near Destin in Florida late on Friday as a category two storm with winds of 105mph. Four people are dead, two in Florida and two in Alabama, two of them killed by trees falling on their homes. About 670,000 homes across four states lost power.

Dive deeper
Joey Williams told an American broadcaster that his wife was killed when a tree came through their roof as they sheltered, having gone out to photograph the damage: “I was able to squeeze out. My wife didn’t make it.” Alabama’s governor Kay Ivey confirmed two deaths in her state and warned about spinoff tornadoes; Florida’s governor had some 16,000 utility linemen standing by. Outages peaked at roughly 670,000 across Florida, Alabama, Georgia and Mississippi at midday on Saturday and had fallen to about 500,000 by the evening. The reason this matters at a British pump is in the energy numbers rather than the weather. Sixty-nine per cent of American Gulf of Mexico oil output and 57% of gas production remained shut in on Saturday, with 113 platforms still evacuated, and the Energy Department has authorised an emergency release of up to four million barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. American diesel already averages $6.28 a gallon against $3.68 a year ago. That is a second supply shock in a week in which Washington suspended sanctions on Russian diesel specifically to bring the price down, and in which twelve people were killed at a Gulf airport.

UK Domestic

DUP MP Says Her Party Can Remove O’Neill as First Minister

“We have it within our gift to ensure that she is actually removed from that position,” Carla Lockhart said outside Drumcree Church, accusing Michelle O’Neill of “breaking the law flagrantly on the Garvaghy Road” and saying it “cannot be business as usual at Stormont”. Her own party leader has ruled out collapsing power-sharing.

Dive deeper
The mechanism is the part that makes this a threat rather than a plan. Under the Stormont rules the DUP cannot remove a Sinn Féin first minister directly; the office falls only if the deputy first minister resigns. That is Emma Little-Pengelly, and both she and the party leader Gavin Robinson said over the weekend that they would not bring the institutions down. So the MP for Upper Bann is at odds with her own leadership, and when asked whether she still had confidence in it she declined to answer. The anger she describes is real enough: “They’re angry at the chief constable for allowing lawlessness to win, for allowing the mob to dictate,” and at Ms O’Neill as “this self-proclaimed ‘first minister for all’… thumbing her nose at unionism and at the Orange Order and at this community”. The sequence behind it: the Parades Commission cleared a restricted march along the Garvaghy Road for the first time in twenty-eight years, thousands of residents including Sinn Féin leaders blocked it, police stopped the parade, and the Northern Ireland Secretary then banned it outright on Friday. Ms O’Neill is among those who have received police letters about the protest and has said she was “proud” to stand with residents.

MPs Move to Strip Police of Immunity From Being Sued

A cross-party amendment from Andrew Mitchell and Jess Phillips, debated on Tuesday, would overturn a 2025 Court of Appeal ruling that made officers immune from civil claims over conduct during criminal investigations. The ruling blocks most claims, including for serious human rights abuses and data breaches, with no exemption for unjustifiable conduct.

Dive deeper
The case that produced it is the clearest argument for changing it. A domestic violence victim fled to a secret refuge; Sussex Police put her new address into a file for the Crown Prosecution Service without adequately marking it confidential, a lawyer read it aloud at a bail hearing, and she had to flee again, saying the refuge “no longer felt safe at all”. Mr Mitchell says that if Parliament fails “to close this legal loophole there will be devastating consequences for victims”. Kate Ellis of the Centre for Women’s Justice describes the practical effect: forces can now argue they cannot be “held legally responsible at all for serious mistakes made in a criminal investigation”, creating an accountability gap in which a force that fails to investigate a rapist, stalker or domestic abuser “faces no consequences”. Also relying on the immunity is the Metropolitan Police, in a claim brought by Ali Kololo, a Kenyan woodcutter who served eleven years after a wrongful conviction over a 2011 attack on two British tourists. The government says the home secretary and lord chancellor have commissioned a review into police accountability and that any change “should be considered alongside the wider findings”. A Supreme Court appeal on the same point is listed this month. Sussex Police and the National Police Chiefs’ Council declined to comment.

Union Leader Wants Exams Moved Out of the Hottest Months

“Perhaps we don’t want exams to be happening in the hottest part of the year,” the general secretary of the National Education Union has said, calling for a serious discussion about moving the school year or lengthening the summer holiday. Classroom temperatures passed 40C in June and at least 760,000 pupils missed lessons.

Dive deeper
Daniel Kebede puts the scale of the disruption at more than fifteen million lost lesson hours during that heatwave alone, and describes the response from members in terms he has used only once before: “The last time I felt that level of panic in the profession was during the Covid pandemic. I was being routinely contacted by members in distress.” His argument is not really about weather but about building stock. Most English schools are Victorian or date from the 1960s and 1970s, built, as he puts it, for a different climate, and he wants a mass retrofitting programme before next summer — which forecasters expect to be hotter still, with the strongest El Niño in living memory peaking at the end of this year. The inequality point is the one that will decide whether this becomes policy: without retrofitting, he warns, “you will have a two-tier system where schools in more affluent areas are able to cope with extreme heat”. A school with air conditioning sits exams in June; a school without one sends pupils home. Moving the exam season would be among the largest changes to the English school year in decades, and no minister has endorsed it. This is one union leader’s proposal, reported by one newspaper, and should be read as the opening of an argument rather than the end of one.

A Decade On, Plan 2 Graduates Are Still Paying Interest

Three friends who started at the same university in 2012, the year fees tripled to £9,000, each borrowed about £37,500. One has paid it off by borrowing from family, one is chipping away, and one on £72,000 a year has watched her balance sit near £47,000. Debts are written off after thirty years.

Dive deeper
The arithmetic is the story. Charlotte, a physiotherapist earning about £50,000: “Since April this year, I’ve paid off in the region of £450 and I’ve accrued over £500 in interest.” Libby, a project manager on £72,000: “It feels like I will never pay it off, so it’s something that I’m kind of just sucking up until the loan is written off.” Lizzy cleared hers by borrowing from her family and reckons she saved about £20,000 — and names what that means: “Buying your way out of the system or not being in at all is in itself a luxury. It buys you freedom.” The Institute for Fiscal Studies found in February that a Plan 2 graduate needs to earn around £63,000 before a £50,000 balance begins to fall; average earnings are £39,039. The live policy question is the repayment threshold, which is set to be frozen for three years, meaning graduates start repaying sooner and pay more each month, and campaigners are watching the Budget on 28 October. Martin Lewis has warned most graduates should not be panicked into overpaying. A BBC investigation in March found the Department for Education had compared repayments to “£30-a-month phone contracts” in school presentations a decade ago.

Flood and Drought Taskforce Meets With 6.3m Properties at Risk

A new Flood and Drought Resilience Taskforce holds its first meeting on Wednesday, chaired by the floods and drought minister Emma Hardy and bringing together more than fifty organisations including the Environment Agency, the Met Office, mayoral authorities, fire services and the farming, water and insurance industries. Some 6.3 million English homes and businesses are at risk of flooding.

Dive deeper
The framing is deliberate and worth noticing: this is a flood and drought body, on the argument that the two are now intertwined rather than opposite problems. Ms Hardy: “We’re facing extreme weather, with flood and drought intertwined, but the government is taking decisive action… This will put communities and businesses at the heart of our work and will make sure the UK is ready for whatever the weather brings next.” The money behind it is at least £10.5bn committed between 2024 and 2036, of which £4.2bn is for existing and new defences and £1.4bn is being spent in the coming year, with £65m for farmers. The return the government claims is roughly £8 of avoided damage for every £1 spent. The backdrop is a year that delivered both halves of the problem: summer 2026 was the hottest on record and brought the third drought in five years and the driest July in almost two centuries, while last winter brought storms Bram, Goretti, Ingrid and Chandra and left England with 42% more rain than its seasonal average. The practical instruction for households is unglamorous and free: check your flood risk, sign up for alerts and write a flood plan before the weather turns.
