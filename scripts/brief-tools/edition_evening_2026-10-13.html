Saudi Arabia Bombs Yemen’s Oil Depots as Aramco Is Threatened

Saudi aircraft struck the Sabaha oil depot on the western edge of Sanaa and hit storage at Ras Isa port in ten waves, after issuing an evacuation warning and publishing a map of the target. Within the hour the Houthis named Aramco’s facilities a legitimate target. No casualties have been reported.

Dive deeper
One detail cuts against the pictures: the Yemeni Oil Company says the storage tanks were empty when they were hit. The Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree told “all employees and citizens present near any facility affiliated with Aramco Petroleum in Riyadh to evacuate it and stay away from it”, naming the company’s Dammam headquarters, King Khalid International and a Riyadh refinery, and promising a “harsh and painful” reprisal. The Saudi cabinet, chaired by Mohammed bin Salman, said the Houthis would face “consequences”; the coalition says it intercepted three drones north of Riyadh. Baraa Shiban of the Royal United Services Institute explains why air dominance is not buying security: Houthi drones now “fly lower and are smaller, and are less easy to detect. They are cheap and it does not take many to get through to be effective.” Underneath it is a humanitarian emergency the strikes will not help — the World Health Organization says suspected cholera cases in Yemen have tripled in three weeks. Watch whether Riyadh’s airport, shut since Saturday, reopens as planned.

Republican Senators Demand the Russian Diesel Deal Is Torn Up

Six Republicans — among them Mitch McConnell, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and John Cornyn — have joined six Democrats to tell the president to rescind the agreement. “This deal is a gift to our adversary and a betrayal of our Allies,” they wrote. The licence permits nearly 36 million barrels through mid-April.

Dive deeper
Their argument is that the deal buys almost nothing at home while paying for the war abroad: it “will have a negligible impact on fuel prices, but the revenues the Kremlin will receive from this agreement will undoubtedly finance its desired war of attrition and advance its security cooperation with China, Iran, and North Korea”. They want the general licence revoked and the sanctions act the president signed this year actually used. Two volumes are circulating and they are not the same thing — the tranche announced on Friday was about 300,000 tonnes, some 2.25 million barrels and less than a day of American consumption, while the licence authorises nearly 36 million barrels. The president’s answer to the criticism was a social media post: “I made the right choice for the American People.” American diesel is averaging $6.26 a gallon, close to 80% higher than at the start of the year. The British relevance runs through the same barrel: diesel sets freight costs, and freight sets shelf prices.

Argentina Takes the Falklands Oil Dispute to a Tribunal

Buenos Aires has asked the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea to block drilling at the Sea Lion field, 130 miles from the islands, which holds an estimated 1.7 billion barrels and is due to start pumping in 2028. Britain has separately asked Interpol to reject Argentine arrest warrants against those involved.

Dive deeper
The field could triple the islands’ economy for a population of about 3,500, which is why the pressure is escalating on both tracks at once. Argentina has sought “red notices” against people linked to the project, including British nationals; the Foreign Office has written to Interpol’s headquarters in Lyon arguing they are politically motivated, and Interpol has replied that it will respect the article of its constitution forbidding political intervention. The foreign secretary Ed Miliband called the attempts “to intimidate businesses and individuals engaged in legitimate commercial activity… unacceptable”, and the minister Uma Kumaran told MPs this was “economic coercion” and that the filing “does not invalidate licenses issued by the Falkland Islands government”. British officials believe President Milei is picking the fight ahead of elections next year; he has called the United Nations “a useless organisation”. A draft Argentine bill would extend sanctions to shareholders, suppliers and the fishing industry. In the 2013 referendum, three islanders voted against remaining British.

Pope Leo’s Lung Operation Concluded Successfully

The Vatican says the surgery to remove a nodule from the 71-year-old’s lung “concluded successfully, on time and as expected, and the Holy Father returned to his room”. He announced the operation himself from the balcony after Sunday’s Angelus, saying the growth was “contained” and caught “at a very early stage”. His diary is clear to 20 October.

Dive deeper
Popes have historically concealed illness, which is why announcing it himself, in person, in St Peter’s Square, is the part that surprised Rome. Cardinal Pietro Parolin, the Vatican Secretary of State, was candid about it: “Am I worried? Yes, because I certainly wasn’t expecting anything like this” — while saying cardinals had been struck by the Pope’s “serenity”. Cardinal Kikuchi Tarcisio Isao of Tokyo said he had felt nothing wrong physically and had moved quickly because he is due in Latin America in early November, including Peru, where he served as a missionary and then bishop. Crowds waited outside the Gemelli hospital, where the thoracic surgery team operated. The Vatican is careful to say there were no symptoms and that nodules are frequently not cancerous, though they are tested once removed. What to watch is not the surgery but the diary: whether the Peru trip holds will say more about his recovery than any statement.

Kabila Takes Command of a New Congo Rebel Coalition

Joseph Kabila, who ruled the Democratic Republic of Congo for eighteen years, has launched the Front for the Refoundation of the Nation at his residence in Goma, the eastern city held by M23 since January 2025. He will lead it, with the rebels’ political chief among his deputies.

Dive deeper
He had previously denied having links to M23 at all, which is what makes the announcement a rupture rather than a development. An adviser, Claude Ibalanky, says the aim is to unite resistance forces “to put an end to tyranny by any means necessary, including the use of force”. The rebels already hold Goma and Bukavu, both airports and the crossings into Rwanda and Uganda, and a correspondent in Goma reports no sign of the government being able to dislodge them — with the added worry that soldiers may defect to a man who once commanded them. Yvon Muya of the University of Ottawa reads the timing as deliberate: “You don’t make an announcement like this without having prepared the actions that follow. We will likely witness an escalation on the military front.” Kabila took office at twenty-nine after his father’s assassination, was sentenced to death in absentia last year for treason and war crimes, which he calls political, and was sanctioned by Washington this year. Talks with M23 had been expected in Qatar next month.

MI5 Says Iran Is Now Aiming at British Military Sites

“The other major shift this year has been Iranian ambitions to come at harder targets, like military sites,” the director general Sir Ken McCallum said in his annual threat update, disclosing more than fifty Iran-linked arrests in a year. State threats have quadrupled in a decade and now occupy MI5 on roughly the same scale as terrorism.

Dive deeper
He was careful about capability in a way the headlines were not: what has actually been seen in Britain “has not been anywhere near” the level of sophistication the Revolutionary Guard deploys closer to home, because much of the work here is contracted to criminals. Much of the aggression, he said, has been aimed at British Jewish communities, “with whom I want to express solidarity”. On Russia he was blunter: with its covert network “eviscerated by almost 1,000 expulsions since the invasion”, the Kremlin is “scrambling to pay criminals to do their bidding” — “not the actions of a great power with the strategic upper hand”. The sharpest domestic trend is elsewhere: “extremist versions of anti-immigration and anti-Muslim grievance are tipping into terrorist intent”, with asylum hotels and immigration centres among the targets contemplated, and the caseload skewing young — MI5 investigated a radicalised thirteen-year-old whose case helped a foreign partner stop a lethal attack. A new AI Threat Assessment Centre is to “separate the bluster from the realities”.

MI5 Confirms the Brexit Attempt but Disputes the Effect

Russia did try to interfere around the 2016 referendum through state broadcasters and “trolls and bots and so forth”, the MI5 chief said, but “there is a really important difference between interference attempted and outcome swayed”. He stressed the result is “not in dispute” and warned against making the trolls “look 10ft tall”.

Dive deeper
This is an intelligence chief adjudicating a row that has run since the weekend, and it lands on both sides of it rather than settling it for either. He confirmed the attempt was real and on the record — Russian state outlets “came in pretty strongly around the referendum”, with a second line of interference through fake accounts — while declining to say it changed anything. He also agreed strongly with the prime minister about a hack-and-leak operation during the 2019 general election campaign, which is the firmer of the two claims. His warning was about the argument itself: if the debate runs for days more, “Russian trolls who didn’t appear to do all that much… will be delighted because they are front page news again”. The Conservative deputy leader Alex Burghart called it “an extraordinary slap down to the prime minister”, which overstates it: Downing Street had already said it did not believe the interference succeeded. Published studies put the reach of Russian state content in the hundreds of millions of impressions while estimates of active troll accounts range from 54 to 150,000 — uncertainty that is itself the point.

High Street Chiefs Ask for Cheaper Young Workers

Executives from Tesco, the Co-op, Costa Coffee, Marks and Spencer and Sainsbury’s met the chancellor at Downing Street this morning. They asked him to raise the point at which employers start paying National Insurance from £5,000 to £6,000, blaming last year’s cut for falling numbers of young and part-time staff.

Dive deeper
The threshold was lowered from £9,100 to £5,000, which pulled large numbers of part-time and entry-level jobs into employer National Insurance for the first time, and the retailers are asking for only part of that back. Helen Dickinson of the British Retail Consortium put the sector’s position in cost terms: “after whacking up retailers’ taxes by 20% in two years, including an extra £6.5bn in new employment costs, any new costs will be paid by consumers at the till and make it harder for retailers to create jobs”. They also want relief on business rates. One source close to the meeting said: “It felt like they were listening.” What is asserted rather than shown is the causal link to youth unemployment — no figure for jobs lost was put on the table, and the retailers did not provide one. The Budget is on 28 October, and the Institute for Fiscal Studies has already said a steady-as-she-goes Budget is not available to this chancellor.

Poundland in Rescue Talks With 12,000 Jobs at Stake

The management team is attempting a buyout of the chain, which has about 600 stores across the United Kingdom and Ireland and employs around 12,000 people, with an Irish property development group named as its preferred partner. A union says it has “deep concerns” the business could enter administration if the deal fails.

Dive deeper
The chain was sold for a single pound last summer by its former owner to a restructuring specialist, which is now looking for a buyer of its own — a reminder that a nominal price buys a liability, not a bargain. Bally Auluk of the shopworkers’ union Usdaw said members had deep concerns about administration and that “we are told that their offers are being turned down”, a claim a Poundland source disputes on the record. The managing director Barry Williams is working with Andy Bond, who formerly chaired the group’s owner. The company expects sales to rise more than 3% this year, which is the argument for the business being viable in the right hands. Discount retail is supposed to do well when budgets tighten, so a chain of this size needing rescue in a year of rising prices says something about the state of the high street rather than about the format. Watch whether the preferred partner converts into an actual offer, because the alternative on the table is administration.

Report Urges Talks on Paramilitary Disbandment

An independent review commissioned by the British and Irish governments concludes there is “sufficient merit in and support for a formal process of paramilitary group transition” in Northern Ireland, and recommends no financial rewards for taking part. Its author warns that “inaction is not a neutral option”.

Dive deeper
Fleur Ravensbergen, a Dutch conflict-resolution specialist who spent a year on the scoping exercise, found “significant willingness to engage” but also “limited support for the term disbandment” — objections that “appeared to be less about what transition should achieve and more about the wording”. She recommends credible monitoring and robust law enforcement alongside any process, and warns that “a window of opportunity exists… but this may weaken over time”. The Northern Ireland secretary Chris Bryant said the groups “may pose as protectors of their communities” but are “largely engaged in criminality… blighting the very communities they perversely claim to protect”, and will consider the report with Dublin before responding — so neither government has accepted it. Dublin has said nothing. The justice minister Naomi Long, who criticised the appointment, has argued the idea that paramilitaries can become law-abiding entities “has long since moved beyond the point of credibility”. The report names no individuals or organisations, and its author declined to comment on the Drumcree standoff.
