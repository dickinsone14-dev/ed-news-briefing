GEO Geopolitical

Trump Declares an Energy Ceasefire and Kyiv Asks When It Starts

“Effective immediately, there is an ENERGY CEASEFIRE in the War between Russia and Ukraine. Both parties have agreed,” the president wrote. Volodymyr Zelensky said it “was news for me” and asked Washington to say when it begins. Moscow has not confirmed it, and Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy continued through the day.

Dive deeper
The post contained no scope, no verification mechanism and no start time. Mr Zelensky’s position hardened into a conditional yes: “Ukraine supports the ceasefire, and it is important that Russia support it as well. We are awaiting details from the American side,” and on American television, “Don’t attack us. Don’t kill our children. We will stop attack your diesel.” What there is no sign of is Russian observance. Mr Zelensky says drones flew throughout the day and that Kharkiv thermal power station number five was hit, the latest of more than two hundred strikes this week on generation and transmission, alongside seventeen on gas and oil facilities. A month ago the president made a near-identical announcement and both sides kept striking each other’s energy infrastructure. Running underneath it is a harder form of pressure, reported by one newspaper citing European officials and carried here at second hand: that Washington is threatening to halt the procurement system supplying Ukraine with weapons unless Kyiv stops hitting Russian refineries. Oleksandr Merezhko, who chairs Ukraine’s parliamentary foreign affairs committee, says a parallel threat to cut intelligence sharing would “blind” Ukrainian air defences and would be “an act of complicity… It would mean more dead civilians and dead children.” Andy Burnham and Mr Zelensky called for precisely this ceasefire on Saturday night. Twelve hours later one was announced over Kyiv’s head.

Riyadh Struck Again as the Houthis Claim Both Attacks

A cruise missile was intercepted near King Khalid International on Sunday while firefighters tackled a blaze caused by a projectile that hit Passenger Hall Four. Dammam’s King Fahd International was attacked too. The Houthis have now claimed both days and warn that every Saudi airport except those at Mecca and Medina is a target.

Dive deeper
Saturday’s toll has risen to thirteen, after an Indian man died of his wounds on Sunday; 308 remain injured. The dead include four Saudis, two Bangladeshis and one national each from America, Jordan, Palestine, Syria, Sudan and Egypt. Brigadier General Yahya Saree says the group used “a mix of ballistic and cruise missiles, as well as drones in the past 24 hours” and will keep “escalating operations deep inside Saudi Arabia”, with a warning addressed to the industry: “We renew our warning to all airlines, experts, employees, workers and travellers against using Saudi airports and airspace, as they are vulnerable to attack and have become a theatre of operations for our forces.” Riyadh’s schools move online until Thursday. Britain is edging closer: the defence secretary Wes Streeting said the United Kingdom could “potentially” provide more support for Saudi Arabia, and the foreign secretary said he was “appalled”. Around two hundred American military advisers are already in the kingdom on intelligence and targeting, though the United States has not yet fought the Houthis directly. If it does, the group has previously attacked shipping at Bab al-Mandab, through which roughly a tenth of the world’s seaborne goods pass. Houthi-run television says a Saudi strike on Sanaa killed a child; Saudi Arabia has not confirmed it and it is not independently verified.

Poland Detains a Suspect in a Plot Against Tusk

“The man threatening an attack has been detained,” Donald Tusk wrote, thanking police for “swift and decisive action”. The plot targeted the prime minister and his deputy, the defence minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz. A Polish national was arrested in Lower Silesia after an email about the planned attack reached the state protection service.

Dive deeper
Jacek Dobrzyński, spokesman for the minister coordinating Poland’s special services, did not soften it: “We are treating this matter as deadly serious… In this case we are talking about a strong likelihood that these threats could actually be carried out.” Police, the protection service and the internal security agency all attended an emergency meeting on Sunday; Mr Tusk was told first thing. It lands in a week of school attacks. Three violent incidents at Polish schools have left a 37-year-old caretaker at a primary school north of Warsaw dead and others injured, among them children, and police have arrested 117 people nationwide for carrying weapons or threatening attacks after searching tens of thousands of schools. Mr Tusk’s diagnosis is the part that travels: “These are very often spontaneous actions inspired by online activity… We are not dealing with any group organized on a big scale,” and “In the past years and months, we have noted — globally and in Poland — a rise in the fascination with violence.” He called it nihilistic violence associated with the extreme right and said some of those detained are active in that milieu. Last year he reported threats after the attempt on the Slovak prime minister Robert Fico.

Pope Leo to Have Lung Surgery After a Nodule Is Found

“The doctors found an early, well-localised nodule on my lung,” the Pope told crowds at the Angelus in St Peter’s Square, announcing it himself. His doctors “believe it would be right to intervene” and he has agreed. The operation is scheduled for the first days of next week, with about a week’s recovery.

Dive deeper
He described the mass as well contained and caught at an early stage, thanked the crowds for their “spiritual closeness” and entrusted himself to their prayers. The Holy See press office says the surgery will take place at the Gemelli Hospital in Rome, which has kept a papal suite since the attempt on John Paul II in 1981. Leo XIV is 71, has no known previous health problems and is regarded as physically active, which is part of why the announcement carries weight: this is not the continuation of a known condition. Born Robert Francis Prevost, ordained in 1982 and a missionary in Peru for years, he is the 267th pope and the first American citizen to hold the office, elected a year and a half ago. His predecessor also had lung trouble requiring hospital treatment. Giorgia Meloni expressed personal solidarity, President Sergio Mattarella sent wishes for a peaceful recovery and Israel’s president wished him a full and speedy one. One wording note for anyone comparing reports: some outlets render the finding as a nodular lesion, attributing that phrasing to the Vatican. The word used here is the one the Pope used himself.

At Least 139 Dead as a Migrant Boat Capsizes off Djibouti

The International Organization for Migration says at least 139 people died, fifteen of them children, when a boat carrying 305 capsized near Obock on Thursday night. Eighty-four are missing and about 82 were rescued, all Ethiopian nationals. The toll was revised up from 109 after thirty more bodies were recovered.

Dive deeper
It is expected to rise again. Alexandre Coissac, who heads the organisation’s mission in Djibouti, says the count includes only bodies buried by the Djiboutian authorities; others buried by migrants themselves are not yet counted pending verification. “This is a devastating loss of life,” he said. “Our thoughts are with the survivors and the families who have lost loved ones or remain without news of their relatives… This tragedy is a stark reminder of the dangers faced by migrants along this migration corridor as they seek economic opportunities.” Sixty-four survivors are being helped at a response centre at Obock. The route is busier than it looks: the organisation logged more than 20,000 movements from Ethiopia to Djibouti in the month to 10 September, and more than 900 people died or disappeared on this crossing during 2025, a year in which attempts rose by 18%. A comparable sinking in March killed nine with 45 missing. The detail that should stop a reader is the direction of travel. This boat was going from Djibouti to Yemen — people paying to cross into a country at war, through the Bab al-Mandab strait that the Houthis are threatening and that carries about a tenth of the world’s seaborne trade.

UK Domestic

Burnham Says Russia Interfered in the Brexit Referendum

“I asked the question coming to Downing Street: well, was there interference in the 2016 referendum from Russia? I’ve had the clear answer that yes, there was,” the prime minister said. He did not say who gave him that assessment, what it rests on, or whether it affected the result — and announced no inquiry.

Dive deeper
The distinction matters more than the claim. No British government has ever conceded interference in the referendum; one has conceded it in the 2019 general election, when the previous administration said it was “almost certain that Russian actors sought to interfere”. Parliament’s Intelligence and Security Committee reported in 2020 that it could reach no definitive conclusion on 2016, criticised successive governments for failing to assess the threat, and noted that no post-referendum assessment had ever been produced — while arguing that whether a hostile state tried to influence a democratic process matters regardless of whether it succeeded. That is the ground Andy Burnham has now stepped onto, and his reason is forward-looking rather than retrospective: “I think we’ve been too much on the back foot on that actually and we haven’t informed people sufficiently about the extent to which people are seeking to disrupt the political process from overseas,” adding that putting it on the record would help voters in Germany and France understand that “some of this politics is instigated via our enemies, namely Russia”. Downing Street’s response does not mention the referendum at all, listing existing measures under the National Security Act. The pro-Brexit group Britain Unbound calls it “another crude attempt to undermine the legitimacy of the decision that the British people made in 2016”. Neither the Conservatives nor Reform had responded by Sunday evening. The interview airs on Monday.

Questions Over the Speed of the Voting Law Overhaul

The bill would let voters use bank cards without photographs as identification, lower the voting age to 16 and tighten rules on political donations. Ministers are reported to want Lords scrutiny cut from an anticipated ten days to fewer than five. The Electoral Commission warns accepting bank cards would make verifying voters harder.

Dive deeper
The numbers behind the identification change are not trivial. The Electoral Commission found about 16,000 people who tried to vote in person were turned away for want of identification and did not come back, and separately that about 4% of those who did not vote in 2024 cited photo identification as a factor. Polling after that election found 6.5% of minority ethnic voters surveyed had been turned away at least once, against 2.5% of white voters. Mr Burnham himself, as one of seven Labour mayors in 2024, called the rules a “brazen assault on our democracy”. The donations clauses are where this meets the item above it, because restrictions under discussion could retrospectively affect the £72m given to Reform UK by the crypto billionaires Ben Delo and Christopher Harborne. The Liberal Democrat peer Mark Pack objects to amending the franchise without proper scrutiny and Conservative peers accuse ministers of rushing; the government says the bill “is going through the proper parliamentary process, giving MPs and peers the time and opportunity to examine and debate its measures”, and that the prime minister has ruled out an early election. The claim about compressing Lords time comes from another newspaper and is carried here as reported rather than established.

Flu Is Arriving Early in England, the Health Agency Warns

“Flu activity throughout September has been higher than in the same period during all past four winter seasons,” the UK Health Security Agency says, with positive tests, GP consultations and accident and emergency attendances all rising. Levels are still low overall. The rise is being driven by cases among 18 to 24-year-olds.

Dive deeper
Two influenza A subtypes are circulating, H1N1 and H3N2, and the vaccine covers both plus influenza B. One caveat is worth carrying rather than burying: the agency notes H1N1 has drifted slightly from the strain used in this year’s jab, and Professor Antonia Ho of the University of Glasgow’s Centre for Virus Research says it is too soon to know whether that affects how well it works. Professor Sir Andrew Pollard, who directs the Oxford Vaccine Group, gives the practical instruction: “There is one simple action for all those who are at risk because of age or their underlying health conditions, which is to get vaccinated as soon as possible and before we reach the peak of transmission.” Free vaccination goes to children and higher-risk groups; others can pay about £20 at most high street pharmacies or under £10 on some retailer membership schemes. Last winter’s programme in England is estimated to have prevented around 100,000 hospital admissions and 7,000 deaths. On whether an early season means a bad one, the experts disagree honestly: Professor Ho says an early rise “may indicate a more intense influenza season overall” while noting that “onset timing alone doesn’t determine severity”, and Sir Andrew says an earlier start does not necessarily mean a worse one. Covid levels are also rising.

A&E Consultant Says She Is Terrified of the Scottish Winter

Dr Fiona Hunter, vice chair of the Royal College of Emergency Medicine in Scotland, says she is “completely terrified” about the coming months and that emergency care is at “unsafe” levels, with some patients spending 72 hours on trolleys in hospital corridors. She describes a “toxic normalisation” of corridor care “which can lead to patients dying”.

Dive deeper
The health secretary Angela Constance vowed in August to eradicate corridor care. Speaking to the same programme she said winter would be “difficult” and repeated that corridor care is unacceptable, setting out three priorities: reducing avoidable admissions including through what she called robust public messaging, fixing the absence of statistics on corridor care, and moving to year-round winter planning rather than seasonal scrambling. One point of fairness belongs in the copy rather than a footnote: she was interviewed before Dr Hunter’s remarks were broadcast, so this is not a reply to them. That second priority is quietly the most revealing. Scotland does not currently collect figures on how many patients are treated in corridors, which means the practice that the health secretary has promised to eradicate is one the government cannot presently measure. Running underneath all of it is structural upheaval: the first minister John Swinney last month confirmed plans to cut mainland health boards from eleven to two. A reader in Scotland facing an emergency department this winter is therefore being told three things at once — that the situation is unsafe, that it is unacceptable, and that nobody yet counts it.

Home Office Revokes a Syrian Surgeon’s Refugee Status

A plastic surgeon who arrived in 2016 and now lives in London with his wife and six children, three of them at university, has had his refugee protection cancelled and faces deportation. The Foreign Office advises against all travel to Syria because the security situation is “volatile”. The Home Office has classified it safe to return to.

Dive deeper
That contradiction between two departments is the story. The Home Office letter puts its reasoning plainly: “Your refugee status has been ceased. Although officials accept that the situation in Syria remains ‘complex’ they consider that changes in Syria are so significant that your fear of persecution and serious harm can no longer be regarded as well founded.” The surgeon, who is registered with the General Medical Council and works in a general practice, does not accept it: “I don’t believe Syria will be a safe place for me to return to. I was targeted by certain radical groups in Syria who are still operating there.” A Home Office spokesperson says: “Britain will always provide protection to genuine refugees, and we will never return anyone to danger.” This is reported as the first time the department has cancelled refugee protection for a particular nationality, and the first such active revocation anywhere in Europe since the fall of Assad, with thousands of Syrians settled in Britain likely to be affected. Those three claims are the reporting outlet’s own, carefully hedged, and are carried here with the hedge intact. The account rests on a single newspaper’s investigation. What is not in dispute is the departmental split: one arm of government says do not go, the other says you may be sent.
