Flávio Bolsonaro Wins the First Round in Brazil

Flávio Bolsonaro took 47% of the vote to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s 45.1% with 99.9% counted, forcing a run-off on 25 October. Every major poll had put Lula first. The right also took 34 of the 54 Senate seats decided, and the Liberal Party is on course to be the largest force in the lower house. “I admit, I was convinced I would win in the first round,” Lula said.

Dive deeper
The margin is two points, 56.1 million votes to 53.8 million, from an electorate of nearly 159 million who also chose 27 governors, 513 deputies and 54 of the 81 senators. The Senate result is the one the Bolsonaro family wanted most, because senators are the only lawmakers who can impeach supreme court justices, and Jair Bolsonaro is serving 27 years for plotting a coup after losing in 2022. His son has promised him an amnesty, and the same for those who stormed the capital on 8 January 2023. The dynasty did well in its own right: Michelle Bolsonaro won a Senate seat in the Federal District, her first elected office; Carlos Bolsonaro took one in Santa Catarina; Jair Renan Bolsonaro won a seat in the lower house; and Tarcísio de Freitas was re-elected governor of São Paulo. The analyst Rafael Favetti argued the upset cuts both ways: “The protest vote foreshadows what is to come in the run-off. While this should benefit Flavio Bolsonaro now, his growth potential in the second round drops precisely because that support has already been front-loaded.” Mr Bolsonaro called it “the end of an era” for the Workers’ Party; Lula said the country had just had “a lesson in Brazilian democracy” and that “in extra time, we will win”. Watch the 4.3% that went to the two also-rans, both of whom say they will endorse nobody.

The US Removes Every Bomber From RAF Fairford

The Pentagon has confirmed that all American bombers at RAF Fairford — thought to be about a dozen B-1s — have returned to their home stations in the United States. The withdrawal follows the 27 September incident at the Gloucestershire base, after which six men were arrested and bailed. The BBC was told it was “a military rather than a political decision”, taken at operational level. The Ministry of Defence has not commented.

Dive deeper
Fairford is a hundred miles west of London and has been the staging post for the longest-range part of the air campaign: B-1s flying from it carried bunker-busting bombs and air-to-surface missiles against deeper Iranian targets, on sorties of twelve to fifteen hours with refuelling in both directions. Running those missions from the continental United States instead is possible — the Pentagon stresses its bombers can strike “anywhere, anytime” — but it is slower and costlier, which is why the move reads as a judgement about risk on the ground rather than about capability. Five men aged 23 to 25, all British nationals from London, were arrested over three suspicious vehicles near the base and bailed; a sixth, a 25-year-old British-Iranian, was arrested on Thursday and bailed on Saturday. Andy Burnham has said there are “strong indications” Iran played a part and Marco Rubio said the episode “clearly” involved “the hands of a foreign actor”; Tehran denies it, and said in July that any base used for aggression against it was a legitimate target. At sea the pressure has not eased: the UK Maritime Trade Operations Centre reported a crude tanker hit by a projectile four nautical miles east of Oman on Saturday and another struck in the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday, its engine room damaged. Watch whether any of the six is charged.

The Euro Falls to a 17-Month Low on Spanish and French Politics

The euro fell 0.6% against the dollar on Monday to its weakest since 19 May last year, as rising euro-area inflation, interest rates and borrowing costs met political uncertainty in Spain and France. Pedro Sánchez is widely expected to call a snap Spanish election after losing two housing decrees on Friday, though his office has declined to comment. France’s draft budget aims to cut the deficit from 5.4% of national income to 5%.

Dive deeper
Spain is the immediate trigger and the more human story. The decrees fell in the 350-seat lower house on Friday, the Catalan separatists of Junts voting with the People’s Party and Vox against measures that would have halted evictions of vulnerable people, curbed short-term lets and made leases renew indefinitely. They had been drafted after the eviction of María del Carmen Abascal, 87, from the Madrid home she had lived in for seventy years, after an international investment fund bought the building and raised her rent by more than 200%. Saturday brought about 70,000 onto the streets of Madrid on the government’s own count and demonstrations in fifty cities, with rubber bullets and tear gas in Valencia. Mr Sánchez’s term does not expire until the summer of 2027, so an autumn election would be a choice rather than a necessity. France is the slower problem: Barclays economists said French fiscal and political developments “cloud the euro area outlook” and are “unlikely to reach an inflection point before next year’s presidential election”, while ING said the draft budget would “not resolve France’s structural fiscal problems”. For British readers the currency cuts the other way: sterling has been firming against the euro, which makes a European holiday cheaper and British exports to the bloc dearer. Watch whether Mr Sánchez actually dissolves parliament.

Dodik’s Party Sweeps the Bosnian Elections

Milorad Dodik’s ally Željka Cvijanović took more than 55% of the vote for the Serb seat on Bosnia’s tripartite presidency, about thirty points clear, with nearly 90% counted. Mr Dodik, barred from office last year after a conviction for defying the international envoy overseeing the Dayton agreement, claims his party and its partners have two-thirds of the Republika Srpska assembly. Turnout fell about eight points to 42%.

Dive deeper
He was not on the ballot and it has made no difference. “No one should ask us to change our politics,” Mr Dodik told a news conference late on Sunday, crediting Donald Trump’s “magnificent victory” in 2024 and Vladimir Putin’s support, and adding: “I cannot believe that we smashed it like this.” At his final rally in Banja Luka on Friday, attended by the Russian ambassador, he told supporters that Bosnia “is dead” and “an impossible country” that “doesn’t make any sense”. The analyst Dragan Bursać put the lesson plainly: Mr Dodik “is still in command. He is everywhere. It is obvious that these measures to ban him from political life are not working, and he is using them.” A two-thirds assembly majority is the number that matters, because it is the threshold at which secession steps stop being rhetoric and become legislation. The other two seats went the other way: Darijana Filipović of the Croatian Democratic Union led commandingly, and the incumbent Bosniak member Denis Bećirović led Bakir Izetbegović by fewer than three points. Of about 3.4 million eligible voters, 1,432,269 cast a ballot. The war that ended in 1995 killed more than 100,000 people. Watch for a response from Brussels or the Office of the High Representative, which had not come by this morning.

Tigrayan Forces Abandon Mekelle as Four Neighbours Issue a Declaration

The Tigray People’s Liberation Front says it has temporarily withdrawn from Mekelle, citing the risk to civilians as the national army closed on the regional capital. Two residents told the BBC that pro-government forces now hold the city and that mobile service has returned in places. The presidents of Egypt, Eritrea, Somalia and Sudan met on Egypt’s coast on Sunday and called for “respecting the sovereignty, unity and territorial integrity of states”.

Dive deeper
Two accounts of the same event sit side by side and both are worth keeping: the government describes retaking the city, the TPLF describes a voluntary and temporary relocation. What is agreed is that a capital has changed hands within about ten days of the offensive beginning, and that the Pretoria agreement, which ended a civil war the African Union’s own mediator estimated killed some 600,000 people, is finished. The regional alignment is the harder development. The four presidents who met at El-Alamein all have strained relations with Addis Ababa, and their joint statement rejected “unilateral measures” harming downstream countries — language aimed squarely at the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam. Ethiopia and Eritrea severed diplomatic relations last week after Addis Ababa accused Asmara of backing a new rebel alliance that the TPLF joined last month alongside six other armed movements, the Amhara Fano militia among them, with the stated aim of removing Abiy Ahmed’s government; Asmara denies it. Ethiopia makes the same accusation against Egypt and Sudan, which they deny, and has expelled an Egyptian diplomat, with Cairo expelling an Ethiopian official in return. The United States has called for restraint and dialogue, and the United Nations warns the fighting is severely disrupting humanitarian work. Watch whether “temporarily” turns into a counter-offensive.

UK UK Domestic Politics

Tories Would Abolish the Environment Agency and Natural England

Andrew Griffith will use his first conference speech as shadow chancellor to promise the abolition of Natural England and the Environment Agency, the scrapping of the Future Homes Standard and of the packaging producer scheme, a single developer levy in place of section 106 and the infrastructure levy, departure from the Aarhus convention, and a third runway at Heathrow. The party says the housing measures would cut up to £50,000 from the cost of building a home.

Dive deeper
“No one else is coming to save our economy,” Mr Griffith says in his extracts. “Only we can inject reality, tell the hard truths and make this a country we are proud to hand on to the next generation.” He calls the tax code “a cat’s cradle of complexity” and Andy Burnham’s programme “a socialist fever dream of the 1970s”, and on aviation refuses “to accept for a single moment that the country of Brunel and Bazalgette, of the vaccine taskforce and the Mulberry harbours, has to sit around waiting until the 2040s to get a third runway built”. Katie Lam, the new shadow housing secretary, says removing the enforcement bodies means “anti-growth ideologues can never again hold our country hostage”. The weakness is arithmetic: the £50,000 is the party’s own figure, unmodelled and unattributed, and no costing has been published for any of it three weeks before the Budget. The reaction told its own story about where the Conservatives now sit. Robert Jenrick, speaking for Reform, said they were “once again copying our policies” and still could not match a £15,000 tax-free allowance; Daisy Cooper for the Liberal Democrats called them “a pointless anti-growth anti-business party”. Watch whether shadow Treasury ministers, told to simplify the tax code line by line, produce a number that survives contact with 28 October.

BT Rescues TalkTalk and the Government Intervenes Within Hours

BT has agreed to buy TalkTalk, which had been close to administration over its debts and carries 1.5 million retail and a million wholesale customers. Within hours Lisa Nandy issued a public interest intervention notice under the Enterprise Act, on public health grounds and on new grounds covering continuity of telecoms supply. The competition authority must report to her by 19 October. Customers need do nothing; services continue as normal.

Dive deeper
The intervention is unusual in both speed and reasoning. “Phone and broadband services are vital national infrastructure,” the Culture Secretary said. “If TalkTalk services fail, there is a genuine risk to life and public services — including to hospitals, schools and emergency care. These are unprecedented circumstances that require action now.” The grounds she is using for continuity of supply do not yet exist in law: she says she will “as a matter of urgency” lay an order before Parliament amending the Enterprise Act to add them, which means the legal basis is being built after the notice rather than before it. That is a measure of how close the company was to failing, and of what runs over those two and a half million connections — among them the lines behind emergency calls, ambulance and hospital communications, and medical alarms in vulnerable households. Alison Kirkby, BT’s chief executive, called the deal “a safety net for the households and businesses who rely on TalkTalk”. No price has been disclosed, and the obvious competition question — one company taking a larger share of a market it already dominates at the wholesale level — is precisely what the regulator now has a fortnight to examine. Watch the 19 October report, and whether Parliament waves through a retrospective widening of ministers’ powers to intervene.

North Sea Strike Ballot Threatens a Third of UK Oil and Gas

More than 160 Apache offshore workers have backed industrial action that could begin later this month, Unite says, affecting the Forties and Beryl fields. The union warns the Charlie platform could be brought to a standstill and the Forties pipeline system, which handles almost a third of Britain’s oil and gas, could go down. It lands on a week in which the average price of diesel reached a record £2 a litre.

Dive deeper
The dispute is about a 4% offer. Apache says its offshore staff “already place them among the highest earners in the UK” on a rota averaging 153 working days a year; its parent company reported $1.4 billion of after-tax profit on $9.2 billion of revenue last year. Sharon Graham, Unite’s general secretary, said the union “will not tolerate unacceptable pay offers”, and the industrial officer Stevie Davies said disruption “would have a direct hit on the Forties pipeline and potentially severely affect the UK’s fuel supplies”. Apache says it has contingency plans and expects no effect on other producers. There is a recent precedent for settlement: Unite called off strikes at Neo Next this summer after a deal worth more than £4,000 on the package. What makes this one matter beyond the rigs is the timing. Diesel is already at a record, the G7 has agreed to release up to a hundred million barrels of emergency crude and diesel after Donald Trump threatened to cut off American supply, and the Strait of Hormuz remains closed. A Forties shutdown would arrive on top of all three. No strike dates have been set, and Ineos, which operates the pipeline, has not commented. Watch for the dates.

Israel Bans Polanski After the Greens’ Zionism Vote

Israel’s foreign ministry has barred the Green leader Zack Polanski from entering the country after members voted 1,022 to 813 in Brighton for a motion defining Zionism as racism and backing a single state in all of historic Palestine. The ministry called it “a clear antisemitic text” and “a stain on the history of the United Kingdom”. Mr Polanski did not vote, having left to campaign in Holborn and St Pancras.

Dive deeper
The Board of Deputies of British Jews and the Jewish Leadership Council said in a joint statement that “Zionism is anti-racism, creating a refuge for Jews in their ancient homeland after millennia of persecution outside it”, and that branding most British Jews and Jewish institutions racist “stokes anti-Jewish hatred after a year in which British Jews have been murdered attending their synagogue, stabbed in the streets, and seen their community buildings firebombed”. They singled out the leader “who neither spoke against the motion nor voted”. The party is visibly split: four of its five MPs opposed the motion, and Caroline Lucas urged members to reject “the over-simplification at the heart of this motion — a refusal to accept complexities which is leading to so many Jewish members feeling scared and unwelcome in our party”. The two deputy leaders took opposite sides, Mothin Ali backing it and Rachel Millward calling the wording problematic, and the chairwoman of Jewish Greens said she could consider her future in the party. Bridget Phillipson said the vote “crosses a line”; Chris Philp called the Greens “toxic to its core”. Under Green rules a members’ motion becomes policy but need not enter the manifesto. Watch Thursday, when Mr Polanski faces voters.

Protesters Confront Police as 149 Migrants Land at Gosport

About 150 anti-migrant demonstrators gathered at a marina in Gosport to stop migrants being brought ashore, setting off flares and scuffling with police in a stand-off that ran into the early hours. The Home Office says 149 people were rescued mid-Channel by the RNLI alongside a French vessel and taken by coach to a processing centre in Kent. Hampshire Police imposed Public Order Act conditions and banned face coverings.

Dive deeper
It is only the second known landing of large numbers this far west along the south coast in recent years, and the Home Office has refused to say why Gosport was used, citing operational processes. The local police and crime commissioner, Donna Jones, is calling on the Home Secretary to intervene “urgently to ensure that UK Border Force has the capacity and vessels to transfer people on the water to Dover”, arguing that “no sites across the southern coastline of Britain should be used as landing sites. We are not equipped and we do not have the resources to keep policing this.” She puts the cost of each protest at £150,000 from her own budget, which she says means less officer time on the streets of Hampshire and the Isle of Wight; the figure is her own and unaudited. The politics arrived three hours earlier. Shabana Mahmood had said in a podcast interview that “we’re not in control of this border”, that the sifting of people in northern France is done by criminal gangs who are also traffickers, and that “you can almost guarantee that every woman that’s in the north of France has probably been raped along the journey”. Of the masked protesters she said they should “sod off”. The Refugee Council welcomed the acknowledgment of sexual violence but said protecting women means “more safe routes, not fewer”. Watch whether Border Force changes its landing practice.
