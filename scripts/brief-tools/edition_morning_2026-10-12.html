GEO Geopolitical

Power Plants Struck Hours After the Ceasefire Was Announced

Volodymyr Zelensky says a drone hit a power plant in Kyiv, causing outages in the capital, and that a strike on the CHP-5 plant in Kharkiv followed. “All of this happened after the president of the United States made his statement about an energy ceasefire,” he said. Moscow has not confirmed agreeing to anything.

Dive deeper
Donald Trump’s announcement was a social media post: “Effective immediately, there is an ENERGY CEASEFIRE in the War between Russia and Ukraine. Both parties have agreed. Do not break it!” No detail followed, and what Mr Zelensky said next is the story. First that the ceasefire was “news to me”; then that if it had been negotiated with Vladimir Putin, “please tell us when the ceasefire begins. We are ready and we will accept it”; then that “Ukraine is ready not to strike at Russian energy and we count on Russia’s similar readiness.” He counts more than 200 strikes on generation and transmission this week and seventeen hits on gas and oil facilities. Two things are not established and are worth not asserting: the Kremlin has said nothing whatever about having agreed, and Washington has not answered the contradiction. Front pages this morning say Putin broke the ceasefire, but no agreement has been shown to exist. Andy Burnham said Britain “strongly supports” the call; Mark Carney said the parallel diesel arrangement “is in direct opposition to the efforts of Ukraine, Canada and our allies”. Watch for whether Moscow acknowledges the deal at all.

Britons Told to Leave Saudi Arabia Overland as Airports Shut

The Foreign Office now advises against all but essential travel to Riyadh province, four southern regions and the cities of Taif, Jeddah and Dammam, and to every Saudi airport except Medina. It suggests British nationals leave by road for Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates or Jordan and fly from there. Further attacks on airports are “very likely”.

Dive deeper
The practical warning is the one to note: travel insurance can be invalidated by travelling against official advice. Behind it is a run of attacks that the sources describe differently and that is better quoted than counted — the latest of several on King Khalid International in the past week. Sunday evening’s projectile hit terminals three and four, killing one person, a Pakistani resident, and injuring eleven; air defences also intercepted a cruise missile nearby. That strike has not been claimed by anyone, and while it has been widely attributed to the Houthis, the group claimed earlier attacks and has said nothing about this one. Saturday’s strike on the same airport killed thirteen and injured 308. A separate projectile hit King Fahd International at Dammam. Riyadh’s schools have moved online until Thursday and Jeddah logged more than 150 cancellations and 374 delays on Sunday. Australia, New Zealand and Canada have issued their own warnings and South Korea has told citizens to leave unless their business is essential. Houthi spokesman Brigadier General Yahya Saree has said every Saudi airport bar Mecca and Medina is a target and that “the worst is yet to come”.

At Least Seventy-Two Gold Miners Killed in Strike on Sudan

A monitoring group says an air raid on a gold mine near the disputed Sudan–Egypt border killed at least seventy-two civilian miners on Saturday. Emergency Lawyers, which documents atrocities in Sudan’s war, accuses Egyptian fighter jets of carrying out the strike inside Sudanese territory. Neither Egypt nor the Sudanese authorities has commented, and the toll is provisional.

Dive deeper
This needs its caveats attached rather than buried. The accusation rests on one monitor, the attack cannot be independently verified, and no government has responded; the group’s own wording is that “Egyptian fighter jets targeted civilian miners in the northern Wadi area inside Sudanese territory” killing seventy-two, and that such acts “may constitute war crimes”. What gives the claim traction is a pattern rather than a confession. A strike on the same area in June killed twenty-three, after which Egypt’s military said it had acted within its own territory against illegal mining, detaining 223 people and seizing eighty-six tonnes of gold ore. The border itself is contested: both countries claim the Halaib Triangle. The wider context is a war that has killed more than 200,000 people since April 2023 and driven civilians into artisanal gold mining that funds both sides of it. Accounts differ slightly on location, one placing the mine in River Nile state and another at Jabal al-Uqaydat, though the incident, the toll and the monitor are the same. Watch for whether Cairo addresses the claim directly, having answered the June strike within days.

North Korea Fires Missiles, One Possibly Hypersonic, Over 900km

Multiple ballistic missiles were launched from the Pyongyang area towards the sea off the east coast on Monday. South Korea’s joint chiefs say one flew more than 900km. Japan’s deputy defence minister Takashi Koyari says at least two were fired seventeen minutes apart, the second on an “irregular” flight path he suggested may indicate a hypersonic weapon.

Dive deeper
Both landed outside Japan’s exclusive economic zone, the first travelling about 220km and the second more than 910km. Seoul’s presidential office said the launches violate United Nations Security Council resolutions; a spokesman for the joint chiefs, Jang Do-young, called them a show of force in the days after South Korea tested a hypersonic weapon of its own under President Lee Jae Myung. The word “hypersonic” is doing a lot of work in the coverage and is worth holding lightly: it rests on an irregular trajectory observed by Japanese radar, not on any North Korean claim, and the two capitals have not agreed a figure for how many missiles were fired at all. What makes the timing pointed is the audience. With American attention and munitions drawn to the Gulf and to Ukraine, a launch that demonstrates range and manoeuvre costs Pyongyang little and tests how much notice Washington can spare. The pattern to watch is whether these tests move from sea-facing demonstrations towards anything over Japanese territory, which is the step that has historically forced a response.

Republican Senators Join Democrats to Call Diesel Deal Unlawful

Four Republicans — Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis and John Curtis — have joined Democrats Jeanne Shaheen, Richard Blumenthal and Ron Wyden in saying the plan to import Russian diesel breaks American law. “The United States should not provide Putin with a financial lifeline while Russia continues to kill Ukrainian civilians,” they wrote, urging the president to reverse course.

Dive deeper
The volumes explain why this is not a symbolic objection: 300,000 tonnes now, 500,000 in November, a million after that and three million more to follow, with Russia’s deputy prime minister Alexander Novak saying exports could reach three million tonnes a month. The Treasury is suspending diesel sanctions until 7 April. Senator Tillis said the deal “absolutely will” put American lives at risk. Not every Republican objects — Thomas Massie wants sanctions lifted on all Russian petroleum products — and the split is less about Russia than about petrol prices before the midterms. The British relevance runs through the pump and the refinery. Russian refinery output is down sharply after Ukrainian drone strikes, which is the shortage this deal is meant to fill, and diesel is the fuel that moves freight. Average British diesel passed £2 a litre for the first time this month, up more than 40% since late February, and van and pickup owners face almost £2.5bn in extra fuel costs this year. Watch the European Union’s response: its foreign ministers have been preparing their largest sanctions package since the invasion, and a transatlantic split on Russian fuel would be the first real test of it.

UK Domestic

Budget a Work of Fiction Without a Plan for £35bn

The Institute for Fiscal Studies says honouring the government’s spending pledges in real terms — defence at 3% of national income by 2030 and a health uplift without squeezing other departments — would cost about £35bn. Its director Helen Miller says a “steady-as-she-goes” Budget “is not an option available to the chancellor”.

Dive deeper
Four different figures are circulating and only one of them is £35bn. That sum is the cost of delivering stated pledges in real terms once this year’s inflation shock is absorbed. It is not a hole in the public finances: the institute still expects the fiscal rules to be met, with headroom of roughly £20bn against the £24bn forecast in the spring, a reduction of about £4bn. Separately it puts £10bn on the 2029-30 borrowing-cost forecast. Other economists think market moves since February could erase about half the headroom — a far larger claim than the institute’s own. Ms Miller’s warning is about honesty rather than arithmetic: “Simply pencilling in implicit cuts that he has no intention of delivering would make the Budget a work of fiction.” John Healey, before this week’s International Monetary Fund meetings, said conflict abroad was “leading to higher prices at the pumps and more expensive mortgages at home”. The institute cautions that its own forecasts are “exceptionally uncertain” and that the official watchdog “may come to a very different conclusion”. The Treasury declined to comment on “rumour and speculation”. The Budget is on 28 October.

Papers Suggest Deloitte Helped Falsify Abramovich Tax Records

Leaked documents indicate that backdated paperwork moved Roman Abramovich’s assets beyond the reach of Cypriot tax, and that his accountants reviewed and signed off records the leak suggests they knew to be misleading. In one 2006 case, $672m of property, aircraft and boats left a company’s books, leaving $75,000 behind. Mr Abramovich denies evading tax or concealing his fortune.

Dive deeper
The material comes from Cyprus Confidential, a leak of more than half a million documents on offshore companies run from Cyprus between 1995 and 2022, first released to the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists. MeritServus of Limassol, the firm at the centre, was owned by Deloitte until 2005. The sharpest document is an August 2005 email saying a plan was devised “after a meeting with Deloitte” and that “it will be necessary to change the financial statements and the accounting records accordingly for 2003 and 2004”; an associate replied “I agree”. A separate 2006 transfer was backdated three years and judged “adequate”. Deloitte Cyprus has not denied the conduct. It says it “acts in compliance with applicable laws” and that it cannot address the specific allegations because the documents are more than twenty years old and no longer accessible to it. Mr Abramovich’s lawyers say no evidence shows he knew of, authorised or directed any falsification, that he was not involved in day-to-day operations, and that relying on professional advice was “entirely proper and legitimate”. No total for tax evaded has been established. Separately, £5.4bn of his assets remain frozen in Jersey.

Burnham to Tell MPs to Embrace Closer Ties With the EU

The prime minister is expected to tell the Commons today that it is time to “face up to the reality that Brexit has done more harm than good” and that “the status quo is not good enough for Britain”. A Downing Street source said failing to pursue a better deal would be “a major dereliction of duty”. He has not yet spoken.

Dive deeper
What is actually on the table is narrower than the rhetoric suggests, and worth separating from it. The concrete asks are the removal of trade barriers, an agreement on food and agriculture standards to cut border checks and paperwork, and closer cooperation on migration, ahead of a summit with the European Union rescheduled for next month after being postponed in the summer. There is no commitment to the customs union or the single market. Andy Burnham has gone further rhetorically than his predecessor, opening the door at conference to options including rejoining, and saying last month that an in-out referendum in a future manifesto was “possible” — but none of that is in today’s statement. Kemi Badenoch calls reopening the debate “a terrible idea”: “So much time lost, arguing with ourselves with the EU. Going round in circles, businesses not knowing whether they should invest.” Senior Conservatives have promised to reverse any deal, including on food standards, which businesses warn would prolong the costs they already carry. Labour MPs in leave-voting seats are uneasy. The Commons sits today for the first time since 15 September, and Westminster Hall debates a petition to rejoin carrying more than 111,000 signatures.

Heat Pump Grants to Be Cut to Help Fund Defence Spending

Hundreds of millions of pounds are expected to come off the boiler upgrade scheme, which offers grants of up to £9,000 from a £2.7bn budget, as the energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh looks for more than £2bn towards the defence investment plan. The scheme is not being abolished. The department says the savings are “a small part” of its overall budget.

Dive deeper
This rests on unnamed sources rather than an announcement, so it is a plan reported rather than a decision confirmed. The lineage is instructive. A £2bn capital cut was agreed under the previous prime minister to help fund a £15bn four-year rise in defence spending — the same rise John Healey resigned as defence secretary in June for calling too small. He must now find a further £4.7bn of savings as chancellor, which is the arithmetic behind the institute’s £35bn warning elsewhere in this briefing. The Treasury has long regarded heat pump grants as a middle-class subsidy. Those who build the things disagree about the timing rather than the principle. Octopus Energy’s chief executive Greg Jackson says heat pumps “can now cost the same to install as a gas boiler after the grant” and that more than 500,000 people have asked his company for a quote in eighteen months: “Cutting the engine now would be brutally damaging.” Madeleine Gabriel of Nesta notes bills are rising because of gas prices, so “cutting funding for heat pumps makes no sense”. Approval for the Jackdaw gasfield is expected this week.

Reform Would Suspend the Asylum System for a Full Parliament

Reform UK says that in government it would reject all new asylum applications, void existing ones and mark claimants for deportation, scrapping the asylum tribunal and deporting more than 90,000 outstanding appeals. It would leave the European Convention on Human Rights, repeal the Human Rights Act and disapply the 1951 Refugee Convention.

Dive deeper
Nigel Farage frames it as emergency powers: the country faces “a national security emergency” and “we cannot accept more asylum seekers while we still have no idea how many people are in the country illegally and the threat they pose”. The machinery described is as striking as the policy. Home Office asylum staff would be “reprioritised to reviewing and overturning previous refugee grants”; visa bans and tariffs would pressure countries that refuse returns, named as Pakistan, Somalia, Eritrea, Syria, Afghanistan and Sudan, escalating to airspace bans. Three different totals now attach to Reform’s deportation plans — 90,000 appeals, around 400,000 made liable by April’s review pledge, and 600,000 over five years from an earlier small-boats promise — and they come from three separate announcements rather than adding up. Labour’s chair Bridget Phillipson called it “a clear sign of desperation and weakness” and “an assault on our common values”. The Conservatives’ Chris Philp said the proposals “largely copy” his party’s. The Liberal Democrats’ Max Wilkinson said both “were the architects of the failed asylum system and now they will exploit it”. Watch whether any of it survives contact with the courts it proposes to bypass.
