GEO Geopolitical

America Sanctions the International Criminal Court Itself

Marco Rubio has announced sanctions on the court as an institution rather than on individual judges, blocking its property under American jurisdiction and barring United States companies and citizens from dealing with it. “Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC,” he said, calling it a “rogue court”. Britain and seven allies have publicly disagreed.

Dive deeper
The sequence of the day is worth setting out plainly, and then leaving alone. At about eleven this morning the Nobel committee gave the Peace Prize to Navi Pillay, a former judge of this court, and its chair said the award was “definitely a message to those who are attacking, undermin[ing] the international system”. Ms Pillay, before any of what followed, said international law was “under significant political attack… particularly visible with the attacks on the International Criminal Court”. The sanctions were announced some five hours later. Nobody in the administration has drawn a connection, and Donald Trump’s only response to the prize was to repost someone else’s criticism of it. Professor William Schabas of Middlesex University is willing to say it out loud as his own reading: “There’s often a political message lurking behind the award of the Nobel Peace Prize and I think this year is no exception.” Mr Rubio vowed to dismantle the court “brick by brick” if necessary, citing its Afghanistan investigation and its warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant, and called on allied signatories to “rein in” it. The Treasury is issuing licences exempting maintenance, technology and pension plans for a period one outlet puts at 180 days. The court’s president Tomoko Akane says it “will continue to fully discharge its mandate”; its deputy prosecutor Nazhat Shameem Khan says the measures are “intended to undermine the rule of law internationally”. Nine of eighteen judges were already designated.

Iran Says It Will Now Pursue Ships Beyond the Strait

“From now on, dealing with offending vessels will not be limited to the Strait of Hormuz,” the Revolutionary Guard said, warning that any vessel using an unauthorised passage “will be pursued throughout the region and its punishment will be definitive”. It separately claimed to have struck a gas carrier south of the strait, a claim no independent source has verified.

Dive deeper
The threat matters more than the claim, because it removes the geographic assumption the shipping and insurance markets have priced around all year. Until today the risk was understood to be concentrated in a stretch of water about thirty-three kilometres wide at its narrowest; the Guard has just told owners it extends to the region. The strike claim is a different quality of fact: it comes through Iranian state-affiliated media, names a liquefied petroleum gas carrier said to have suffered a fire in its engine room and propulsion, and is not independently confirmed. Separately, and possibly describing a different incident, the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations centre logged a vessel struck by an unknown projectile thirteen nautical miles west of Al Jazeera in the United Arab Emirates, with the fire since extinguished. The two should not be merged. Brent had fallen about a dollar to $103.18 by the early afternoon after Donald Trump ruled out striking Iran before the November midterms, and recovered into the close as the threat landed. For a British motorist the mechanism is dull and reliable: war risk premiums on hulls and cargo feed into freight rates, freight rates feed into the price of a barrel delivered to a European refinery, and that reaches the forecourt with a lag of a few weeks.

Ethiopia Accuses Eritrea of an All-Out Invasion of Tigray

In a letter to the United Nations Security Council, Ethiopia’s foreign minister Gedion Timothewos calls Eritrean troop movements into Tigray a “blatant act of aggression that engages Ethiopia’s inherent right to self-defence”, and says forces advanced up to sixty kilometres. Eritrea says it faces “an unprovoked war of aggression”. Both accusations are now on the record.

Dive deeper
Eritrea’s information minister Yemane Gebremeskel, who on Thursday would say only that he was “not in the business of refuting wild allegations”, has now gone further: the Ethiopian account is “duplicity in their most extreme forms”, and Addis Ababa is “openly working to bring about regime change in Eritrea”. The sixty-kilometre figure is one belligerent’s claim in a letter to the Security Council and should be read as that. What is independently reported is the air war: Ethiopian drone strikes hit Adigrat on Wednesday and Thursday, and the monitoring group Acled records multiple strikes on Eritrean troops and ammunition. A resident told a news agency there were “many, many drone strikes”. No casualty figures have been verified, and no international news organisation can currently report from Tigray, which is the single most important caveat on everything written about this war. Underneath the exchange of letters is a concrete object: Abiy Ahmed’s stated ambition for Ethiopian access to a Red Sea port, in a country that has been landlocked since Eritrean independence in 1993. He won the Nobel peace prize in 2019 for making peace with the government he is now accused of invading.

China Agrees to Halve Hybrid Car Exports to the European Union

The deal “opens the prospect of cutting China’s export of hybrids by more than a half”, the EU trade commissioner Maroš Šefčovič said in Beijing — several million vehicles over four years. “It is the first time that China has accepted to moderate its exports without going through the phase of prior trade tension.” Talks continue on rare earths.

Dive deeper
The pressure behind it is a trade deficit running at €1.18bn a day and, in Mr Šefčovič’s words, “literally thousands of job losses” at risk in European manufacturing. The agreement runs to sixteen points and he is careful not to oversell it: “This is far from the end. It’s a crucial first step, but only a first step.” He briefs European Union diplomats on Sunday before a leaders’ summit on Thursday. The clause with the most direct consequence for British industry is not about cars at all. China’s commerce ministry says it is “willing… to continue facilitating the approval of export licenses for rare earths and permanent magnets destined for the EU” — the materials that go into electric motors, wind turbines and guided weapons, and the choke point Beijing has used as leverage all year. Britain is outside this arrangement, which is the awkward part: a deal that eases supply into the single market does not automatically ease it into the United Kingdom, and the question of whether British manufacturers end up buying rare earths through European intermediaries is now a live one. A trade war on the other side of the Channel has been postponed rather than resolved.

France Concedes 3,000 Teachers and Brussels Catches the Protest

The education minister Édouard Geffray has promised 3,000 extra supply teachers within days, directed at the roughly 500 schools in greatest need — the first material concession of a three-week movement in which 6,877 people have been arrested, 86% of them under eighteen. In Brussels, tens of thousands marched against austerity and riot police used tear gas and water cannon.

Dive deeper
The French concession followed the largest day yet. On Thursday 71,000 protested nationwide with 24,000 at the Bastille; a school was ransacked in Bordeaux and a deputy head beaten in Marseille. By Friday only 140 of France’s 3,500 lycées were closed, a sharp fall, though another day of action is called for Tuesday. What makes this a European story rather than two national ones is what the Belgian protesters said. The socialist and Christian unions marched from Central to South station under the slogan “stay out of our pockets”; police say the main rally was peaceful and that “groups of troublemakers were responsible for serious incidents before and after the march”, with wooden beams, bottles and bins thrown and baton charges in reply. Crowd estimates differ sharply — police counted 30,000 and the unions 50,000. Young people there told reporters they were inspired by what they had seen in France. It follows clashes in Liège in which more than two hundred were arrested and dozens of police injured. Two governments trying to cut deficits, two sets of students who have decided the bill is landing on them, and a pattern that is now crossing borders faster than either government is moving.

UK Domestic

Burnham to Curb Non-Compete Clauses Covering Five Million Jobs

Non-compete clauses “have gone too far”, Andy Burnham told a summit in Manchester, promising detail alongside the Budget on 28 October. Around five million jobs in Britain carry one, typically running six months after an employee leaves. He said he hoped it would prove “the Bosman ruling for the innovation sector”.

Dive deeper
The Bosman comparison is doing real work. That 1995 judgment let footballers leave at the end of a contract without a transfer fee, and it moved bargaining power to the player permanently. Mr Burnham is arguing that a clause originally meant to protect trade secrets has spread until it binds people who hold none, and that the cost is paid in innovation: “I think that’s a drag on innovation.” He did not specify the scope beyond saying it would cover the “everyday economy” as well as start-ups and scaling firms. Three options have been under examination since late last year: an outright ban, a ban above a salary threshold, or a limit on duration. The previous government looked at the same question and ruled out a total ban. The Recruitment and Employment Confederation has warned against “sweeping” changes, arguing the clauses play a “vital role” in protecting commercially sensitive information and client relationships. Two things are worth holding until 28 October. The first is that a clause only bites if an employer is willing to enforce it, and most are not, so the practical gain depends entirely on where the line is drawn. The second is a sourcing point: this pledge rests on a single approved outlet’s account of the speech, and the government’s own release covering the same event makes no mention of non-compete clauses at all.

The King Settles Andrew’s £1.5m Royal Lodge Bill

The Crown Estate assessed £1.8m of dilapidations on Royal Lodge when Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor surrendered his lease, against about £302,000 of compensation he was owed for leaving early — a net £1.5m. It is understood the King paid that sum from private income. The estate says it has given him nothing and has received what it was owed.

Dive deeper
The arrangement that produced this is the part worth understanding. In 2003 he took a seventy-five-year lease on the ninety-eight-acre estate and paid about £7.5m up front for repairs, which meant he owed no rent thereafter and earned a right to compensation if he surrendered within the first twenty-five years, on a sum falling each year. Leaving in October 2026 was worth about £302,000 to him. Set against that, the Crown Estate’s assessment of the property’s condition came to £1.8m, covering the whole estate including several properties, gardens and large outbuildings; recouping dilapidations on exit is standard practice. The two figures net off, and the balance fell on him. A Crown Estate spokesman: “The lease for Royal Lodge has ended and our focus now is firmly on its future use and the opportunities to generate additional financial value for the nation from its early return.” It will be marketed by two national agents. Whether Andrew repays his brother is described as a private matter. Context from a National Audit Office report this year: he had been subletting three cottages on the estate while paying a peppercorn rent for more than two decades. The payment is said to have no connection to the King’s position this week that his private support should not fund Andrew’s legal costs.

Bank Tax Break Has Cost the Public £6bn, Says the TUC

The 2023 cut to the bank surcharge, from 8% to 3%, has cost £2.3bn, £1.7bn and £2bn in successive years on the TUC’s reading of HMRC receipts. Over the same period the four largest banks made £200bn of pre-tax profit and paid a record £25bn bonus pool. The union wants the money used to cut energy bills.

Dive deeper
The surcharge was cut to offset corporation tax rising from 19% to 25%, so the banks were meant to end up roughly where they started. The TUC’s argument is that the offset overshot. Its general secretary Paul Nowak puts the ask directly at the chancellor: “The Tories’ tax break for banks has cost the UK public purse £6bn and counting… At a time when families are struggling with soaring energy costs, taxing banks’ booming profits to cut bills is just plain common sense.” Three options are costed. Setting the surcharge at 16% would raise about £24bn over four years; matching the 35% energy windfall rate would raise £60bn; simply restoring 8% would raise £9bn. This is a union’s own analysis rather than an independent assessment, which is a different class of claim from a contested fact and should be read as advocacy with arithmetic attached. The counter-pressure is already in the room. Jamie Dimon of JP Morgan warned the prime minister and the chancellor against further levies at a meeting last month, and earlier this year suggested he could abandon a planned £3bn London headquarters. John Healey has three weeks to decide which of those two arguments he finds more persuasive, and the gilt market has spent this week reminding him that the Budget needs funding either way.

Second Head Teachers’ Union Passes No Confidence in Ofsted

The Association of School and College Leaders has passed a motion of no confidence in “the ability of Ofsted’s leadership to address the damage being done by the new inspection system”, calling the grading arrangement an “overbearing mess”. It will survey members on whether to hold an informal ballot for industrial action.

Dive deeper
Its general secretary Pepe Di’Iasio says the union did not act “lightly”, that Ofsted’s “warm words about recognising wellbeing pressures… have not been matched by the action necessary”, that negotiation had “hit a brick wall”, and that “the current system is not fit for purpose”. The chief inspector Sir Martyn Oliver said on the same day that an independent survey of providers showed Ofsted was “on the right track”, and the inspectorate acknowledges that “workload and wellbeing remain a challenge”. The Department for Education says it is working with both to ensure accountability is “fair, proportionate and focused on helping every school get better”. The report cards at issue replaced the old one- or two-word judgements last November, a change made after the death of the head teacher Ruth Perry, whose inquest in January 2023 found an Ofsted inspection had contributed to it. The system intended as the remedy is now the subject of a second union’s no-confidence motion in a month, the National Association of Head Teachers having warned in September that it could pose a “risk to life”. For parents the immediate question is narrower: whether the inspections their school is preparing for will happen on schedule, and the answer now depends on a ballot that has not yet been called.

Lib Dem Treasury Spokesman Quits to Try to Unseat Davey

Charlie Maynard, the party’s Treasury spokesman and MP for Witney, has resigned from the frontbench and wants Sir Ed Davey replaced. It is the second frontbench departure in a week, after the care and carers spokesperson Alison Bennett four days earlier. The deputy leader says most MPs are “furious at this distraction”.

Dive deeper
Daisy Cooper’s defence is that the arithmetic is not there and the timing is self-harming: “The vast majority of our MPs support Ed, want him to lead the party into the next election, and are furious at this distraction,” adding that “we are the last line of defence against a Reform UK government”. The former leader Tim Farron was blunter: “To think we’re helped by this kind of rubbish, you’d need to be smoking the kind of stuff even we aren’t in favour of legalising.” On the other side, Julian Brazil, who leads Devon county council, says Mr Maynard has “put his head above the parapet — we’ll see if other people follow”, and that while Sir Ed has been “a fantastic leader” the job is “best done by someone else”. The mechanics make a challenge hard rather than impossible. A contest requires a no-confidence vote passed by a majority of all the party’s MPs; a candidate then needs nominations from a tenth of the parliamentary party plus two hundred members across at least twenty local parties. Sir Ed led the party to seventy-two seats in 2024, its best result in more than a century, and holds seventy-one now. He has been leader since 2020, acting from 2019. The context is a week in which a Green leader nearly took a Labour seat and the Liberal Democrats were largely absent from the argument.
