Trump Says Threats Pulled the Bombers Out of Fairford

Asked whether a specific threat prompted the withdrawal from RAF Fairford, Donald Trump said: “Yeah I think we can say that. They didn’t fly out by accident.” He added that “there were some threats”, that Washington knows who made them, and that they “would be linked to Iran”. That contradicts his own Secretary of State, who said hours earlier that he would not tie the move to the incident.

Dive deeper
Set the two accounts side by side. Marco Rubio on Monday: “I wouldn’t just directly tie that to the move that was made”, and “it’s not unusual to see movement of assets to different parts of the world based on exercises, redeployments”. JD Vance, later: an “abundance of caution”. The president, speaking by Marine One before flying to Nebraska: “There were some threats. And rather than tempting the enemy, we’ll capture the enemy and beat the enemy. But why should we leave them there? So we moved them around a little bit. Not very far.” Asked whether Iran had brought combat drones into Britain, he said he could not say, “but if they did they’ll suffer greatly”. About a dozen B-1s went home after the 27 September incident, in which police were called to three suspicious vehicles near the Gloucestershire base; five men were arrested and bailed, and a sixth, a British-Iranian, later. Christoph Bergs of RUSI judged the redeployment will cut America’s ability to surge sorties against Iran quickly, though London had already restricted the base to defensive missions, and suggested Diego Garcia or the Gulf as alternatives. One source close to the White House reached for Ukraine’s Operation Spiderweb, the covert drone attack on five Russian airbases last year, as the model of what can be done at distance. The nature of the threat is still not public. Watch whether the State Department reconciles the two accounts.

Yemen’s Claim to Have Taken Mocha Is Disputed by Its Own Officials

Yemen’s military says its forces captured Mocha on Monday afternoon with Saudi air support and have “secured” the Bab al-Mandab strait. Government and military sources told Reuters and AFP something narrower: that forces reached only the city’s outskirts, and that what they hold is the Dhubab military airfield and the road to it. The Houthis have not commented on Mocha at all.

Dive deeper
The gap between the two accounts is the story, because the strait is the reason any of this matters to a British motorist: with Hormuz effectively shut, Saudi crude has been going out through the Red Sea, and the Houthis took the coast overlooking Bab al-Mandab last month. Dhubab airfield is 45 kilometres south of Mocha, so holding one is not holding the other. Major General Turki al-Malki put a hundred fighter jets behind the push and said the coalition had destroyed 324 “high-value” targets since the offensive began, along with weapons depots and stores of booby-trapped boats and naval mines. Operation Yemen Dawn was announced on Sunday by Rashad al-Alimi, who said every opportunity for de-escalation was “exhausted”. The cost so far, on the government health ministry’s own count, is at least 959 killed and 5,000 injured since 6 August, with 197,000 displaced on United Nations figures. The Houthis say they struck Riyadh’s airport and an Aramco refinery; satellite imagery examined by the BBC shows heavy smoke at the Aramco Riyadh refinery on Saturday and a plume at the Khurais processing facility on Sunday. Their coordination centre has told airlines that Saudi airspace outside Mecca and Medina is no longer safe. António Guterres condemned the cross-border attacks and said he was deeply concerned by the escalation. Watch for independent confirmation of Mocha.

Quebec Separatists Win, and Promise a Referendum After Trump

Paul St-Pierre Plamondon’s Parti Québécois has won the most seats in Quebec, returning to power for the first time since 2014 after taking just three seats in 2022. Canadian media project a minority; 64 of the 127 seats are needed for control. He has promised a third independence referendum in his first term, but only once Donald Trump has left office.

Dive deeper
Support for independence runs at about 30%, low by the standards of the referendums of 1980 and 1995, the second of which was lost narrowly; there has not been one in three decades. So the immediate constitutional fight is a smaller one. Mr Plamondon has promised to “abolish the monarchy in Quebec” by defunding the lieutenant-governor’s office — which represents the King and grants royal assent to provincial legislation, so removing it would invite a serious challenge and cast doubt on the validity of future Quebec laws. He was among the members who refused to swear allegiance to the Crown in 2022, after which the oath was made optional, and says he is ready for a legal battle with Ottawa. Daniel Beland of McGill cautioned that the result reflects a fragmented multiparty system rather than a surge in secessionism, but added the point that will be read in Washington: a pro-independence party winning in Quebec a fortnight before an independence-related referendum in Alberta “might suggest to the Trump administration that national unity remains a major issue in Canada”. Mark Carney, still popular in the province but weakened there by August’s American tariffs, congratulated him and offered to work together for workers hit by them; Oxford Economics puts the tariff cost to Quebec at two billion Canadian dollars. Watch Alberta.

A Russian Drone Injures a Moldovan for the First Time

A drone struck the Palanca border crossing on Monday morning, damaging a passenger bus and injuring a Moldovan border police employee — the first confirmed Moldovan casualty of Russia’s drone campaign. Geolocated imagery indicates the drone ricocheted into Moldovan territory. Russian forces have been increasingly striking Ukrainian crossings with Moldova, Romania and Poland.

Dive deeper
It is a small injury and a significant one, because it is the point at which a war fought over a border starts producing casualties on the wrong side of it, in a country that is not in NATO and cannot ask for its protection. It arrives in the same week that two drones sank a Turkish-owned ship carrying corn inside Romania’s exclusive economic zone; Volodymyr Zelensky says the captain was killed and a crew member injured, while Romanian authorities identified two dead and rescued eleven, and the vessel’s flag state is not yet clear. Assessments note Russia has been targeting civilian vessels carrying crop products specifically, which is an economic weapon rather than a military one. Ankara has said nothing about the sinking that any approved source has carried. Overnight on 4 October Russia launched 205 Shahed-type drones, 49 of them jet-powered, alongside loitering munitions and decoys, from seven locations including occupied Crimea; 172 were brought down, 13 places were struck. In Kharkiv a guided bomb killed five and injured 59 by the regional administration’s later count, higher than the figures given earlier in the day. Ukraine also says it has nearly exhausted its reserve of railway locomotives and is negotiating to buy more from countries on the same gauge. Watch whether Chisinau asks the European Union for consultations.

France Faces a National Day of School Protests Today

Organisers have called nationwide action for Tuesday after rejecting the government’s concessions as “nothing concrete”. More than 190 students have been injured and over 5,000 people arrested in a week; more than 500 schools were closed by Monday’s protests, and the education minister says 24 have been burned and ransacked since the movement began. A teachers’ union is urging members to join.

Dive deeper
Sébastien Lecornu announced measures on Sunday promising “visible changes” in schools while drawing a hard line on disorder: “We will make no concessions to violence. We stand by students and staff who have been assaulted, as well as police officers and gendarmes who have been injured.” The main student organisation answered that the government had offered nothing concrete and called for mobilisation against “this political deadlock and what we describe as unprecedented repression of the movement”. The injuries are what has radicalised it. A fifteen-year-old’s hand was blown off near a school in Lens on Monday, and last week a fourteen-year-old was badly hurt at Saint-Ouen-l’Aumône, reportedly struck on the head by a tear gas grenade. The grievances are unglamorous and old — overcrowded classrooms, decaying buildings, too few teachers — which is part of why the movement has spread from Parisian secondary schools in September to universities and now, potentially, to staff. Jean-Luc Mélenchon calls it the most powerful student movement since 1968, which is the sort of claim that is either rhetoric or prophecy depending on who turns out. Watch whether the teaching unions actually strike, because that is the difference between a protest and a shutdown.

Tories Pledge a £10bn “Britannia Shield” Air Defence System

The Conservatives will promise a layered national air defence of radar, command and control, and interceptor missiles, at £10 billion over ten years. James Cartlidge sets it out today as “the most significant upgrade to UK air defence for generations”. Kemi Badenoch said that “if a missile or drone is launched at Britain, we do not get a second chance to wish we had prepared”.

Dive deeper
A billion a year is the number to hold on to, because the arguments about it are all arguments about scale. The government has already committed around £790 million to integrated air and missile defence in its investment plan, plus more than £350 million to double the Army’s Sky Sabre batteries, so the Conservative offer is larger but not transformative — and Israel’s Iron Dome, the obvious comparison, defends a country roughly a twelfth the size of Great Britain against short-range rockets. Britain owns no Patriot batteries and in an acute crisis would lean on Type 45 destroyers. The threat case is real enough: a Russian drone found at Leipzig airport in August, Iranian-proxy drones striking the RAF base in Cyprus, Iranian missiles fired at the Chagos Islands this year with American interceptors doing the work, and a defence committee that reported last November that the UK has “next to nothing”. Wes Streeting called the record “abysmal” and said the party had cut the Army to its smallest since Napoleon and left a £17 billion hole in the equipment plan. The Liberal Democrats’ James MacCleary called it “Truss-style fantasy economics” and asked which parts of the country the “Patchwork Dome” would not cover. It would be funded inside the existing 3% of GDP pledge, from welfare reform; spending is about 2.6% now. Watch Mr Cartlidge’s text for a costed coverage map.

Polanski Rejects “Exclusion” as Labour MPs Refer the Greens to the EHRC

Zack Polanski has said he will not accept “any exclusion, or refusal to engage with anyone, because of the religious or communal body they are part of”, in his first comments since Sunday’s vote. More than a dozen Labour MPs have referred the Greens to the equality watchdog and nine have referred the party to Sussex Police. A longstanding member has resigned.

Dive deeper
The arithmetic is becoming the story. The motion passed 1,022 to 813 — around 0.4% of a membership above 200,000 — with four of the five Green MPs opposed and the leader not voting at all. Ellie Chowns, who used to co-lead the party, said she was “deeply saddened” but disputed that the motion is antisemitic, while arguing that governance “urgently needs to be modernised” when fewer than one in a hundred members actually took part. Sandy Ruthven, who chairs Amnesty International UK’s charitable trust, has resigned, accusing the party of failing to confront antisemitism. The Equality and Human Rights Commission says it will “consider each complaint carefully” and has not opened an investigation. The European Green Party, which covers 47 parties, wants to “unequivocally distance” itself and will “immediately demand clarity”; the German Greens’ co-leader Felix Banaszak called the motion “intolerable and unacceptable”. Mr Polanski, who is Jewish, added that “antisemitism and criticism of the Israeli state are, and must always be, completely distinct”, and he remains barred from Israel. The timing is unforgiving: he is the Green candidate in Holborn and St Pancras, where polling is on Thursday, and he called the contest fifty-fifty last week. Watch Thursday, and whether the commission opens a formal investigation.

The FCA Reviews Its Own Conduct After a Whistleblower’s Death

Simon Andriesz, a 57-year-old British banker who said the Financial Conduct Authority put him through “absolute hell”, took his own life in Thailand on 23 September. The regulator had wrongly told him he did not qualify for whistleblower protection because his identity was known, and apologised. It has asked a newly appointed non-executive director to review its handling of his case.

Dive deeper
Mr Andriesz, a former managing director at BGC Partners, had told the FBI in 2020 and 2021 that the American commerce secretary Howard Lutnick had undeclared business ties to Jeffrey Epstein, and said he had found an email chain in the Epstein files suggesting the two discussed a shared startup in 2018. Mr Lutnick says he had no personal or professional relationship with Epstein and is not accused of any wrongdoing. The British thread runs through Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor: Mr Andriesz said he uncovered evidence that Mr Lutnick had sought in 2013 to go into business with him and use his contacts to attract investment, in exchange for a £1 million loan. BGC says the venture did not proceed. His wider charge was institutional rather than personal — that the regulator “protects the institution and then exposes the whistleblower to extreme retaliation”. The review will be led by Lea Paterson, a former Bank of England director who joined the FCA board only recently, which is precisely the objection. John McDonnell, who chairs the all-party group on investment fraud, called the death a “profound and preventable tragedy” and said the regulator is being allowed to “mark its own homework”, demanding a genuinely independent evaluation or his group will run its own. Watch whether the FCA concedes one.

Jeffrey Archer Dies Aged 86

The novelist, former Conservative MP and former deputy chairman of the party has died at 86. He resigned his Commons seat in 1974 facing bankruptcy, rebuilt a fortune through bestselling fiction, returned to the top of his party, and was jailed in 2001 for perjury and perverting the course of justice. His last novel is due out this month.

Dive deeper
His career is the clearest case in modern British politics of a man who twice built and twice destroyed a public reputation. He left Parliament close to ruin, wrote his way out of it, became deputy chairman under Margaret Thatcher, and was the Conservative candidate for Mayor of London before a former friend disclosed that he had asked him to provide a false alibi in the 1987 libel action he had won against a newspaper. He served two years. Kemi Badenoch said on Tuesday morning that he had texted her the previous morning, hours before he died, with the line “Great outfit, great speech, no need to reply”, and called him “a great communicator” and “a great storyteller”. That is a fair summary of what his party valued in him and of why the rest of it never quite settled: he was an exceptional salesman for a cause, and the thing he most reliably oversold was himself. Watch the tributes for how the conference handles a figure most of its leadership privately found useful and publicly found difficult.

Experts Warn of a Rise in Babies Entering Care Proceedings

The number of babies brought into care proceedings is rising, and specialists have warned that some may be adopted unnecessarily, according to evidence given to the BBC. The concern is that decisions taken at speed, and under pressure, are harder to reverse for the youngest children than for any other group in the system.

Dive deeper
This matters because of where it sits in the sequence. Care proceedings for an infant start a statutory clock, and the younger the child the more strongly the system favours permanence — which in practice means adoption, the one outcome that cannot be undone if the original judgement was wrong. Set that against a decade in which local authority children’s services have been among the most financially squeezed parts of local government, and the risk is not malice but throughput: thresholds moving quietly because the alternative is a service that cannot cope. The counter-argument is just as serious, and it is why this is contested rather than scandalous — the cases where the state waited too long are the ones that end in inquiries. What would settle it is data on how many of these proceedings end in adoption against reunification, broken down by the age of the child, and whether that ratio has shifted. This is a single broadcaster’s reporting of expert concern rather than a published dataset, and it should be read that way until the figures follow. Watch for the family justice statistics, and for whether the president of the Family Division comments.
